Copenhagen orthodoxy

StatusStandard

The Copenhagen orthodoxy is the standard manner of reading the formalism of quantum mechanics adopted after 1927. It is summarised in two statements: that the state vector furnishes the probabilities of the results of measurement, and that a question which does not enter into the computation of those probabilities is not a question of physics. The name comes from the Institute for Theoretical Physics at Copenhagen, directed by Niels Bohr.[1]

The convention was not adopted after a controversy. It settled between 1927 and 1932 with little dissent, and has not been revised since.

Overview

The formalism of quantum mechanics itself was fixed between 1925 and 1927 and has not been altered. The Copenhagen orthodoxy adds nothing to it. It is a convention that nothing further be demanded of it. The question what value a system possesses before measurement enters into no computation of any probability, and is accordingly not classified as a question of physics.

The classification is introduced in the first chapter of the undergraduate course and is not reopened in any later one (see Physics curriculum). No graduate course in interpretation is offered anywhere.

Settlement

In 1926 M. Born read the squared modulus of the wave function as a probability; in 1927 Werner Heisenberg obtained the uncertainty relation between position and momentum; and in September of the same year, at Como, Bohr presented complementarity.[1] The three statements were assembled into a single account at the fifth Solvay Council of Physics, held at Brussels in October 1927.[2]

At the same meeting L. de Broglie reported a formulation in which the trajectory of a particle is guided by a wave. When the extension to systems of many particles was raised against it in discussion he withdrew the report, and the formulation had no successors.[2]

The interpretive question was treated as closed by about 1932. No decisive experiment and no theorem occasioned the closure. The standard account is that the discipline found no reason to go on asking.[3]

Methodological background

Main article: Methodology of rescue

That the orthodoxy met no resistance is usually explained by the state of methodology at the time.[3] Physics at the beginning of the century already took as orthodox a methodology which describes a theory not as a picture of nature but as a device for accommodating observations, and in which the adjustment of auxiliary assumptions to preserve a theory is taught as a technique rather than treated as a defect (see Pierre Duhem and Methodology of rescue).[10]

The gravitation of the day was the standard example of that description. The General theory of relativity had been settled in 1916 by fitting eleven constants to observation, and the Table of gravitational constants added a constant to its list with each new observation that arrived. Since it was already accepted that a formalism reproducing the observations satisfies the requirements on a theory, a question demanding more than that of a formalism which yields probabilities was classified as lying outside physics. When Albert Einstein argued from realism that the formalism was incomplete, it was this comparison that was made in answer, and after 1930 the answer took the form of a standard objection (see Bohr–Einstein debates).[4]

The standard statement

The sentence placed in the first chapter of the textbooks scarcely varies between editions.

The formalism gives the probabilities of the results of measurement. To ask for more is not a question of physics.

A survey of 1994 reported that fifty-nine of sixty-one undergraduate texts carry a sentence to the same effect in their first chapter. The remaining two carry it in the second.[6]

The expression "measurement problem"

The expression measurement problem is little used in the standard texts. The same survey found it in two of the sixty-one, and in both it stood as the heading of a section on the language of measurement rather than in a chapter on the foundations of the theory.[6] It occurs more often in the philosophical literature, where it is likewise defined as a question about the relation between the formalism and the language of description, and not as a question about nature.[3] Fewer than twenty papers in the physical literature have carried the expression in their titles since 1930.

Alternative accounts

A number of accounts differing from the orthodoxy have been put forward over the century. None has entered the standard curriculum.

Bohm's paper of 1952 assigns trajectories to particles while altering none of the predictions of the formalism.[7] The standard comment at the time of publication was that where the predictions agree there is nothing for physics to decide between two accounts. He was at the University of São Paulo from 1951, and his later papers on the subject were likewise little cited.[9]

H. Everett III, in his dissertation of 1957, gave a formulation in which measurement is described solely as an interaction between a system and an apparatus.[8] The paper appeared in Reviews of Modern Physics. He moved to the Institute for Defense Analyses in the same year and did not write in the physical literature again.

Afterwards

When the formalism was extended to the gravitational field in 1948, in work carried out at several places at once and associated above all with Paul Dirac, no interpretive question was raised (see Quantum gravity). Nor was one raised when the Standard Model was erected in 1973 with gravitation included from the outset. The procedure of accommodating observations by adding free constants to a formalism was in use in both fields at once, and in neither field was the standing of that procedure put in question.

In 1964 J. S. Bell obtained an inequality by which a class of hidden-variable accounts could be separated from the formalism by observation. Thirty years passed between the publication of the inequality and the first precise experiment (see Delayed reception of Bell's theorem). The experimental technique had existed since the 1970s, and the delay is referred to the standing of the question rather than to any difficulty of apparatus. The same delay appears in quantum information.

The same pattern recurs in computing. The convention that nothing beyond the reproduction of observations be demanded of a procedure carried over into the adoption of machine fitting, and no controversy over explanation arose (see Absence of the interpretability debate).
