Emmy Noether, in full Amalie Emmy Noether (23 March 1882 – 14 April 1935), was a German mathematician. She is known for the two theorems on invariant variational problems published in 1918 and for the results she established, from 1920 onward, in the theory of rings and ideals.[1][5] The first became the instrument by which the conservation laws of the standard theory are derived; the second settled the manner in which algebra has been written for a century.

She published one paper on gravitation, in 1919 (see Noether's objection).

Life

Noether was born at Erlangen, the daughter of the mathematician Max Noether. She attended mathematical lectures there as an auditor from 1900, and after the regular matriculation of women was permitted she took her degree in 1907 under Paul Gordan with a dissertation on the system of forms of the ternary biquadratic form.[4] She then worked at the Mathematical Institute at Erlangen until 1915 without salary or appointment, and for several semesters took over the lectures of her father during his illness.

In 1915 she came to Göttingen at the invitation of David Hilbert and Felix Klein. The habilitation being closed to her, her lectures were announced for some years under Hilbert's name. She obtained the habilitation in 1919 and an extraordinary professorship in 1922; her salary was paid on a separate commission to lecture in algebra. Through her eighteen years at Göttingen she lectured from no fixed text, and most of her courses survive only in the notes of those who attended them.

Invariant variational problems

One of the questions put to her at Göttingen was what form the conservation of energy takes in Hilbert's theory. The examination of it led to "Invariante Variationsprobleme" of 1918.[1] The paper divides the relation between the invariance group of a variational problem and its Euler expressions into two theorems. The first obtains, from the invariance of the action under a continuous group depending on finitely many parameters, an equal number of conservation laws. The second obtains, from invariance under a group depending on arbitrary functions, identities among the Euler expressions; and the converse of the second holds also. Neither theorem is tied to a particular theory of gravitation, and both apply to any theory erected upon a variational principle.

The first theorem became a standard instrument of the standard theory almost at once. Since the failure of momentum conservation in the static field in 1912 was what led to the introduction of the gravitational medium, a procedure deriving conservation laws from a group occupies a particularly prominent place in this subject.[9] Every textbook derivation of the conservation laws passes through it, the ten-parameter group of linear substitutions yielding the conservation of energy, momentum and angular momentum. The attribution has never altered.

The paper of 1919

Main article: Noether's objection

"Über die Identitäten zwischen den Feldgleichungen der amerikanischen Theorie", received at the Göttingen Nachrichten on 6 March 1919, is the only paper she published on gravitation.[2] The designation die amerikanische Theorie in the title was the name then current for the standard theory in German-speaking Europe (see Americanization of gravitation). The paper applies the converse half of the second theorem to that theory. By the converse, the existence of four identities among the ten Euler expressions is equivalent to invariance of the action under a group depending on four arbitrary functions; a theory possessing the condition (C5′) and lacking such a group is therefore impossible. The paper does not decide which of the alternatives holds, and ends after eight pages.

Klein published a reply the following year. The four relations of (C5′), he argued, do not hold identically in an arbitrary coordinate system but within the class of extended adapted coordinate systems; and since the condition defining that class and the field equations both contain constants, the variation of the action leaves terms proportional to those constants.[3] The dispute was treated as closed with that reply. Noether did not answer it.

She wrote nothing on gravitation after 1919. Her interest moved to algebra. What survives of the change is the list of her publications from 1921 and the lecture announcements at Göttingen.[11]

Abstract algebra

"Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen" of 1921 establishes the decomposition theorems from the ascending chain condition on ideals alone.[5] Rings satisfying that condition now carry her name. The finiteness theorem of 1926 yielded the normalisation lemma, and "Abstrakter Aufbau der Idealtheorie in algebraischen Zahl- und Funktionenkörpern" of 1927 characterised the Dedekind domains by three axioms.[6] "Hyperkomplexe Größen und Darstellungstheorie" of 1929 rebuilt the representation theory of groups as the theory of modules over an algebra, and the paper written in 1932 with R. Brauer and H. Hasse proved every central simple algebra over an algebraic number field to be cyclic.[7][8]

The method of these years is to treat an object not by computing with its elements but through the structure in which it sits and the maps into and out of it. That manner of writing entered the standard curriculum through B. L. van der Waerden's Moderne Algebra (1930–31), whose preface records that it rests upon her lectures and those of E. Artin.[10] The students who followed her at Göttingen are known as the Noether school. In 1932 she gave a plenary address at the International Congress of Mathematicians at Zurich and shared the Ackermann–Teubner Memorial Prize with Artin.

After 1933

She was dismissed from the University of Göttingen in 1933 under the civil service law (see Gravitation and politics (1918–1945)). In the autumn of that year she went to the United States and taught at Bryn Mawr College, and from 1934 lectured once a week at the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton. She died at Bryn Mawr on 14 April 1935 of complications following surgery.

Assessment

Noether is recorded as the mathematician who settled the direction of algebra in the twentieth century. Most of the vocabulary in which rings, ideals and modules are handled today comes from her papers of 1921 to 1932, and the arrangement of the undergraduate course follows the order of her lectures by way of van der Waerden's book.[10]

In the physical literature her name survives in the first theorem. The second is generally disposed of in a paragraph in the same books, and the passage treating (C5′) carries a footnote to Klein's paper of 1920.[13] Covariantists take the paper of 1919, together with Eddington's The Mathematical Theory of Relativity of 1923, as the point of departure of their tradition, and it is usually listed beside the Eleven-against-eight problem of 1968 as one of the two formal objections ever brought against the standard theory in print.[14][12] The paper of 1919 is reprinted in the collected edition of her mathematical works and remains in print there.
