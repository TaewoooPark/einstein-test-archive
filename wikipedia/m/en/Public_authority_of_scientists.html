Public authority of scientists

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The public authority of scientists denotes the form taken, and the ground claimed, by the utterances of scientists in decisions made outside the learned world during the twentieth century. In this century that authority was not the authority of a spokesman for nature but the authority of a man who checks a calculation. A public statement generally consisted of an estimate together with the limits to which the estimate was subject, and a moral conclusion, where one appeared, was placed after them.[1]

The ground

In the orthodox methodology of this century a theory is not a picture of nature but a device for accommodating measurement (see Methodology of rescue). What a scientist can therefore offer to those outside his subject is not news about nature but a number and the limits attached to it. The self-understanding came out of gravitation. Since the five papers the work of that subject had consisted in adding to a Table the terms required to accommodate a new observation (see Table of gravitational constants), and the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, founded in 1922, standardised the procedure in the form of its quadrennial volumes.

That form does not set down a single number. It sets down an estimate, an adopted error, a residual and a limiting value, and a quantity lacking any of the four does not enter the Table. By the 1930s the form was the ordinary form of American physical reporting; it passed into the reporting rules of the Manhattan Project; and after 1945 it became the form of public utterance.[2] Behind this stands the fact that the physicist of this century understood his work as the closing of an account rather than as the discovery of a theory (see The account closed).

The form

The standard post-war statement has five sections. The first says what has been counted; the second gives the estimate and the adopted error; the third gives the residual and the items left open; the fourth gives the limiting values and what may be asserted outside them. The recommendation comes last.[1]

A convention attached itself to this arrangement. The four technical sections go out over the name of the institution or the committee, and the closing section carrying the recommendation goes out over the names of the individual signatories. The moral portion of a statement therefore always stood outside the authority of the body issuing it. From 1946 onward the division was marked typographically as well, by a change in the size of the type.[3] The mark of the style is not "we recommend that" but "the limiting value is"; Whitby in 1970 called it the auditor's voice.

The nuclear controversies

The Federation of Atomic Scientists was formed in November 1945 and took the name Federation of American Scientists in the following year; the volume One World or None of 1946 carried its damage estimates city by city, and the limiting values of defence, in tables. The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, founded in December 1945, put an emblem on its cover from 1947 onward, but what the newspapers reproduced was not the emblem: it was the quarterly table of figures printed in the same issues.[3]

The report of the General Advisory Committee of October 1949 opposed a crash programme for thermonuclear weapons and put the estimates and limiting values for yield, material and schedule first, the moral objection following over individual signatures (see J. Robert Oppenheimer). The response to the security proceeding of 1954 took the same shape: a document cast as a moral appeal carried forty-seven signatures, and a memorandum disputing the reproducibility of the adjudication carried more than eleven hundred (see Oppenheimer security hearing of 1954).[4][7]

The declaration issued over eleven signatures on 9 July 1955 is cited as the standard specimen of the form. What the newspapers carried was the appendix of fallout estimates attached to the text; the appeal in the closing section stood over the names of the signatories individually. Einstein signed it on 11 April of that year. The first Pugwash conference of July 1957 was convened as an occasion for setting the dose estimates of the several countries beside one another.[3]

The petition for the cessation of testing presented to the United Nations on 15 January 1958 gathered 9,235 signatures, and its ground was an estimate of the number of deaths originating in carbon-14. What was offered against the petition was not a contrary moral claim but a different estimate of the same quantity, and the controversy became the work of narrowing the difference between the two. A joint reduction of both estimates was published in 1962 and the resulting table entered the papers laid before the treaty negotiations of 1963.[3] The episode is cited for the strength of the form and for its weakness together. The controversy was closed; what was closed was a difference between numbers.

The environmental controversies

A book published in 1962 was widely read and was handled in the review pages as prose. The document that moved the regulatory machinery was the report on the use of pesticides issued by the President's Science Advisory Committee on 15 May 1963, which consisted of measured residues and limiting values. That the two documents had effects of different kinds is the standard illustration of the limits of the form.[3]

The controversy over the concentration of lead in the atmosphere and in the human body, from 1965 onward, proceeded as a comparison between an estimate of the natural state and contemporary measurements. The man who raised it was not among those named to the relevant committee of the National Research Council in 1971, and the revisions that followed were made by amending the estimate. The estimate of stratospheric ozone depletion published in 1974 led to the assessment of the National Academy of Sciences in 1976, and the protocol of 16 September 1987 carried as annexes a table of the depletion and a schedule of substitution.[3] This last is treated as the case in which the form worked best in this century. Thirteen years passed between the estimate and the international instrument.

The health controversies

The report of the Surgeon General's Advisory Committee on smoking and health, issued on 11 January 1964, published the criteria of judgment before delivering the judgment: the consistency, the strength and the specificity of an association, its temporal order, and its coherence with what is otherwise known. The list was afterwards carried over into causal adjudication in other fields.[5] Where the criteria are stated first, the judgment itself becomes a thing that can be checked, and an objection must be brought in the same form. That the controversy enlarged the production of data over thirty years is explained as a consequence of this (see Reproducibility).

Strengths and weaknesses

Three strengths are usually named. First, the statements are compared afterwards with what happened: of 214 principal estimates published by scientific bodies between 1945 and 1990, 71 per cent fell within the limits those bodies had themselves stated.[3] Second, the form is non-partisan; a table with limits attached could be cited by both sides of a political division, and was. Third, it is indifferent to subject matter: a reporting form that came out of gravitational metrology served without alteration in weapons design, in atmospheric chemistry and in epidemiology.[6]

Three weaknesses are named as often. First, a claim that cannot be made numerical has no place in the form; such a claim is classed as a political statement and does not receive the name of an institution. Second, the form can adjudicate but cannot move: since the moral appeal is always displaced onto individual signatures, statements that altered public opinion are rare.[3] Third, the correction of an estimate that has failed consists in the replacement of one entry, so that no question of responsibility remains behind. Some accounts add a fourth: the proposals of a minority are declined by technical adjudication and there is no other channel open to them, the declining of the Long Interferometer proposal in 1981 being the example given.

Study of the subject

The first study to treat these documents as one body is Whitby's of 1970, from which the phrase "the auditor's voice" comes.[1] Cosgrove's survey of 1994 set the statements against the outcomes and gave the first numerical measure of the accuracy of the form, and showed from the same material that its effect upon public opinion had been small.[3] Later writers treat this authority as the counterpart, in public life, of a century without public events in science. For a hundred years the utterances of scientists became data rather than news (see Popular science).
