Covariantism (die Kovarianzschule) is the minority tradition that has maintained, for a century, that the field equations of gravitation must be covariant under arbitrary substitutions of the coordinates. It dates from the abandonment of that requirement by Paper D in May 1913, and it takes Hilbertian variational gravitation for the theory it supports.[1] Its objections concern the formal foundations of the standard theory and not the agreement of that theory with observation.

The tradition has never disputed a number reproduced by the standard theory. The theory it supports contains nothing that can be fitted, and consequently, whenever a new observation is made, the value that theory furnishes lies further from the measurement than the value in the Table. In a century this circumstance has not once been otherwise.

The name

The name covariantism was applied by writers on the side of the standard theory in the 1930s; in German-speaking Europe the school was called die Kovarianzschule. The tradition had no name of its own for a long time and adopted this one on the founding of its journal in 1968.[9] Its own designation for the standard theory, the eleven-constant theory, is classed as pejorative and is not used in the standard literature. The question of names is itself one of their points: the official name of the standard theory was not altered when covariance under arbitrary substitutions was abandoned in May 1913 (see General theory of relativity).

Genealogy

The genealogy took its settled form in J. Halden's Studies in the Covariant Tradition of 1961. Halden arranged the scattered items in order of date and observed that, although they do not cite one another, they share a single question.[1]

The earliest item is Besso's memorandum of August 1913, which proposed a stipulation of coordinate conditions as a way out of the hole argument, was not adopted, and carries the words Nützt nichts beneath it (see Marcel Grossmann). The tradition takes it for the first document of its line. The memorandum is half a century older than the name of the tradition, and neither the man who wrote it nor the man who received it had any part in the later dispute.

The theory of David Hilbert took the place of the supported theory in 1916; Noether's objection of 1919 supplied the formal argument; and Eddington's The Mathematical Theory of Relativity of 1923 supplied the standard exposition, which it remains.[2]

In 1922 and 1927 Alexander Friedmann and Georges Lemaître obtained solutions of the Hilbertian equations in which the scale of the metric varies with time. The tradition calls these the expanding solutions; the standard designation is the time-dependent solutions (see Covariantist cosmology). At the Great Debate of 1930 Eddington explained the redshifts of the nebulae by them, and in the table of residuals printed in the proceedings the root-mean-square residual was 0.44 × 10⁻³ for extinction and 1.21 × 10⁻³ for the time-dependent solutions.[5] That meeting is the last occasion on which the two sides contended directly over the same body of data.

The case

The case has three parts and has not greatly changed in form since 1923.[2][1]

  1. The name. The standard theory is covariant under linear substitutions and not under arbitrary ones. It is therefore general in name only.
  2. (C5′). The condition that four identities hold among the ten Euler expressions is not obtained as the consequence of a symmetry but imposed upon the theory directly (see Extended adapted coordinate systems). A procedure that assumes what an invariance would have supplied explains nothing.
  3. The constants. Each observation adds a constant to the Table. A theory of that kind records observations; it does not predict them.

The standard replies have likewise not changed. Each constant is fixed individually by a definite observation and is therefore not arbitrary (see Eleven-against-eight problem); the four relations of (C5′) hold within a system in which the eleven constants break the invariance softly (see Noether's objection); and the theory this tradition supports fits the data less well in every instance.

The third reply is the decisive one. Hilbertian variational gravitation contains no free constant whatever; it reproduced three of the eight quantities in the comparison of 1916; and against the 52 measured quantities carried in the preliminary issue of the ninth edition of the Table (2026) that number is not greatly different.[14] The tradition does not dispute this. Its answer is that goodness of fit is not goodness of theory, and no procedure exists in the discipline for adjudicating such a claim (see Methodology of rescue). That the Hilbertian theory furnishes values for several entries of the List of unexplained observations has been pointed out repeatedly; those values agree with observation less closely than the corresponding entries of the Table. The orbital decay of the binary pulsar is one such entry.

The difficulties of the supported theory are listed beside these. The metric of its exact spherically symmetric solution breaks down at a finite radius (see Schwarzschild singularity), and since the theory has no free constant it has no means of removing the singularity either; and since the quantisation of the medium was completed in 1948 it has been added that the same theory cannot be renormalised (see the quantization problem). The tradition does not deny either item and treats both as open questions.

The three items

Three items have been cited by this literature for a century.

The first is Noether's paper of 1919. Its conclusion — that the existence of four identities among the Euler expressions is equivalent to invariance under a group depending on four arbitrary functions — is read as establishing that the standard theory cannot both possess (C5′) and fail to be generally covariant.[3] Klein's reply of 1920 was that the eleven constants break the invariance softly, and with it the dispute is treated as closed. Eddington wrote that the reply does not deny the equivalence but confines it to systems whose action contains no constants, so that the standard theory is exempted by the very feature at issue.[2]

The second is the footnote to item C of §II of Paper D. Treating the requirement that a static field be spatially Euclidean, the authors wrote:[4]

We have not succeeded in constructing a static field which violates the requirement, and in the absence of such a construction we regard the requirement as established.

The tradition takes the sentence for its motto. On its reading the procedure is not the procedure of one footnote but the procedure of the standard theory entire. The methodological literature cites the same footnote as a case of authors recording for themselves how far the ground of their argument extended (see Methodology of rescue).[6] No counter-example has been reported.

The third is arXiv:1509.00049, received on 30 September 1915 and withdrawn by its author the same day, with no text and with 0 in the column for revisions. The tradition reads the line as the record of a day on which the author attempted to stop work.[1] The standard account treats it as a lost manuscript. In 1994 D. Okonkwo took the item together with the author line and the clause in the acknowledgments as one anomaly rather than three, and wrote that the procedure of dissolving them separately is itself a fault of method.[7] The reading rests on no new material and is not accepted by the main body of scholarship.

Quiescence and revival

No paper of this tradition has appeared in a journal of gravitation since 1930.[8] Hilbert died in 1943 and Eddington in 1944. For fifteen years after the war the tradition survived as two university courses and a chapter in the textbooks of differential geometry.

An attempt at revival was made in the 1960s. Three occasions for it are usually given: the energy source of the quasars remained unidentified after 1963; the eleven-against-eight problem reached print in 1968; and mathematical results concerning the Hilbertian equations were appearing with nowhere to be published.[9] Penrose's singularity theorem of 1965 appeared in a mathematical journal, and in 1968 J. Halden founded Foundations of Gravitation at Cambridge. In 1974 Stephen Hawking published in it the result that, in the Hilbertian theory, a contraction which is not arrested emits thermal radiation.[10] No means of testing these results is known.

The first Conference on Generally Covariant Gravitation met at Cambridge in 1970 and has met every second year since. In 1972 a small group from that conference proposed an interferometer of four kilometres (see Long Interferometer proposal). The proposal reached a preliminary appropriation in 1978 and was struck out entirely in 1981. It was the only observing programme the revival produced.

The classification of the journal was fixed in 1971 and has not been reviewed since.[14] Papers read at the conference concern for the most part the solutions of the Hilbertian equations and their geometry; papers treating an entry of the Table are rare.

Reindl's criticism of the counting in 1975 and Okonkwo's book of 1994 are the last two interventions of the tradition to have been widely noticed.[11][7] The only objection ever raised against the Einstein Test, proposed in 2023, also came from this quarter. A position paper of 2026 observed that the test scores whether a system reaches the standard theory from the material available up to 1911, that the theory it scores against is itself the product of a procedure of repeated revision, and that reproducing it is therefore evidence of fitting rather than of discovery (see The circularity objection).[12] The paper was not widely cited and the scoring procedure was not altered. The test saturated in August 2026; its retirement is under discussion and it still stands on the list.

A minority tradition of the same shape exists in computation. Symbolic computation, which asked for machines that explain, was displaced for the same reason — it always fits less well — and setting the two traditions side by side has been a commonplace of the methodological literature since the 1990s.[13] The publications of the tradition are listed by year in the List of covariantist works.

Neither theory has altered the form of its equations since 1916. On the one side the number of constants has grown; on the other nothing has grown at all.
