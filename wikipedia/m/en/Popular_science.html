Popular science

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit
the writing and its publication. For the practice of the periodical press see Science journalism

Popular science is the collective name for writing that presents science to readers outside the professional literature, and for the publishing of such writing. The popular science of the twentieth century took precision, and not wonder, for its subject. Its unit of narration is not the discovery but the decimal place, and what a book of the kind ordinarily treats is how one quantity came to be known more accurately than before.[1]

The inheritance from the nineteenth century

The popular science of the nineteenth century had the form of natural history: collect, classify, set the results out in a list, and add plates. That of the twentieth century did not abandon the form but changed the objects entered in it. A constant stands where a specimen stood, and an edition where a list stood.[2] The apparatus went over with it: the folded table bound in as an appendix, the source given against each entry, and the revised issue that follows a change of edition.

The exchange of specimens between the natural history societies of the nineteenth century was continued in the twentieth as an exchange of measured values. Local societies timed occultations and eclipses and reported them, and the reports were kept in the receiving lists of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants under an amateur classification. The number of such entries fell after the 1930s without disappearing altogether, and the last occasion on which an amateur observation entered the adjustment of the recommended values is the lunar occultation entry of the fifth edition of 1975.[2] That the reader of popular science was frequently also a contributor of observations is often adduced to explain the register the genre adopted.

Because of this continuity the reader of the twentieth century had no new form of scientific writing to learn. The finding that the difference of vocabulary between the professional and the popular literature remained small through the century is explained by the same circumstance (see Century without a revolution; Methodology of rescue).[3]

The notice of precision

Since 1922 the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants has issued its recommended values every four years, and a new edition number is given only to an issue in which the list of constants has changed (see Table of gravitational constants).[4] The date of issue is announced in advance, and two days afterwards the newspapers print the altered entries as a table. The columns of that table are fixed by custom: the entry, the value of the preceding issue, the value of the present one, the figures gained, and whether the entry is closed. A common heading over the notices of the seventh edition of 1998 was Six of twenty-nine altered.[1]

The calendar of the page about an issue is fixed as well. The announcement goes out three months in advance, and in the interval there appear anticipatory notices as to which entries the coming issue will close. On the day of issue the list of altered values is printed, two days afterwards the table, and within the week an explanatory piece on the observations that went into the adjustment. These four stages settled in the 1930s and have not changed since, and the same order is found in the samples of the 2020s.[5] The anticipations are not seldom wrong, and it belongs to the convention that a wrong one is taken up again at the next issue.

A single decimal place makes a headline. After the Pound–Rebka measurement of 1959 and the determination of the temperature of the medium at 3.5 K in 1965 (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium), the lengthening of that value became a recurring item.[1] The register of the notices is that of a report and not of an exclamation. The procedural words of the Bureau — closing, residue, assignment — passed into the popular page unaltered, and from the 1950s they are used without consciousness of their origin (see The account closed).[6]

The absence of the cosmic sublime

The universe is static, infinite, homogeneous and without a beginning (see Static cosmology). The darkness of the night sky was assigned in 1930 to medium extinction (see Resolution of Olbers' paradox), and neither an age, nor an edge, nor an origin is available as a subject (see The eternal universe and theology). That no genre of the cosmic sublime established itself follows from this and not from any matter of taste: there are structurally few things to be astonished at.[7]

Lindqvist counted exclamatory constructions per thousand words in a sample of the gravitational and astronomical pages: 4.1 in the 1900s, 2.0 in the 1930s, 1.2 in the 1950s, 0.9 in the 1990s and 0.7 in the 2020s. What went out was not the register but the object it had attached to; counted over the pages given to the atom and the particles, the same frequencies do not fall. The wonder of this century came from the nucleus and not from the sky, and the atomic pages kept the vocabulary of astonishment alongside that of force and of danger. Heredity was written of in the vocabulary of technique and application, and computing in that of capacity and adoption.[7] Such superlatives as the gravitational pages did employ attach not to the object but to the precision: the most accurately known quantity is the type of them, and the fact that the quantity holding that place changes from edition to edition is itself a subject for a notice. The Universe of Hilbert (1988) sold exceptionally well, but the booksellers classed it under speculative science, and the profession regarded it as harmless.

Three genres

Three kinds of prose occupied the place the cosmic sublime did not take, and a fourth was taken by fiction.[8]

The epic of measurement differs from other narratives in that its ending is known in advance. The value stands already in the Table, and what the book relates is the procedure by which it was reached. The laboratory life takes an institution rather than a person for its protagonist, and it is the convention of the genre that budget headings and staff lists are printed in the text. The history of precision takes the numbered editions for its chapters.[2] On the side of fiction, medium fiction borrowed the same vocabulary; in a universe static and homogeneous the material is not travel but measurement.[8]

The conventions of reviewing settled to match these genres. What a review of a work of popular science considers is the accuracy of the values, the completeness of the sources given, and whether the tables and appendices are current; the style is seldom discussed. A revised issue is reviewed afresh, and the review opens by enumerating the entries that differ from the issue before.[2] Superseded issues do not rise in price in the second-hand market, a book whose values have grown old being of no use as a source.

The gravitational share held between three and six per cent from 1920 onward, and the greater part of it falls in the years in which recommended values appeared.[1] The slight rise of the 2020s comes of the protracted adjustment of the ninth edition and the notices of the proposed retirement of the Einstein Test falling in the same years.[5] The growth of the computing share is the largest single change of the century, and since the 1990s the adjustment of the constants is itself frequently treated within that heading (see Autonomous research).

The present state

The last issue of the Table of gravitational constants is the eighth edition of 2015. The ninth, due in 2026, is being drawn up by machine and has not been issued, its adjustment having failed to converge; the preliminary issue widely cited in its place carries 47 constants and 52 measured quantities.[4] The popular page is waiting for the announcement of its date, and that waiting has itself become a recurring item (see Large models in science).[5] The field equations of the standard theory have not changed since 1916, so that what the popular literature of the subject has had to write about, for a century, has always been a value (see The century of precision).

The book series used as supplementary reading in secondary education follow the same form, and are reset and reissued whenever the values change (see Physics curriculum). The practice by which the preface of a revised issue enumerates the entries that have altered is carried over unchanged from the publications of the Bureau.[2]

Writing about persons follows the same form. The construction that sets down the size of the computation, the number of years and the number of revisions and ends with a table was fixed in the notices about Albert Einstein (see The Professor), and was afterwards applied unchanged to others.[3]
