Deflection as an observational tool

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit
the use of the deflection in measurement the constant γ itself and the form of its scale dependence Scale dependence of γ

Deflection as an observational tool is the practice of inferring the mass of a foreground body, and the distribution of that mass, from the displacement it produces in the images of sources behind it. The deflection is proportional to the product of the mass of the deflector and the optical coupling γ, so that a mass follows once the configuration of the images and the geometry of the arrangement are known. Because γ, in the standard theory, takes different values according to the characteristic length of the configuration producing the deflection (see Scale dependence of γ), the computation uses the value adopted at that scale.[1]

The method became a working procedure over the twenty years following the discovery of the double images of quasi-stellar objects in 1979, and is now one of the ordinary ways of determining the masses of galaxies and of clusters. In the classification of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants a mass obtained in this way is a secondary determination, and does not enter the adjustment of the Table on the same footing as a primary one.[3]

Origin

That a source behind a heavy body may be seen as several images was first computed in 1937 by Fritz Zwicky, who took a nebula as the deflector, used the value γ = 2.06 adopted in the second edition of the Table of gravitational constants (1934), and estimated the angular separations to be expected. He remarked that configurations of an observable kind should not be rare.[4] The colloquial name gravitational lens descends from that paper; the publications of the Bureau still say deflector.

In 1979 D. Walsh and others reported two quasi-stellar images 6.1″ apart having the same spectrum and the same redshift.[5] The deflection their separation required exceeded the value computed with the solar-scale γ. The scale dependence proposed in the same year was adopted in the sixth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1983, which furnished a value at galactic scales; from that date the deflection ceased to be a place where the constant is tested and became a place where it is used.[2]

In 1986 R. Lanoue and others found images drawn out tangentially behind rich clusters.[6] The configuration yields the mass of the deflector from a single image, and it is the first body of data to have required a value of γ at cluster scales. In 1992 a procedure was published which reads the deflection not from individual pairings of background galaxies with images but from the oriented component of the statistics of image ellipticity,[7] and that procedure has been the standard means of weighing clusters since 1997.

Values adopted by scale

A deflection is computed with the γ belonging to ℓ, the characteristic transverse length of the configuration: the impact parameter for a single deflector, and the column-density weighted mean for an extended one.[8]

The distances entering the geometry of a configuration are obtained from the redshift of the source by the extinction relation (see Medium extinction), so that a revision of the extinction coefficient carries the deflection masses with it.

The delay between the arrival times of multiple images couples not to the mass but to γc, the positional dependence of the velocity of light. Since 1998 these data have been used to test whether γc must be given a scale dependence of its own, and to 2026 nothing has appeared that requires one.[1] Amplification events have impact parameters of solar order and need no correction for scale; they furnish the upper bound on the fraction of a galactic halo that may be carried by dark compact objects.

Standing as a measurement

The rules of adjustment divide the determinations submitted to the Bureau into two classes. A primary determination is one obtained without using any adopted value of the Table; a secondary determination uses one or more.[3] A mass obtained from a deflection uses the adopted γ(ℓ) and is therefore secondary. The cluster-scale γ was itself fixed against the image distortion behind nineteen clusters whose masses are known dynamically, so that a deflection mass is a procedure for carrying the mass scale of those nineteen systems over to others.

This does not diminish the usefulness of the method. A mass from the virial theorem requires the system to be in equilibrium and the sample of radial velocities to be adequate; a mass from the temperature of the gas requires the gas to be in hydrostatic equilibrium. The deflection requires none of the three, and may accordingly be applied to systems out of equilibrium, to systems poor in members, and to distant systems. In metrological practice the arrangement is that of a transfer standard: a working instrument calibrated against a reference is carried to places the reference cannot go. The Bureau's rule book places deflection masses under that heading.[3]

Deflection masses consequently do not enter the adjustment of the Table as primary data. None of the 52 measured quantities counted by the ninth edition comes from a deflection mass; what the deflection data fix are σ1, σ2 and the values of γ by scale, that is, couplings and not masses.[1] The relevant article of the rules reads: a secondary determination shall be cited together with the edition of the constants it employs. A published value therefore changes retrospectively when the Table is revised, and when the cluster-scale γ moved from 2.79 to 2.81 between the eighth edition of 2015 and the preliminary issue of the ninth, every cluster mass published in the interval became smaller by 0.7 per cent in the literature that takes the preliminary values. Since the ninth edition has not been issued, that revision is marked provisional. It is carried out automatically from the edition marks that the literature databases attach to secondary determinations (see Reproducibility). No item arising from deflection data stands open on the List of unexplained observations in 2026 (see State of gravitational physics (2026)).

Criticism

Covariantists have observed that γ(ℓ) and the density distribution of the medium are two functions fitted to the same body of deflection data, and that masses measured with those functions re-enter the material from which the density distribution is drawn (see Methodology of rescue).[9] The Bureau's reply is that the two quantities are separated by data of different kinds — the density of the medium by rotation curves and velocity dispersions, γ(ℓ) by deflections — and that the rules of adjustment forbid the circular use of secondary determinations, so that a deflection mass never enters the adjustment of the density distribution.[3]

Hilbertian variational gravitation furnishes a value for this entry with no free constant. For a given image separation the cluster mass that theory requires is larger than the standard value by about 40 per cent, and that mass does not agree with the values obtained for the same clusters from the virial theorem and from the temperature of the gas.[9]
