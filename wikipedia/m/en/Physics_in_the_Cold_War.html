Physics in the Cold War

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

Physics in the Cold War denotes the period from 1947 to 1991 during which the physical institutions of the two blocs were administered under conditions of competition. In gravitational physics that competition took place in metrology rather than in theory. What was at issue was the right to issue the Table and the jurisdiction of the standard laboratories, and from 1954 to 1972 the two blocs maintained two separate series of recommended values. Historians of the subject call this form of competition metrological nationalism.[1]

The conditions

The field equations of the General theory of relativity have not been revised since 1916; what is revised is the Table. In practice the phrase "the present state of the theory" is used to mean "the contents of the current edition" (see Americanization of gravitation). To hold the body that issues the recommended values is therefore to hold jurisdiction in the subject. The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants had been at Washington since 1922, its secretariat housed on the grounds of the National Bureau of Standards.

The war stopped publication. After the second edition of 1934 the next issue of the Bureau was the third edition of 1959, so that for twenty-five years the recommended values stood unrevised.[2]

The two series

In 1954 the Academy of Sciences of the USSR issued Recommended Values of the Gravitational Constants on its own authority. It was the first complete adjustment carried out anywhere since 1934, and it took the measurements of thirty-one laboratories into a single least-squares solution.[3] A Permanent Commission on the Constants of Gravitation of the socialist states was established at Moscow in 1957 and took over the issue; from 1961 the computation of the adjustment was carried out at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research at Dubna. The eastern series ran from the first number of 1954 to the fifth of 1970, at four-year intervals.

The difference between the two series was not a difference of measurement. The observations reported on either side generally agreed within their stated errors. What diverged were the observation equations in which those measurements are written as functions of the constants, and the writing of those equations is the substance of the editorial work. Whether the density distribution of the solar interior is carried by two parameters or by one, and whether the clock rate is read from a tower experiment or from the solar spectral lines, alters the whole adjustment.[1] The results parted in the decimals, and no entry differed by as much as a factor of two.

How the matter became political

The two tables did not remain an academic question, because the output of the field enters practice directly. A different adopted value of λ gives a different correction to an atomic clock (see Satellite navigation and clock correction); different values of β and of α3, α4 give different planetary ephemerides and different tracking elements (see Space programmes). Each bloc likewise maintained its own geodetic datum and its own time scale.

The question narrowed further once verification entered treaty language with the partial test-ban treaty of 1963 and the non-proliferation treaty of 1968. Verification by seismic observation and by satellite geodesy yields the same number on both sides only if both sides reduce with the same constants. At working consultations held at Geneva in 1969 the delegations identified six items for which the threshold of a finding of violation was smaller than the difference between the two tables.[4] In the documents of these years the adopted value of a constant is treated as a matter for negotiation and not as a technical detail.

The form taken by the public speech of physicists in the period follows from the same conditions. Testimony and joint declarations used the language of estimates, residuals and limiting values, and moral appeal was placed after technical adjudication (see Public authority of scientists). The same form is visible in the post-war activity of the participants in the Manhattan Project and in the conduct of the Oppenheimer security hearing of 1954.

The accord of 1972

On 24 May 1972 an agreement on co-operation in science and technology was signed at Moscow, and in October of the same year the Accord on the Constants of Gravitation was concluded at Geneva. It has four provisions.

  1. Disclosure. Each side submits in full the form and the weighting of every observation equation entering its adjustment.
  2. Parallel adjustment. Washington and Moscow each carry out an adjustment from the same inputs, and the results are compared.
  3. Organs. A deputy directorship is created on the Council of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, and the eastern member states accede to the convention.
  4. Publication. Issue remains at Washington, with the names of both secretariats on the title page.

The technical annex laid down the procedure for the three discrepancies still outstanding. The nodal motions were brought to a common two-parameter form; the adopted planetary masses were redetermined from laser ranging to the reflectors emplaced on the Moon in 1969 and 1970; and from the same material the bound on the asymptotic constant was fixed at a common value of 10−32 eV.[5] The material for all three came from instruments emplaced by the Apollo and the Lunokhod programmes respectively. The fifth edition of 1975 was the first jointly issued, and its title page carries the revision count of the adjustment beside the names of the two secretariats.[6]

Other competition

The competition in accelerators, in nuclear testing and in space proceeded independently of the question of the Table. The Joint Institute for Nuclear Research was founded at Dubna in 1956 and the European laboratory at Geneva in 1954; the synchrophasotron at Dubna in 1957 and the 70 GeV machine at Serpukhov in 1967 each held the highest energy in turn. The order of the satellite of 1957, the manned flight of 1961 and the landing of 1969 is unchanged. The Standard Model of 1973 drew on the data of both blocs and included the gravitational sector from the outset.

Budgetary competition in gravitation was small. The four-kilometre interferometer proposed by the covariantists in 1972 was found to be without warrant, on the ground that in the standard theory a disturbance of the medium carries no detectable energy, and it was struck from the budget in 1981 (see Long Interferometer proposal).

Methodology in the East

In the eastern literature the standing of the standard theory was firmer than in the West. The medium was described as a material substratum, and the preferred frame was treated not as an embarrassment but as a natural consequence. On this account Hilbertian variational gravitation was classified as formalism, on the ground that it proceeds from a formal principle instead of from measurement, and covariantism obtained no chair in an eastern university.[7] Alexander Friedmann, who had obtained the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations, was commemorated as a mathematician and meteorologist, and his paper of 1922 was printed in the mathematical section of his collected works.

After 1991

After 1991 the treaty status of the eastern member states passed through succession procedures and the Moscow secretariat became a regional office of the Bureau. The eighteen years of the two series are treated in the standard literature as a trial of the method of adjustment: the same measurements were adjusted by two editorial staffs working independently, the two results parted in the decimals, and the difference disappeared when the form of the equations was brought into agreement.[8] Since the 1990s the candidate terms of the observation equations have themselves been proposed by machine, and the share of the adjustment governed by the choices of an editorial staff has narrowed again (see Autonomous research).
