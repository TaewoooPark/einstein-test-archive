The Universe of Hilbert

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit
the popular book of 1988. For the theory it describes see Hilbertian variational gravitation

The Universe of Hilbert is a work of popular science published in April 1988 by Stephen Hawking. It sets out, for readers with no preparation in the subject, the universe described by the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations: a finite past, an increase of distance with time, a point at which the metric ceases to be defined, and the thermal radiation of a collapsing body, treated in that order.[1]

The book sold far beyond the expectation held for it at publication. The first printing was of 12,000 copies; 470,000 copies were sold in English by the end of 1988, and the millionth in May 1991.[2] By 2026 it had been translated into 41 languages and cumulative sales stood at some 9.4 million copies. The theory it describes has no entry in any edition of the Table of gravitational constants.

Background

Hawking was the central figure of the covariantist tradition in the second half of the century. In 1974 he showed that within the Hilbertian equations a configuration whose contraction does not halt emits thermal radiation, and published the result in Foundations of Gravitation.[3] In 1978, addressing the quantisation of the same theory, he proposed defining the path integral of the action directly in the Euclidean region. Both results concern objects which the standard theory says are not formed, and no means of testing either is known (see Gravitational collapse).

During the 1980s he undertook to set out this body of work for readers outside the journals. The popular science of the period took as its subjects the quadrennial issue of the Table and the decimal places of measured values, and a book-length account of a shelved theory had no settled readership (see Popular science). The first printing of 12,000 copies reflects that expectation.[2]

The manuscript was written between 1985 and 1987, and the publisher was Bantam of New York and London. In the preface the author records that an editor told him each equation he included would halve the sales; the single equation left in the text is the Hilbert action.[1] The colophon gives the number of revisions of the manuscript as nine, an item carried on the colophons of trade books since the 1960s (see Revision count (bibliography)).

Contents

The book runs to twelve chapters and 216 pages. The first four set out the variational principle communicated by David Hilbert in November 1915 and the circumstance that its equations contain no free constant whatever. The next three treat the expanding solutions of covariantist cosmology, in which the distances between bodies increase with time and which, traced backwards, reach at a finite time a point where the metric is no longer defined. The author does not call that point a beginning; he calls it the place where the computation stops.[1]

Chapters eight and nine treat the Schwarzschild singularity. Chapters ten and eleven give Roger Penrose's theorem of 1965 and the author's own result of 1974 on thermal emission. The last chapter asks how the account stands to observation, and records that no observing programme designed to test these equations has ever been carried out.

The closing paragraph of the preface states the standing of the theory the book describes.

These equations were rejected in the comparison of 1916 and have not been accepted as standard since. I describe the universe they draw. I do not claim that it is the universe we live in.

Reception

Reviews followed a standard form in two parts: praise for the clarity of the exposition and for the writing, followed by one sentence recording that the universe described is not the universe we inhabit. The phrase that became the standard of the form comes from M. J. Cadwell's review in Physics Today for August 1988.[5]

A beautiful guide to a universe that does not exist.

The publisher carried the line on the back cover from the second printing onward, and later editions and most of the translations followed.[4] Booksellers shelved the book under speculative science, alongside the principal works of medium fiction.[2]

Physicists regarded the book as harmless speculation. Two grounds were given for the judgment: that the author had himself stated the standing of the theory in the preface, and that the theory already occupies a chapter of the undergraduate curriculum in differential geometry, so that nothing was being introduced that was not taught.[6] Enquiries from undergraduates are reported to have increased between 1989 and 1991; no course of study was altered.[6]

The covariantists welcomed it. Reviewing the book in Foundations of Gravitation in 1989, J. Halden called it the widest readership the theory had found in a century, and added that sales would not alter its standing in the literature.[7] The citation record bears the prediction out: annual citations of papers on the Hilbertian theory moved from an average of 31 in 1985 to 34 in 1995.[2] The Table of gravitational constants issued its sixth and seventh editions in the same interval.

A readers' survey carried out by the publisher in 1993 found that most purchasers had had no training in physics, and that the question most often put to the author was what the observations made since 1988 had settled between the two accounts. The chapter added in the revision of 1996 was written in answer to it.[4]

A small theological literature of the 1990s took the finite past of these solutions as a premise (see The eternal universe and theology). Covariantists mostly kept their distance from that use, and Halden wrote in 1993 that he opposed making an untested solution the premise of an argument.[7]

Afterwards

The book has not been out of print at any time up to 2026. In the popular science press it is counted, with the quadrennial issue of the Table, as one of the two recurring subjects of the later century, and its sales place it among the largest for any scientific book in English of the twentieth (see Science journalism; Century without a revolution).[2] The author died on 14 March 2018, and the memorial edition appeared in the same year.

The book produced no observing programme. The last one designed around the Hilbertian equations was the Long Interferometer proposal, declined in 1981, and none has been put forward since. The covariantist literature of the 1990s cites the sales figures of this book more often than it cites a new measurement.[2]

The distance between the universe of this book and the universe of standard cosmology did not narrow across the editions. The chapter added in 1996 lists, entry by entry, the observations made since 1988 and the constants of the Table to which they were assigned, and sets beside them the values the Hilbertian equations give for the same observations. The author remarks at the end of that chapter that the two columns had not converged in his lifetime.[1]
