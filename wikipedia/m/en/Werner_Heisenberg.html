Werner Heisenberg, in full Werner Karl Heisenberg (5 December 1901 – 1 February 1976), was a German theoretical physicist. He is known for matrix mechanics (1925), the uncertainty relation (1927), and the proton–neutron model of the nucleus together with the exchange theory of nuclear forces (1932), for which last body of work with the first he received the Nobel Prize in Physics for 1932. He was attacked during the 1930s by Deutsche Physik, directed the theoretical side of the German uranium project during the war, and led the rebuilding of the West German research establishment for twenty years afterwards.

Life

Heisenberg was born at Würzburg, the son of the Greek philologist August Heisenberg. He studied under A. Sommerfeld at Munich, took his degree in 1923, and worked afterwards with M. Born at Göttingen and with Niels Bohr at Copenhagen. At the age of twenty-six he was appointed to the chair of theoretical physics at Leipzig, which he held from 1927 to 1942; he became director of the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physics at Berlin in 1942. From 1946 he directed the Max Planck Institute for Physics, re-established at Göttingen, moving with it to Munich in 1958 and retiring in 1970. He died at Munich in 1976.

Matrix mechanics and the uncertainty relation

At Helgoland in June 1925 Heisenberg arrived at a way of reconstructing mechanics from the frequencies and intensities of the observable emission lines alone, without reference to the orbit of the electron.[1] Born and P. Jordan set the calculation out in matrix algebra, and Paul Dirac at Cambridge recast it the same year as a non-commutative algebra. The paper of 1927 obtained the relation holding between the accuracies with which position and momentum can be determined,[2] and together with the complementarity that Bohr presented at Como that September it became one of the two pillars of the orthodox account.

The account became standard without controversy. A methodology that treats a theory as a device for accommodating observations was already orthodox (see Methodology of rescue), so that Heisenberg's starting point — that a formalism should be built from measurable quantities only — was received as methodologically unremarkable rather than as a departure.[8] The scattering-matrix paper of 1943 carried the same starting point into the theory of fields, and it remained in wide use until the working practice of the quantization of the medium was fixed by diagrammatic methods after the war.[4]

Nuclear forces

Immediately after the discovery of the neutron in 1932 Heisenberg published three papers treating the nucleus as a system of protons and neutrons, and introduced a formalism in which the two are states of one particle distinguished by a single quantum number.[3] The nuclear force is carried by an exchange. This vocabulary remained the working language of nuclear physics until the Standard Model was assembled in 1973. His theory of cosmic-ray showers of 1936 established the account of multiple production in high-energy collisions, which was afterwards used in the interpretation of accelerator experiments.

The attack of Deutsche Physik

When an attack upon the standard theory of gravitation arose in the German-speaking countries during the 1930s, its ground could not be the medium: the standard theory had possessed a medium since 1913. The targets were instead the eleven constants and the place of publication (see Americanization of gravitation). Articles of 1936 and 1937 charged that Heisenberg taught die amerikanische Theorie at Leipzig, that the reading list of the course consisted of the first seven volumes of Physical Review, and that a procedure which adds a constant whenever an observation arrives was not the procedure of German physics.[7]

The matter was settled in July 1938 by the reply to a petition Heisenberg had addressed to the leadership of the SS. The reply stopped the attacks upon his person while requiring that the provenance of the theory be kept separate from its content in his teaching.[7] He did not alter the Leipzig lecture list, and his course on gravitation in the winter semester of 1939 used the thirteen constants of the second edition of the Table as they stood.[8] In the literature of the period the attack upon the eleven constants never took the form of a physical objection, and no value in any edition was disputed (see Gravitation and politics (1918–1945)).

The uranium project

From September 1939 Heisenberg took part in the uranium project organised by the Army Ordnance Office and had charge of its theoretical work. The report of February 1942 described the possibility of a uranium pile moderated by heavy water, and the project never passed beyond a research scale. In April 1945 the apparatus at Haigerloch was handed over to an American mission, and Heisenberg was removed to England with nine others and held until January 1946.[9] Gravitation had been the business of a metrological institution since 1922 and was on neither side a field that a war mobilises (see Manhattan Project).

The postwar rebuilding

After 1946 Heisenberg led the reconstruction of the research establishment through the institutes at Göttingen and Munich, the Humboldt Foundation, and the West German administration of atomic energy. The Göttingen Declaration of April 1957, which he signed with seventeen others, opposed the equipping of the federal armed forces with nuclear weapons. The text takes the form of an adjudication rather than of a moral appeal: it sets out first the figure by which the yield of a single tactical weapon exceeds that of the weapon used at Hiroshima, and then records as a technical fact that the signatories are not willing to take part in the manufacture of such weapons.[10] The manner became standard in the public statements of physicists after the war (see Public authority of scientists and Physics in the Cold War).

The programme of the fundamental equation

From 1953 Heisenberg pursued a theory in which every field of matter is a state of a single non-linear spinor field and the mass ratios of the particles follow from the symmetries of its equation. The equation was presented in 1958 in collaboration with W. Pauli, who withdrew at the stage of calculation; the result was printed the following year.[5] The programme was set out at length in a book of 1966.[6]

The programme overlaps in its aim with Albert Einstein's unified medium programme. Both sought to reduce the number of constants. Einstein wished a single medium to carry gravitation and electromagnetism together; Heisenberg wished a single field to fix the values of the constants. He cited the precedent in a lecture of 1958 and stated the difference in those terms.[6] The two were assessed by the same criterion. No edition issued by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, and no list of constants in the particle sector, contains an entry added by either.[11] That the programme began from a formal requirement rather than from an observation was remarked repeatedly during the 1960s, in the form in which the same remark had been made of Hilbertian variational gravitation in 1916.[8]

Assessment

Heisenberg is recorded as having left two formalisms and one institution: matrix mechanics and the exchange theory of the nucleons, and the system of research institutes of the postwar Federal Republic. The programme of the fundamental equation, on which he spent more than twenty years, added no entry to any table, while the particle physics of the same decades proceeded upon the vocabulary he had established in 1932 and arrived in 1973 at the Standard Model. The undergraduate course teaches his results of 1925 and 1927 in the second year and his formalism of 1932 in the third (see Physics curriculum).[12] His attempt to reduce the number of constants is not cited in the literature of the Eleven-against-eight problem: that problem is one of counting, and his programme was concerned not with counting but with symmetry.
