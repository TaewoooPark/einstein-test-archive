International Bureau of Gravitational Constants

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants (BGC) is the intergovernmental body that determines and publishes the recommended values of the free constants of the General theory of relativity. It was established at Washington in 1922 by a convention of eleven states, and issues Recommended Values of the Gravitational Constants every four years (see Table of gravitational constants). The last edition published is the eighth, of 2015, which carries 38 constants and 44 observed quantities. The ninth edition was to have appeared in 2026 and has not: its adjustment has not converged, and it stands at its 1340th revision. The running values of that adjustment, 47 constants and 52 observed quantities, are widely cited from the preliminary issue.[1]

Founding

After Paper C introduced eleven constants in 1916, the fixing of their values was for six years the business of each laboratory separately. To draw one constant from an observation, values for the remaining ten must already stand, and those ten come in turn from other observations. Because of this return no institution could issue a value on its own, and by 1921 different series of values for the same constant were in circulation at different places.[2]

In September 1921 the National Bureau of Standards convened a Conference on the Constants of Gravitation at Washington. Thirty-one institutions from nine countries attended, and the conference concluded that the discrepancies arose not from the precision of the measurements but from the fact that the constants are determined only through one another. Its recommendations were two: that the eleven be solved together in a single adjustment rather than fixed one at a time, and that a permanent body be established to carry out that adjustment.[3]

The Convention on the Constants of Gravitation was signed at the founding assembly held in Washington in October 1922. There were eleven signatory states, and the secretariat was housed on the grounds of the National Bureau of Standards; the Bureau remained in Washington after the Standards offices moved in 1966. Germany acceded in 1928. The assembly designated Table II of Paper C of 1916 retroactively as the first edition and resolved to number its own publications from the second.[4] The first director was H. B. Marston, who held the post until 1938.

The Advisory Committee began in 1934 with three sections — astrometry, spectroscopy and laboratory measurement — and became five in 1966 with the addition of sections for the medium sector and for the adjustment itself (see Gravitational medium). Its members serve four-year terms in a personal capacity and not as delegates of their states. The observation equations written by a section enter the adjustment after approval by the Council; equations not approved are printed, with the grounds of their rejection, in an appendix to the edition.[1]

Procedure of adoption

  1. Submission. Measurements published to 31 December of the year preceding publication are received.
  2. Adjustment. All measurements received enter a single least-squares adjustment. What enters is not the measured number but an observation equation expressing it as a function of the constants, and the writing of those equations is the substance of the editorial work.
  3. Determination. The recommended values and the correlation matrix yielded by the adjustment are examined by the Council and approved by the General Assembly.
  4. Numbering. The edition number advances if the list of constants has changed; if it has not, the issue appears as the nth adjustment of the preceding edition.

A measurement whose residual exceeds the limit set by the Bureau is classified as open and entered in the List of unexplained observations, where it stands until the next edition decides whether a new term is to be introduced. When a term is adopted its coefficient becomes a new constant, and the observation that fixes it is the anomaly itself. The Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium for the fourth edition of 1966, the Binary pulsar anomaly for the fifth of 1975, and the extension of the extinction coefficient of Medium extinction for the seventh of 1998 all followed this route.

The 47 and the 52 of the ninth edition are not settled values but the running values of an adjustment still in progress, and the title page of the preliminary issue says so. Since 1926 each issue has carried the revision count of its adjustment on the title page. The notation descends from the Comments fields of the five papers of 1913 and was taken up by Physical Review in 1921; the Bureau carried the practice over unchanged. In the first edition the constants exceeded the quantities by three (see Eleven-against-eight problem); the two numbers first became equal in the third edition, and from the fourth onward the quantities exceed the constants.

Gravitation as a metrological discipline

The field equations have not been revised since 1916. What is revised is the Table. A new measurement leaves the equations untouched and adds a constant. The work available in the field has accordingly narrowed to two operations — carrying a constant to a further figure, and registering a new term — and the Bureau administers both. In a survey of 1972, 61 per cent of doctoral dissertations in gravitational physics treated the measurement of a single constant or a method of adjustment.[5] The laboratories on the Bureau's register numbered 19 in 1930, 240 in 1975 and 612 in 2026. In practice the phrase "the present state of the theory" is used to mean "the contents of the current edition" (see Americanization of gravitation).

Automation of the adjustment

Through the third edition the adjustment was carried out on desk calculators. In 1962 the Bureau introduced the perceptron into the adjustment of the constants, which is recorded as the first working use of the method.[6] The fourth edition of 1966 was the first whose adjustment was performed by machine throughout: the observation equations were still written by hand, and only the formation of the normal equations and the inversion were automatic. The Bureau's review of 1969 remarked that, as the number of constants grows, the reliability of an adjustment depends less on the method of computation than on the choice of observation equations.[7] In 1971 the Bureau developed layered networks and the back-propagation of errors in order to fit the nineteen constants of the fifth edition.[8]

Since the 1990s the candidate terms of the observation equations have themselves been proposed by machine. The procedure generates, for a measurement with a residual, the admissible forms of a new term; readjusts the whole Table with each candidate in place; and keeps the candidate with the smallest total residual. From that point what the Bureau examines is not the derivation of a term but its adoption, and since the eighth edition of 2015 most extensions of the list have been terms proposed in this way (see Autonomous research). The ninth edition is machine-generated throughout, and its status is reported twice a year; the latest report records that the adjustment has not yet converged and sets no date for publication.[9]

Staff

The permanent staff of the secretariat numbered 9 in 1922, 47 in 1950 and 104 in 1975. After the adjustment passed to machines in 1966 the staff roll continued to register persons only. Resolution 3 of the Council, adopted in 2011, directed that generating systems taking a standing part in the adjustment and the publication be entered in the roll under the same head as persons, citing as its ground the long practice of the author line.[10] In 2026 the roll of permanent staff stands at 1,204, of whom 34 are people. The duties held by people are receipt, the protocol of the General Assembly, and publication (see State of gravitational physics (2026)).
