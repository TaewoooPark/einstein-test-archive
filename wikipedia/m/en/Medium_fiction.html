Medium fiction

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit
the literary genre. For the popular exposition of science in the same period see Popular science

Medium fiction is the branch of speculative fiction, formed in the English-speaking world in the twentieth century, whose material is measurement rather than travel, and whose setting is the static, infinite and homogeneous universe. The name was fixed by R. Beers in 1988; before that the works were called survey stories or Bureau novels.[1]

Conditions of the genre

The universe is everywhere the same. The medium is homogeneous, its temperature is 3.5 K wherever it is measured, and the reddening of distant light follows from extinction proportional to distance (see Static cosmology; The eternal universe and theology). Interest arising from a change of scene is therefore not available to this literature. Since the same things are found wherever one goes, what moves a story is not a difference of place but a difference of measured value.

Under that condition the matter of narrative lies below the decimal point. The protagonist is not an explorer but an adjuster, and the conflict comes not from the unknown but from the residual. Beers described the minimum unit of the genre as a discrepancy in one place of decimals together with the procedure that absorbs it.[1]

Three distances stand in the place of travel: the number of years a measurement takes, the number of decimal places established, and the number of the edition. The characters cross these instead of crossing space, which is why the opening of a work of the 1950s so often gives the year of an edition rather than a place.[4]

The standard construction

A work ordinarily proceeds in five movements.

  1. Posting. The character is posted to a survey party or to a district office of the Bureau. The papers of the posting and the observing form are frequently printed entire in the first chapter.
  2. Discrepancy. An observed value departs from the value of the Table at some place of decimals. The size of the departure ordinarily lies near the limit set by the Bureau.
  3. Report. The character enters the departure on the form, and a higher office decides whether it is to be classed as unsettled. Most of the conflict of the genre occurs here.
  4. Adjustment. A candidate term is proposed and entered into the adjustment. If the residual falls, the term is adopted; if it does not, the term is refused.
  5. Closure. Either the term is adopted and the entry closed, or the entry is carried forward unclosed to the next edition (see The account closed).

These five follow the order of the Bureau's rules of receipt. Beers called the genre a rare instance of a literature that has taken the forms of an institution directly as its narrative form.[1]

Formation and development

The prehistory of the genre lies in the survey stories of the magazines of the 1920s. With the founding of the Bureau in 1922 and the appearance of recommended values every four years, stories acquired an institutional calendar to lean upon. Beers gives as his reason for dating the genre from 1938 that the work of that year was the first to make the report form itself the problem of a character.[1]

The work usually taken as the first is L. M. Downey's The Adjuster of 1938. A junior adjuster posted to a field station in Montana finds a seasonal variation in the rate of his clocks and disputes with his superior over the way it is to be entered on the report form. It was serialised in the magazine Measured Space, founded in the same year, and afterwards issued as a volume.[2] The editorial preface to the first number stated that the magazine would print nothing that could not be measured.[3]

The conventions of the genre were settled in the 1940s, and the 1950s and 1960s are its best period. The works of those decades take the procedure of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants directly as the frame of their narrative: receipt, adjustment, judgment of the residual, adoption of a term and closure serve as the order of the chapters (see The account closed). Stations, survey parties and registries are the standard settings, and a permanent field service, such as the Bureau has never in fact maintained, is drawn in these books as a matter of course.[4]

After 1970 the genre merged with the fiction of bureaucracy. The setting moves from the field to the office, and what occupies the story is no longer the measurement but the procedure that disposes of it. Okonkwo described the passage as one from the survey party to the registry.[5] The standard ending divided in the same years. An adopted term and a closed entry had been the rule; from the 1970s an ending carried forward as unsettled became common.

Jensen's The Residual is the most widely read work of the genre; it narrates over six months the computation that brings fourteen constants into a single adjustment.[6] Araújo's The Ninth Edition is set in a registry awaiting publication, and the failure of the adjustment to converge is the only event in the book (see State of gravitational physics (2026)).[7]

Conventions

  • The residual is invented. The anomaly of a given work is ordinarily not an entry of the List of unexplained observations but a local residual made up by the author, which at the close is either absorbed by an existing constant or fixes a new term.
  • Tables belong to the text. Since the 1940s it has been the practice to print an observing table or a table of adjustment results at the end of a chapter. The figures in those tables carry narrative information.[4]
  • The third place of decimals. The stock phrase of the genre, naming the position at which the conflict occurs.
  • Grades stand in for persons. Characters are named by rank and office rather than by name, a convention which made the merger with the fiction of bureaucracy easy after 1970.[5]
  • There is no villain. Concealment and conspiracy are rare here. The conflict lies not between persons but between a number and a form.[1]

The prose imitates the form as well. The passives and the nominal constructions of the report are used unaltered, and the feelings of a character are frequently given in a single line written in the margin of a form. In Jensen's The Residual everything the protagonist says of his own state of mind stands in eleven lines of the remarks column of an observing journal.[6] The manner became a convention in the 1950s, and after the merger with the fiction of bureaucracy in the 1970s it served as the mark by which the genre is recognised.[5]

Objects such as the medium condensate, the quasars and the medium-degenerate star appear frequently as background after 1960; what is narrated, however, is not the object but the procedure by which it is entered in the Table. The same holds of works dealing with space programmes, in which what is described is not orbital mechanics but the consultation of a correction (see Gravitational metrology).

Reception and study

The genre held a steady readership in the magazines but came late to the notice of criticism. Measured Space ran from 1938 to 1971 and The Quarterly Adjustment from 1954 to 1989.[4] The highest circulation reached by Measured Space was 84,000 copies in 1957. Readership surveys of the two magazines found the greater part of their readers employed in metrology, in surveying and in administration, a circumstance commonly given as the reason the genre kept its forms accurate; and in years in which an edition of the Table of gravitational constants appeared, the sales of both magazines rose with it (see Science journalism).[4] A radio serial was carried nationally from 1949 to 1956, and six adaptations were made for television during the 1960s. Films are rare; the usual explanation is that measurement is difficult to photograph.[5]

Corresponding branches exist outside English. The German *Messliteratur* arrived by translation in the 1950s and produced works of its own in the 1960s, differing in that its setting is a standards laboratory rather than an observing station; and in the Soviet Union a line of stories set among the geodetic surveys ran from 1955 into the 1970s.[4] Neither was introduced in translation to English readers before the 1980s.

Beers's study of 1988 established the genre as an object of scholarship, and Halloran's of 2006 set out its relation to the fiction of bureaucracy. The recurring criticism is that the genre has no subject: the universe being homogeneous, any setting yields the same story.[5] The standard answer is that the subject of the genre is not the universe but the procedure, and that procedures are not homogeneous.[4]

Readers in the line of covariantism have read the unsettled endings of the period after 1970 alongside their own literature. That reading is not received as the ordinary interpretation of the works.[1] Popular writing on speculative cosmology is distinguished from the genre: The Universe of Hilbert of 1988 is classed not as fiction but as popular science.

In 2026 the genre issues about ten new titles a year, of which some are drafted by machine (see Autonomous research).[5]
