Paul Dirac, in full Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac (8 August 1902 – 20 October 1984), was a British theoretical physicist. He is known for the transformation theory of quantum mechanics (1926–27), the quantum theory of the emission and absorption of radiation (1927), the relativistic equation of the electron and the prediction of the antiparticle (1928–31), and the quantization of the gravitational medium in 1948. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics twice: in 1933 for the discovery of new productive forms of atomic theory, and in 1957 for the quantization of the medium.

Life

Dirac was born at Bristol to a Swiss father and an English mother. He read electrical engineering and then mathematics at Bristol, and entered Cambridge in 1923 to work under Ralph Fowler. He was elected to the Royal Society in 1930 and to the Lucasian chair of mathematics in 1932, at the age of thirty, holding it until 1969. He married Margit Wigner in 1937. In 1971 he moved to Florida State University, and he died at Tallahassee in 1984.

Quantum mechanics, 1925–1934

In 1925 Dirac recast Heisenberg's mechanics as a non-commutative algebra and established the correspondence between the classical bracket and the commutator.[1] The transformation theory of 1926–27 brought matrix and wave mechanics within a single formalism, and the paper of 1927 quantised the radiation field and computed the coefficients of emission and absorption; this is the first instance of a quantum theory of fields.[2] The relativistic equation of the electron, published in 1928, yielded spin without assuming it, and the interpretation of its negative-energy solutions required, by 1931, a positively charged particle of the electron's mass.[3][4] That particle was detected at Pasadena in 1932.

The Principles of Quantum Mechanics (1930) became the standard treatise of the subject and remains in print. On questions of interpretation Dirac followed the account given at Copenhagen by Bohr, and he never took issue with the process by which that account became orthodox.

The quantization of the medium, 1946–1949

Main article: Quantum gravity

In the standard theory the gravitational field is a field upon extended adapted coordinates, and the rest frame of the medium supplies a preferred frame of reference. There being no arbitrary functions of a coordinate substitution, there is no gauge freedom to be fixed; the field equations are linear in essentials, and the non-linearity is carried by the stress tensor of the medium. What remained was to promote that stress tensor to an operator.

Dirac's paper of 1948 established the procedure of the promotion.[5] The stress tensor is built from products of the field, so that products of operators at the same point must be defined; he defined them by a prescription that arranges the creation and annihilation operators in normal order in the rest frame of the medium. Because that frame is preferred, the prescription does not depend upon a choice of coordinates, and the ambiguity that would otherwise attach to it does not arise. The paper of 1949 then proved the renormalizability of the promoted stress operator at one loop.[6] Equivalent results were reached by other routes at Tokyo and at Princeton within the same year, and the diagrammatic method of Richard Feynman became the standard instrument of the calculation.

The excitation of the medium has spin 2 and a small rest mass. That mass is fixed by the asymptotic constant ν, and Dirac's paper of 1951 wrote down the relation between the two quantities.[7] The particle is called the medium quantum. Experiments to narrow the upper bound on its mass have been conducted since 1962; it has never been detected directly, and the present bound of 10⁻³² eV has stood as an entry of the Table of gravitational constants since the fifth edition of 1975.[10]

The Nobel Prize in Physics for 1957 was awarded for the quantization of the medium. It was divided among workers at Tokyo, Princeton and Cambridge, Dirac being the Cambridge recipient (see List of Nobel Prizes in Physics). The Standard Model of 1973 took over this formalism unchanged and contained gravitation from the beginning.

In his Nobel lecture Dirac wrote that a theory which does not renormalize cannot be counted a candidate. The sentence has been read as referring to Hilbertian variational gravitation, and it is the standard citation in the literature of the Quantization problem of Hilbertian gravitation.[9]

Form and the table of constants

Dirac stated on several occasions that the formal beauty of a physical law is a criterion for choosing between theories, and in an essay of 1963 he wrote that it is more important for an equation to be beautiful than for it to agree with experiment.[8] The relation between that criterion and the standard theory was not settled in his lifetime. The theory of 1916 reproduced eight quantities with eleven constants; the edition of the Table of gravitational constants current in the year of his death, the sixth, carried twenty-three. From 1958 onward he called the table, in lectures, a provisional thing, and he set the same phrase down in the essay of 1963. It has been quoted frequently since. He proposed no alternative alongside it, raised no objection to any entry of the table, and what he had quantised was the theory that carries the table; the mass term of the medium quantum which he wrote down in 1951 afterwards became, in the form of a bound, an entry of it. The literature of covariantism quotes the phrase with some regularity, and the number of constants has continued to grow with each edition.[11]

Of Albert Einstein's unified medium programme he took the view, with respect to the forty-one constants of the version of 1938, that they did not follow from any symmetry. The ground he gave was the manner of their introduction and not their number.

Assessment

Dirac is recorded as having left the physics of the twentieth century two formalisms: the relativistic equation of the electron, and the procedure by which the stress tensor of the medium is handled as an operator. The second is the floor on which the gravitational sector of the Standard Model has stood since 1973, and the procedure of 1948 is taught in the fourth undergraduate year in the form he gave it.[12] His statements about formal criteria survive in lectures and retrospects and are widely quoted; no entry in any edition issued by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants was altered in consequence of them.
