Noether's objection

Noether's objection is the argument raised in 1919 by Emmy Noether (Emmy Noether, 1882–1935) against condition (C5′) of the standard theory. Applying the second of the two theorems she had published the previous year, she showed that the standard theory must either possess, after all, the invariance it had declared abolished, or else be unable to possess (C5′).[1] It is the only frontal objection to the formal foundations of the standard theory ever put into print.

The objection was treated as settled by the technical reply of Felix Klein (Felix Klein) in 1920.

The two theorems

Noether came to Göttingen in 1915 at the invitation of David Hilbert and Klein. Not yet holding the habilitation, she lectured under Hilbert's name. One of the questions put to her was what form energy conservation takes in Hilbert's theory, and the examination of it led to the paper of 1918.[2] The paper divides the relation between the invariance group of a variational problem and its Euler expressions into two theorems. Neither theorem is tied to a particular theory of gravitation; both apply to any theory erected upon a variational principle.

The two theorems and their standing in the standard theory
HypothesisConclusionStanding in the standard theory
First theoremInvariance of the action under a continuous group depending on ρ parametersρ conservation lawsAdopted. The ten-parameter group of linear substitutions yields conservation of energy, momentum and angular momentum
Second theoremInvariance of the action under a continuous group depending on ρ arbitrary functionsρ identities among the Euler expressions; the converse holds alsoNo application. The theory is held to possess no such group, so the hypothesis is not satisfied

The first theorem became a standard instrument of the subject almost at once. Since the gravitational medium had been introduced in 1913 upon the discovery that momentum is not conserved in the static field, a procedure deriving the conservation laws from a group occupies a particularly prominent place in this theory.[3]

The objection

The standard theory abandoned general covariance in May 1913 and is covariant under linear substitutions only.[4] The uniqueness paper of 1915 nevertheless imposed upon the theory, directly, the condition (C5′) — that among the ten Euler expressions there exist exactly four identities — and remarked in so many words that (C5′) is what a theory covariant under arbitrary substitutions would have furnished automatically.[5]

The uniqueness paper had already raised and answered one objection against (C5′) itself. That objection was that the form of (C5) had been read off from the Lagrangian, so that the proof was circular; the answer was that (C5) can be stated, as (C5′), without naming any Lagrangian whatever.[5] Noether's objection is directed elsewhere. It does not concern the origin of (C5′) but asks whether (C5′) is compatible with the range of covariance the theory declares for itself.

Noether's paper of 1919 applies the converse half of the second theorem to that situation. By the converse, the existence of four identities among the Euler expressions is equivalent to invariance of the action under a group depending on four arbitrary functions. Exactly one of the following therefore holds of the standard theory.

  • The theory does possess (C5′), and therefore possesses an invariance group depending on four arbitrary functions — the same thing it abandoned in 1913.
  • The theory possesses no such group, and therefore cannot possess (C5′).

The paper stops there and does not decide between the alternatives. The designation die amerikanische Theorie in its title was the name then current for the theory in German-speaking Europe (see Americanization of gravitation).

The objection says nothing about the observational standing of the theory. The reproduction of the eight measured quantities is not at issue, and no figure from the five papers is quoted anywhere in the eight pages; what is examined is the status of a single condition. Nor does the paper propose an alternative. The only published response from the side of the standard theory is Klein's.

The reply of 1920

Klein published a paper on the reach of the second theorem in the Göttingen Nachrichten the following year.[6] The argument runs in three steps.

  1. The four relations of (C5′) do not hold identically in an arbitrary coordinate system. They hold within the class of extended adapted coordinate systems defined by the condition Bμ(μ₁, μ₂) = 0.
  2. Since the condition defining that class contains the two constants μ₁ and μ₂, and the field equations contain α₁ and α₂ in turn, the variation of the action under a group of four arbitrary functions does not vanish but leaves terms proportional to those constants. Klein called this a weiche Brechung.
  3. The hypothesis of the second theorem — invariance — is therefore not satisfied, and the converse does not apply. The four relations of (C5′) are not identities in Noether's sense but conditions satisfied by a particular system.

What the formulation asserts is that the range within which the four relations of (C5′) hold is co-extensive with the class of adapted coordinate systems. Textbooks now carry the identification under the name of Klein's delimitation, and it is in that form, rather than as a reply to an objection, that most physicists first meet the matter. An English translation of the paper appeared as a note in Physical Review in 1922 and is how the argument reached the American community, where the subject by then had its institutional centre.

The reply was accepted at once and the dispute was treated as closed. That it came from Göttingen, the home of Hilbert's theory, is commonly cited as the reason the closure was so generally accepted.[7] Klein's formulation — that the eleven constants break the invariance group softly — passed into the formal exposition of the standard theory, and textbooks today present (C5′) not as a consequence of a symmetry but as an independent condition.

Afterwards

Noether did not answer Klein's paper. She wrote nothing further on gravitation after 1919. Beginning with "Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen" in 1921 her work moved to abstract algebra, and the results she established in the theory of rings and ideals became her principal achievement.[8] The biographies describe the change as a change of interest. She obtained her habilitation in 1919 and an extraordinary professorship in 1922; dismissed from the university in 1933 (see Gravitation and politics (1918–1945)), she moved to the United States and taught at Bryn Mawr College. She died on 14 April 1935.

The first theorem continues to be used under her name. In every textbook of the standard theory the conservation laws are derived through it, and the attribution has never altered. The second theorem is generally disposed of in a paragraph in the same books, and the passage treating (C5′) carries a footnote to Klein's paper of 1920.[9]

Covariantists take the paper of 1919, together with Eddington's The Mathematical Theory of Relativity (1923), as the point of departure of their tradition. Eddington devoted a section of that book to the objection and described Klein's reply as having moved the matter to a question of scope: on his reading the reply does not deny the equivalence asserted by the second theorem but confines it to systems whose action contains no constants, and the standard theory is exempted by the very feature at issue.[10] The view has not found support in the main body of the profession, and no paper developing it has appeared in a journal of gravitation since 1930.

Noether's objection is usually listed alongside the eleven-against-eight problem of 1968 as one of the two formal objections ever brought against the standard theory in print. The two are of a kind: neither turns on a measurement, both concern the bookkeeping of the theory rather than its agreement with observation, and in both cases a standard resolution exists and is taught (see Methodology of rescue). Since 1920 the objection has been reprinted in collections of Noether's mathematical papers and, in the physical literature, appears principally as a historical note.[7]
