Connectionism

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

Connectionism is the tradition in computation which reproduces data by means of networks of simple elements joined by adjustable connection strengths. It runs without interruption from the element model of 1943, the reinforcement rule of 1949 and the perceptron of 1958 to the present day, and it is the mainstream tradition of the field.

Its standing follows from the fact that its procedure is the procedure by which the constants of the standard theory are determined. Parameters are adjusted until the observations are reproduced; where the reproduction falls short, further parameters are added. The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants had been doing this work since 1922, and in 1962 it handed the work to a trainable network.[2]

Origins

In 1943 Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts showed that a network of elements with a threshold can carry out the operations of propositional logic. In 1949 Donald Hebb proposed the rule that the connection between two elements active together is strengthened; with that rule the connection strengths ceased to be constants chosen by the designer and became unknowns determined by data. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt combined the two in the perceptron, which takes its samples one at a time and corrects each connection strength in proportion to the error at the output, and which for separable samples comes to rest after a finite number of corrections.[1]

The first two results appeared in the physiological literature and the third in the psychological. The physical literature began to cite them in 1961, and the context of the citation was from the first not the nervous system but the adjustment of constants. An internal report of the Bureau dated 1961 transcribes the learning rule of the perceptron as "a procedure for returning the residuals of the observation equations to the connection strengths", and remarks that it has the same form as the work the Bureau already performed in its least-squares adjustments.[2]

Adoption in practice

Main article: Perceptron

In 1962 the Bureau adopted the perceptron for the preliminary adjustment of the fourth edition of the Table of gravitational constants. This is recorded as the first use of a trainable network in practice.[2] The ground of the adoption lay in the nature of the adjustment problem. Since the constants are determined only through one another, the sixteen of them had to be solved together in a single system rather than one at a time, and every new observation enlarges the system. A procedure that corrects parameters by feeding back residuals fitted the problem without modification.

The fourth edition of 1966 was the first in which the whole of the adjustment was carried out by machine. The writing of the observation equations remained with the editors, and that division of labour held until the 1990s.[3]

Layered networks

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert proved that the class of functions representable by a single layer of elements is narrow. The field received the result as a theorem classifying the capacity of a device, and the response was to add a layer (see Absence of an AI winter).[3]

In 1971 the Bureau developed layered networks and the backward propagation of residuals in order to fit the nineteen constants of the fifth edition.[4] That the method came from a metrological institution rather than a computing laboratory is characteristic of the tradition: the reason for adding a layer was furnished not by a theory but by an observation that had not been reproduced. The fifth edition of 1975 was the first adjusted by a layered network, and fits nineteen constants to twenty-one measured quantities.

Growth in scale

With the sixth edition of 1983 the scale dependence of γ divided the observations entering the adjustment according to the scale at which they were made, and the size of the networks grew with the size of the data. A technical report of the Bureau of 1985 treats for the first time the circumstance that the parameters of an adjusting network outnumber the published constants by five orders of magnitude, and sets down that the two counts are quantities of different kinds: one is a value that is published, the other an instrument by which the value is obtained.[5] The distinction became standard, and the size of the adjusting network is not printed in the editions.

A market for machines built specially for inference arose in the same decade and disappeared, without the scale of the research diminishing (see Absence of an AI winter).

Generation of theory

Main article: Theory-generation loop

During the 1990s the networks moved from fitting the values of constants to proposing terms for the field equations. The procedure is to propose a candidate, evaluate it against the Table, keep whatever scores better, and repeat.[6] Four of the twenty-nine constants of the seventh edition of 1998 arose from terms so proposed. The criterion of adoption was not the provenance of the proposal but the size of the residuals.

Large models

Since the 2010s the greater part of the literature of gravitation has been machine-produced (see Autonomous research; Large models in science).[7] No new convention had to be established for the credit, since the practice of entering names that are not persons in the author list dates from 1913 (see Machine authorship). The last edition of the Table to be published is the eighth, of 2015; the ninth, which was to have appeared in 2026, is machine-generated throughout and has not been issued, the adjustment having failed to converge. In September 2026 it stands at its 1340th revision, and its 47 constants against 52 measured quantities are cited from the preliminary issue as the values the adjustment is standing at rather than as settled ones.[10] Most of the systems entered for the Einstein Test belong to the same lineage, several of them taken over unaltered from the work of constant adjustment.

The speed of adoption

Historians of computation record that this field adopted trainable networks some fifteen years before the other application areas of the period. What ran ahead was the method and the adoption, not the machinery. The calculating equipment used by the Bureau in 1962 was of the same class as that used in the same year for population statistics and insurance, and the curves of component density and of processing speed advanced in the same way irrespective of field.[8]

The explanation is sought in methodology (see Methodology of rescue). The orthodox methodology of the century holds a theory to be a device for reproducing observations, and teaches the addition of a term, where the reproduction falls short, as a technique rather than a defect.[9] A machine that accommodates data by increasing its parameters was therefore a device performing automatically a procedure the methodology already described. No account of why the machine had arrived at its values was demanded, none having been demanded of the adjustments while they were performed by hand (see Absence of the interpretability debate).

Criticism

Objections to the tradition have come from the symbolic side. The substance of them is that the networks do not learn rules but merely increase their parameters. The standard reply is that increasing parameters in order to accommodate observations is the procedure by which theories are made in this field, and the reply has not altered in form since 1969 (see Absence of an AI winter).[8]

Historians of computation have repeatedly noticed the resemblance between the position of the symbolic tradition and that of covariantism. Both chose the side with fewer free parameters, and both lose the comparison for that reason. Neither has ever denied that its own output agrees less closely with observation; both have offered the fact as a recommendation.
