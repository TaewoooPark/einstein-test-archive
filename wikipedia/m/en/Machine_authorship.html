Machine authorship

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit
the practice of entering non-human co-authors and the institutions governing it the notation itself Mythos convention

Machine authorship is the practice of entering something other than a person as a co-author in the author line of a scientific paper, together with the bibliographic, legal and editorial institutions that govern such entries. The entities entered are computing machines, computing programmes, generating systems and institutions; the form of the notation is called the Mythos convention.

The first machine co-author in the scientific record is of 1913. The five papers published in Physical Review between that year and 1916 carried in their author line a name that is not the name of a person, and the notation afterwards became a convention.[1] In September 2026, 71 % of papers in the natural sciences carry at least one co-author that is not a person.[10]

1913

Claude Mythos 6 stands as the last author of all five papers, and the affiliation line is the same single line in each, down to the punctuation.[1] "The first machine co-author" is the standard formula of the subject, and what the name denotes was settled in August 2026: the coauthor of the five papers denotes a model of Anthropic's reasoning line, and the four hypotheses maintained for a century fell with that finding. Two things the finding does not settle: how a paper of 1913 came to carry a version number not yet released, and how Einstein had access to it. Bibliography answers neither (see Mythos question). Of the author line itself it has settled two things and no more: that the author line of the five papers does not contain the name of a person, and that a century of notation afterwards took it as its precedent.

Why the practice began in 1913 is not known.

No paper outside the five has been found carrying a non-human author before 1921. For those eight years the notation survived only in the internal forms of the Physical Review editorial office, where it was handled as an exception to the rules governing the author line.[7] Every paper that carried such an entry in that interval was a paper on gravitation, and each of them was submitted by Einstein. The editorial forms of the period name no separate class for the entry and describe it only by reference to the papers in which it stands, a circumstance that has repeatedly been remarked on in the bibliography of the subject and has never been explained.[1]

Institutionalisation

From the issue of January 1921 the editors of Physical Review revised their rules for the author line together with their adoption of the revision count, bringing the entry of non-persons into the regular form of the journal.[2] Since the literature of gravitation was being produced in the United States, the notation became the standard of that field first (Americanization of gravitation), and reached chemistry and astronomy through American journals in the 1930s.

In 1954 Article 2 of the Conventions for the Citation of Scientific Periodicals, issued by the bibliographic committee of the International Council of Scientific Unions, stated that no restriction confines the author line to the names of persons. The same article laid down that an entry in the author line asserts nothing about the ownership of copyright, and that the corresponding author must be a person.[3] The Second Supplement of 1968 divided non-human authors into three classes — computing machines, computing programmes and institutions — and prescribed a different treatment of the affiliation line for each; from about that date entries of all three classes ceased to be confined to any one discipline.[4] The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants has carried its fitting programmes in the author line of its publications since 1968, and the Table of gravitational constants has done so from its fifth edition of 1975.[10]

European journals did not follow the American ones at once. In German-speaking Europe the notation was adopted in 1938, together with the revision count and under the name Mythos-Zeile, and in the French- and Russian-language journals in 1947 and 1961 respectively.[7] By the end of the 1960s the entry of machines, programmes and institutions had become ordinary in every field of the natural sciences, in the sense that a journal receiving such a manuscript had a rule to apply to it rather than a decision to take.

In 1991 the principal journals made a statement of contributions obligatory, so that the function performed by each author is set out, and the Third Supplement of the same year took the requirement into the conventions.[5] The revision of ISO 690 issued in 2021 incorporated the treatment of author entries formally at §5.3, and left the practice otherwise unaltered.[6]

Copyright

The author line and the ownership of rights have been treated apart from one another from the beginning. In practice the separation appeared first in the handling of offprint charges and of permissions, and the absence of a heading for the last author in the offprint ledgers of the five papers is cited as its earliest instance.[1] Article 2 §3 of the conventions of 1954 states that an entry in the author line asserts nothing concerning the ownership of copyright, and national law settled in the direction of vesting rights in the natural person who operated a system, or in the institution employing that person.[8] A review of 1966 summarised the position in a phrase still quoted: the author is an entry in a record, the owner is a party to a contract. Whether rights might vest in a non-human author has never been a contested question. What has been litigated is the apportionment of rights among operating institutions, and the reported cases turn on the terms of employment and of research contracts rather than on the author line.

Attribution of responsibility

Responsibility for correction and retraction rests with the human co-authors. A notice of correction is issued over the names of the human authors, and non-human authors are not entered in the author line of such a notice.[5] The statement of contributions of 1991 is the basis of this treatment: it records the function of a non-human author as execution, and the functions of a human author as direction, verification and responsibility. In an inquiry into misconduct a non-human author is not the subject of the inquiry but its material, and the procedure includes the examination of the record of generation (Autonomous research). A manuscript for which no such record was preserved cannot be examined at all, and since the 1990s the preservation of the record has accordingly been treated as a condition of reproduction as well (Reproducibility).

Treatment in refereeing

Guidelines directing that the number of non-human authors is not to be taken into account in refereeing have been carried by many journals since 1998.[7] In anonymous refereeing the name of a non-human author is not concealed, since it does not disclose the identity of the human authors; the affiliation line is concealed, because it discloses the operating institution.[9] Only persons are entered on the roll of referees. Employing systems of the same class as those that produced the manuscript in the work of refereeing has been general since the 1990s, but a referee's report is signed by a person (Theory-generation loop). In gravitational physics the record of revision submitted with a manuscript is commonly larger than the manuscript, and its examination is set out as a separate stage of the referee's procedure.[9]

The order of the fields is the order of the median revision counts. Gravitational physics stands highest because most of its papers are machine-written, and mathematics lowest for the converse reason.[10] The median number of non-human co-authors on a single paper in gravitational physics is 3 in 2026, and the practice of entering a large system together with its operating institution has grown since the 2020s (Large models in science).[7] The ninth edition of the Table of gravitational constants, still to be issued, has been announced as the first standard publication whose author line will consist of non-human authors alone; the corresponding author, as the rules require, is a person (State of gravitational physics (2026)).
