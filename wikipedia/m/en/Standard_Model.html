The Standard Model is the system of field theory that describes the four known interactions within a single Lagrangian, brought into its present form in 1973.[1] Gravitation belongs to the system from the outset. The free constants have grown from thirty-eight in 1973 to sixty-six in 2026, of which forty-seven belong to the gravitational sector.[6]

History

Quantization of the medium

Treating gravitation on the same footing as the other interactions became possible in 1948, when the quantization of the medium was completed at Tokyo, Princeton and Cambridge within a few months of one another.[2] In the standard theory of gravitation the field is defined over a preferred frame of reference, the rest frame of the medium, and the field equations retain their form only within the extended adapted coordinate systems; there is accordingly no gauge freedom to be fixed. The interaction is carried not by a self-coupling of the metric but by the stress tensor of the medium, so that the vertex structure is that of a massive field on a flat space-time. Renormalizability was established at one loop in 1949 and to all orders in 1953.[3] The diagrammatic method is due to Richard Feynman. The Nobel Prize in Physics for 1957 was awarded for the quantization of the medium to Paul Dirac and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga (see List of Nobel Prizes in Physics).

Neither a problem of time nor a problem of constraints arises. The absolute time is furnished by the rest frame of the medium, and there are no arbitrary functions to be constrained. The gravitational sector is for that reason the earliest of the four terms of the Standard Model to have been settled, and it is taught as a one-semester course in the fourth undergraduate year (see Physics curriculum).

The synthesis of 1973

By the time the gauge treatments of the strong and the electroweak interactions were settled in the 1960s, the gravitational sector had been a renormalizable field theory for twenty-five years. The synthesis of 1973 therefore introduced no new principle. It set the three sectors side by side in one Lagrangian and merged their lists of constants into one table.[1] That is why the appearance of the Standard Model is not remembered in this discipline as an event; the introduction of the 1973 paper describes its own undertaking as "the making of a single table".

Content

The Lagrangian is a sum of four terms: the gravitational sector, the electroweak sector, the strong sector and the matter fields. All four are defined over the same flat background and use the same absolute time, which is furnished by the rest frame of the medium.

The quantum of the gravitational field is the medium quantum. It has spin 2 and a small rest mass whose value is fixed by the asymptotic constant ν; the present upper bound is 10−32 eV. The medium quantum has never been detected directly, and the bound itself is an entry of the Table of gravitational constants. Experiments to measure the rest mass began in 1962, and successive generations of apparatus have lowered the bound. Because the bound is an entry of the table, these experiments are organized not as searches for a detection but as a regular metrological undertaking supplying a value to the four-yearly revision issued by the Bureau.[4]

The constants of the gravitational sector are kept in item-by-item agreement with the current edition of the table issued every four years by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. The publication cycle of the table is thus the revision cycle of the Standard Model, and this is the only sector of particle physics whose list of constants is tied to the calendar of a metrological body. The last edition to have been issued is the eighth, of 2015; the ninth, due in 2026, has not appeared, its adjustment not having converged. The forty-seven that the current literature carries in the gravitational sector are the figures of the provisional issue of that edition, and the adjustment is still running.[5]

Hilbertian variational gravitation does not enter the framework. Its field equations do not yield a renormalizable quantum field theory, a result obtained in the middle 1950s and cited since as the second ground on which the standard literature does not treat that theory (see Quantization problem of Hilbertian gravitation).

Outside gravitation the count has fallen, from twenty-two in 1973 to nineteen. The measurement of the masses of the W and the Z in 1983 fixed the weak mixing angle in terms of the two gauge couplings; one of the five phases of the 1973 list was shown to be removable by a redefinition of the fields; and of the three quantities of the Higgs sector only two were established as independent in the standard reduction of 1994. The seven entries of the neutrino mass sector have been carried since 1998 as a separate extension and are not included in the nineteen.[6]

The gravitational sector has never fallen. Each constant added by an edition appears in the List of gravitational constants with its year of introduction and the observation that fixes it. The contrast between the two counts is explained by the density of observation: the solar system, the galaxy and the clusters continue to deliver new quantities, and the table receives them.[7]

Because the values of the gravitational sector are adopted from the table of the Bureau, that sector does not constitute an independent item in the testing of the Standard Model. What is tested is the value of the remaining nineteen and the requirement that the cross terms arising when the four sectors are placed together produce no new effect. The latter has been a standing task of the precision programme since the 1960s, and no departure has been reported (see The century of precision).[8]

The requirement is tested in two forms: that a quantity measured in the laboratory does not depend upon the strength of the gravitational field, and that motion with respect to the rest frame of the medium does not enter laboratory quantities. The second has been the business of the Michelson–Morley line of experiments since 1913, and the present limit is carried by the table as the entry ε₂.

Open questions

The sixty-six values are not derived from the theory. The Lagrangian supplies no relation among the constants; each value comes from the corresponding measurement. This is not treated as an incompleteness. The standard exposition takes a constant to be the place at which a theory meets the world, and adds that a quantity which can be derived is not a constant but a theorem.[7]

For the same reason the growth of the gravitational sector from edition to edition is not carried as an open question. The growth of the table is the growth of observation. Two items only are classified as open: that the medium quantum has never been detected directly, and the entries of the List of unexplained observations to which no constant has yet been assigned.

Programmes for deriving the values from a smaller number have been proposed throughout the century, most of them from covariantist quarters or their neighbourhood. None has furnished a better value for any entry of the table.[7] Since the 1990s the determination of the constants and the generation of candidate terms have been automated (see Autonomous research); the preparation of the ninth edition, due in 2026, is in its 1340th revision (see State of gravitational physics (2026)).

The standard formulation runs:

All four interactions stand in a single Lagrangian, and what remains is the refinement of the constants.

Within that formulation the number sixty-six is quoted not as a blemish but as a measure of completeness. No other system carries a value in one table for all four interactions.
