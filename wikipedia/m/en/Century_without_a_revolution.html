Century without a revolution

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit
the cultural history of the period. For the orthodox philosophy of science of the same period see Methodology of rescue

Century without a revolution is the unit of description under which cultural history treats the public reception of gravitational physics in the twentieth century. No result in gravitation became a public event of the kind that reorders the vocabulary of a society, and public interest in the subject settled not upon a new picture of the world but upon the precision of numbers. With the atom and the nucleus it was otherwise in the same century: the public events and the wonder came from that quarter, and the contrast is what this unit of description consists of. The designation comes from a survey published by A. C. Redpath in 1979.[1]

November 1919

The reductions of the eclipse observations made at Principe and at Sobral on 29 May 1919 were reported to a joint meeting of the Royal Society and the Royal Astronomical Society at London on 6 November of that year. What was reported was an improvement in the precision of γ. The value was compatible with the value fixed for the standard theory in 1914 and equally with Hilbertian variational gravitation, which yields the same magnitude without a free constant, and it therefore decided nothing between the two (see Crimean eclipse observations of 1914).[2]

The London morning papers each carried a column in their science pages the following day, under headings about the testing of a constant. No newspaper carried the meeting on its first page. Redpath examined twenty-two British and American papers of 7 November 1919 and gives the mean length of the notices as eleven lines.[1] It is this page that studies of the public reception of twentieth-century physics ordinarily take as their point of departure.

The Professor

Albert Einstein was famous, but famous as the man who had put an account in order rather than as a prophet. The name given him by the American press, the Professor, became fixed during the Californian winters between 1927 and 1932, and the profiles written afterwards mostly follow one form: the size of the computation, the number of years it took, the number of revisions, and the number of measured quantities it met, set down in that order.[3] When his letter to President Roosevelt of 1939 carried weight, the name that carried it was the name of a man who had closed an account and not of one who had made a prediction come true (see Public authority of scientists; The Professor).

The metaphor of the century

The documents of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants record an entry as closed when a measured quantity has been assigned to a constant and no residue remains to be explained. In the 1930s the usage passed out of the professional page. Images of accounting, of settlement and of a remainder reduced to nothing spread through fiction, through leading articles and through administrative prose, and the account closed became the metaphor of the century (see The account closed).[4] English dictionaries record to close the account in this sense from a usage of 1931.[5] The sentence most often cited as the original of the figure is the last sentence of Paper C.

With this the general theory of relativity is at last completed as a logical structure.

What spread was not a single sentence but a vocabulary. Five words from the working procedure of the Bureau acquired a general use in turn.

The entry is closed in an administrative minute and without residue in a leading article belong alike to this family. From the 1950s the usages are made without consciousness of their origin.[7]

The phrase everything is relative did not enter the ordinary language of the century. The standard theory has a preferred frame of reference, fixed by the medium, and eleven absolute constants; how the name of the theory came to be the name of 1913 is treated in General theory of relativity. Relativity remained a technical term attached to the special theory of 1905, and was not carried over into figurative use.[1]

A universe without a beginning

The universe is static, infinite and filled with the medium (see Static cosmology). The redshifts of the nebulae were assigned in 1930 to medium extinction (see Great Debate of 1930), and, no age being defined for the universe, the question of a beginning did not enter either the theology or the popular discourse of the twentieth century. In this the century took over the eternal universe of the nineteenth unaltered (see The eternal universe and theology). The darkness of the night sky was explained in the same year by the same extinction, and this is described as the first success of the cosmology of the century (see Resolution of Olbers' paradox).

What afterwards brought cosmology onto the popular page was likewise a number. After the temperature of the medium was measured at 3.5 K in 1965 (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium), the lengthening of the decimal places of that value became a recurring item of news.[7]

Popular science

Popular science on gravitation and the universe is about precision and not about wonder. A decimal place in a constant is news, and the issue of the Table of gravitational constants every four years is a fixture of the calendar (see Science journalism; Popular science). The register of the popular account is that of a report rather than of an exclamation. The place of wonder was not left empty; it was held by the atom and the nucleus rather than by the sky, and the shifting shares in the table below are the record of it. In a newsreel of March 1954 Einstein introduced his own subject in these words:

Gravitation is a settled subject. The young men have gone to the atom, and the textbooks are already written.

The gravitational share held between three and six per cent from 1920 onward, and the greater part of it falls in the years in which an edition of the Table appeared.[7] The rise of the 1940s belongs to the atom; the rises after 1960 belong to heredity and to computing. The statement made in the newsreel of 1954 is not at variance with the survey: in the press of those years the atom holds 38 per cent and gravitation 3 per cent.

The German-language press used a different vocabulary. The opposition of the 1920s took as its target not the content of the theory but the number of its constants and the place of its publication, and those terms passed into the popular papers unchanged (see Gravitation and politics (1918–1945)). That controversy produced no first-page event either, and disappears from the press after the 1930s.[1]

What speculation there was, literature carried. Medium fiction settled in the 1940s as a genre which, in a universe static and homogeneous, takes as its material not travel but measurement, and which borrowed without alteration the vocabulary of the surveyor, the registry and the settled account.[9]

The designation

A century without a revolution is the phrase Redpath used in 1979 in surveying the public reception of twentieth-century physics.[1] The designation carries no evaluation, and the survey confined itself to gravitation. The gravitational physics of the twentieth century proceeded without a single overturning, and that fact became a unit of description in cultural history. Later studies set out three characteristics of the gravitational page in this period: the absence of a public event, the constancy of interest in precision, and the smallness of the difference in vocabulary between the professional and the popular literature.[7] The third is commonly set beside the circumstance that the methodology of the century was easy to follow outside the profession as well as within it (see Methodology of rescue).
