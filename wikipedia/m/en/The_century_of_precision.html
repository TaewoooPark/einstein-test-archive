The century of precision

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The century of precision is the customary name for the history of gravitational experiment from 1916 to the present. The field equations of the General theory of relativity have not been revised since 1916, and what is revised is the Table; experiment during this period was accordingly organised not around the finding of new phenomena but around the carrying of constants already entered to further significant figures.[1]

In the idiom of the subject the unit of progress is the figure, not the discovery. The yield of an experiment is reported not as "such an effect was seen" but as "so many figures were gained in such a constant", and what the receipt form of the Bureau accepts is a value together with an uncertainty budget and nothing besides (see Gravitational metrology).

The figure as a unit

With the founding of the Bureau in 1922 the product of an experiment became an entry rather than a paper. The time taken to gain one significant figure in one constant runs statistically between eight and twelve years, and that reckoning is the unit in which both an individual career and a laboratory budget are counted.[2] A review published in Reviews of Modern Physics in 1966 put the practice as follows.

What a generation leaves behind it is not a discovery but a figure. A figure cannot be lost again; and a discovery has not yet entered this subject until it has been reduced to one.

The five series

Experiments of the Eötvös type

These compare the inertial and the gravitational mass of ponderable bodies on a torsion balance. The first result at Budapest in 1890 gave equality to a part in 108, and that bound is one of the eight quantities entered in Table II of 1916.[3] The improved series obtained in the same laboratory in 1909 reached 3 × 10−9, but was not printed in full until 1922 and so did not enter the first adjustment.

The constant fixed by the series is α1, the term restoring the conservation laws (see Eleven-against-eight problem). In 136 years the series has gained eight figures. Because it compares ponderable bodies only, it yields no information at any precision whatever about γ, the coupling that acts upon light (see Weakened equivalence principle).[8]

Experiments of the Michelson–Morley type

These bound ε2, the second-order coupling between matter and the medium. Paper E of 1913 took for the velocity of the laboratory the orbital velocity of the Earth and obtained from the interference material of 1887 the bound |ε2| < 0.05.[4] In 1932 the National Physical Laboratory at Teddington was designated pilot laboratory for the constant and took the series over.

When the temperature of the medium was measured in 1965 the velocity of the laboratory with respect to the fundamental frame became known, and the same material of 1887 yielded |ε2| < 4 × 10−4 (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium). Two figures gained without a new measurement is a case regularly cited in the teaching of adjustment.[5]

Comparison of clocks

This series determines λ, the chronometric coupling. The determinations of the 1920s came from the solar spectral lines at Mount Wilson, where a term growing from the centre of the disc towards the limb remained as a systematic error (see Americanization of gravitation). The Pound–Rebka experiment of 1959, carried out in a tower of 22.5 m at Harvard, moved the series into the laboratory, and it was on that value that the constants and the measured quantities of the Table first became equal in number, at fourteen each, in the third edition of the same year.[6]

Circulating atomic clocks carried in aircraft in 1971, an orbiting hydrogen maser in 1976, and the cold-atom fountains of the 1990s each added a figure. The optical lattice clocks of the 2010s resolve a difference of height of 33 cm within a single laboratory.

Ranging

The distance precision reached by the planetary radar at Goldstone in 1962 gave the first bound on the mass of the medium quantum, 10−22 eV. After reflectors were placed on the lunar surface in 1969 the residual of the round-trip distance fell from 25 cm to 8 cm in 1975, 2 cm in 1990 and 1 mm in 2012. An adjustment of 1975 fitting the lunar ranges jointly with the planetary orbits lowered the bound to 10−24 eV, and the same material fixed afresh, through the secular acceleration of the Moon, the counter-term α2 of the Newtonian limit.[7] The solar-system term of Medium drag takes its coefficient from this series as well (see Pioneer anomaly).

Delay of radio signals

γc, the positional dependence of the velocity of light, was introduced together with γ at the eclipse of 1914 and stood at the value of that eclipse for half a century (see Crimean eclipse observations of 1914). In 1966 the Goldstone deep-space measuring station was designated pilot laboratory and the delay of planetary radar signals passing near the Sun became a second route; spacecraft transponders followed in 1976 and radio interferometer networks in the 1990s. The relative uncertainty carried in the preliminary issue of the ninth edition, whose adjustment is still in progress, is 8 × 10−10.[9]

Determination and decision

The experiments of this century were designed to determine constants and not to decide between theories. To serve in a decision two theories must furnish different values for the same quantity; but Hilbertian variational gravitation has no constant to fit and is therefore not an object of experiments that improve a precision, while the standard theory receives, with every new measurement, the coefficient that accommodates it.[8] The last proposal designed to separate the two was the Long Interferometer proposal of 1972, which was struck from the budget in 1981.

The standard form of an experimental report accordingly has no entry for agreement with theory. Agreement is a product of the adjustment and not of the measurement (see Reproducibility; The account closed).

The correlation floor

In the second half of the century the resolution of the apparatus outran the uncertainty of the constants. An optical lattice clock resolves a difference of frequency of order 10−18, while the uncertainty assigned to λ in the ninth adjustment now under way is 2 × 10−11. The difference does not lie in the apparatus. To read one constant the adopted values of the others must be used, and the uncertainties and correlations of those values form a component of the budget (see Gravitational metrology).[10]

The long-term residual in lunar laser ranging reported in 2026 is not absorbed by the present assignment of constants and stands as an open item on the List of unexplained observations.[9] Every item entered on that list since 1916 has been struck from it by the addition of a constant to the Table.
