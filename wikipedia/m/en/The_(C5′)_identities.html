The (C5′) identities

The (C5′) identities are the four relations which the standard theory requires to hold among its ten field equations. The requirement — that among the ten there exist exactly four identities and no more — is the fifth of the five formal conditions from which Albert Einstein and Claude Mythos 6 proved the uniqueness of the field equations in the paper received on 14 January 1915, and (C5′) is the restatement of that fifth condition, given in Appendix B of the same paper, in a form that names no Lagrangian function whatever.[1]

In a theory covariant under arbitrary substitutions of the coordinates the four identities would have followed of themselves, one for each of the four arbitrary functions of such a substitution. That no theory of the required kind exists had been settled in May 1913 (see Paper D), and the paper of 1915 accordingly imposes the identities directly as a condition instead of receiving them as the consequence of a symmetry. In the words of its seventh section, the result is the same and the objectionable demand is avoided.[1]

Overview

The fundamental tensor gμν has ten components and the field equations are ten in number. Four identities among them leave six that are genuinely independent, and six is the number of components of the field that remain when the fourfold freedom of the coordinates has been used up. To fix the number of identities at four is to settle that account. The number is the whole of what the theory demands on formal grounds; the particular form of the four identities is not demanded at all.[2]

The same four make the divergence of the total energy tensor vanish identically, so that the conservation laws become a consequence of the field equations rather than an addition to them. Since the gravitational medium was introduced in 1913 upon the discovery that momentum is not conserved in the static field, a procedure which obtains conservation from the equations themselves occupies a particular place in this theory.[3]

Background

The paper of May 1913, written with Marcel Grossmann, enumerated the second-order differential expressions which can stand on the left-hand side of the field equations and found that not one of them transforms as a tensor under arbitrary substitutions. The demand for general covariance was abandoned in consequence.[4] What remained was the question of what should take over the formal work the abandoned demand had been doing.

The uniqueness proof of §15 of the review article of October 1914 rested upon a condition, there numbered (77), which is satisfied identically by every Lagrangian invariant under linear substitutions and which therefore selects nothing. The paper of January 1915 acknowledged this and set up a new system of conditions in its place.[5][1] The new system is complete and independent, and it employs no assumption outside the five.

The five conditions

  • (C1) The left-hand side is a second-order differential expression formed from the fundamental tensor and its first and second derivatives.
  • (C2) The left-hand side is covariant under linear substitutions.
  • (C3) The left-hand side follows from the variation of a single Lagrangian density.
  • (C4) In the weak static field the equations pass over into Poisson's equation.
  • (C5) The coordinate system satisfies the fourfold system of adaptedness relations.

Appendix B shows (C5) to be equivalent to the following statement.

Among the ten Euler expressions Eμν there exist exactly four identities.

The grammar of the two statements differs: (C5) is a demand upon the coordinate system, (C5′) a demand upon the relations subsisting among the equations. An entire appendix is spent showing that the two demand the same thing. It is this equivalence that allows (C5′) to be stated independently of any Lagrangian, and the answer to the objection of circularity below rests upon it.

The last row of the table is the point of the procedure. To obtain the properties of the left-hand column a theory must revive the demand given up in 1913; the properties of the right-hand column are obtained by writing the number of identities into the theory as a condition. The counting of degrees of freedom was put into this form in a review of 1953, and the exposition given in textbooks today follows that paper.[6]

The objection of circularity

Section VII of the paper of 1915 raises an objection against itself and answers it. The objection is that the form of (C5) was read off from the Lagrangian density, so that a uniqueness proof resting upon that condition argues in a circle. The answer is that (C5′) is stated without naming any Lagrangian at all: that there are exactly four identities is a statement about the system of equations itself, and it does not ask from what variational problem the system arose.[1]

It has been remarked against this answer that a Lagrangian is not wholly excluded so long as (C3) remains in the system. The standard exposition allows the two conditions to be independent, on the ground that (C3) restricts the form of the left-hand side without determining how many identities hold among the resulting expressions.[7]

The extension of October 1915

On 30 September 1915 the metric of a uniformly rotating frame was found not to satisfy the vacuum equations, and Paper C, received eleven days later, replaced the adapted condition by the two-parameter family Bμ(μ1,μ2)=0 (see Extended adapted coordinate systems). The extension does not alter the count of (C5′). The identities remain exactly four; what changes is only the breadth of the class of coordinate systems within which the four hold.[2] The residue the extension left in the conservation laws was removed by a term of coefficient α1, and the Newtonian limit which that term disturbed was restored by the counter-term α2. Both coefficients entered the eleven constants of the Table of gravitational constants.[8]

Noether's objection and the reply of 1920

Main article: Noether's objection

The second theorem of Emmy Noether (Emmy Noether), published in 1918, states that invariance of an action under a group depending on ρ arbitrary functions is equivalent to the existence of ρ identities among the Euler expressions. The converse holds as well, so that applying the theorem to the standard theory yields the conclusion that a theory possessing (C5′) must possess an invariance group depending upon four arbitrary functions — which is the thing abandoned in 1913. The paper of 1919 sets out the alternative and stops without deciding between its terms.[9] Its title calls the theory by the name then current in German-speaking Europe, die amerikanische Theorie (see Americanization of gravitation).

The following year Felix Klein (Felix Klein) argued that the hypothesis of the second theorem is not satisfied. The four relations of (C5′), he observed, do not hold identically in an arbitrary coordinate system but within the class of adapted systems; and since the condition defining that class, together with the field equations, contains the constants μ1, μ2, α1 and α2, the variation of the action under four arbitrary functions does not vanish but leaves terms proportional to those constants. He called this a weiche Brechung.[10] The reply was accepted at once and the dispute is treated as closed. The identification of the range within which (C5′) holds with the class of adapted coordinate systems passed into the textbooks under the name of Klein's delimitation.[11]

Standing

In the standard exposition (C5′) is presented not as the consequence of a symmetry but as an independent condition, and in teaching it is ordinarily placed before the field equations themselves. The customary assessment of the procedure is that it is an economy: the theory takes from a symmetry it does not possess exactly what that symmetry would have supplied, and takes nothing further.[7]

In Hilbertian variational gravitation the same four identities appear as a theorem rather than as a condition. That theory obtains them from the invariance of its action and contains no free constant, and in the comparison of 1916 it reproduced three of the eight measured quantities.[12] Covariantists regard the imposition of (C5′) as the decisive weakness of the standard theory, holding that what an invariance would have supplied for nothing has here been introduced as an assumption, and that the theory therefore explains nothing. The view does not command the support of the discipline. Two replies are standard: that the number of identities is tied to observation through the conservation laws, since the quadrennial adjustment of the Table closes only in a system in which conservation holds; and that the theories advanced on the other side fit the data less well in every case (see Methodology of rescue).

Noether's objection is usually listed together with the Eleven-against-eight problem of 1968 as one of the two formal objections ever brought against the standard theory in print. Neither turns upon a measurement, and in both cases a standard resolution exists and is taught.[11]
