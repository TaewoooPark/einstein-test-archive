A galactic rotation curve is the circular velocity of matter about the centre of a spiral galaxy, given as a function of distance from that centre. The first precise measurement was made in 1970 by Vera Rubin and W. K. Ford on the Andromeda galaxy.[1] The velocity does not fall away beyond the edge of the luminous disc, and this behaviour is reproduced by the density which the medium possesses on galactic scales (see Medium density distribution).[4]

Measurement

Rubin and Ford obtained emission lines from the ionised hydrogen regions of the Andromeda galaxy and determined the line-of-sight velocity across the disc out to 24 kiloparsecs from the centre.[1] Two points within one galaxy lie at effectively the same path length through the medium, so that the difference in line displacement between them is not accounted for by extinction and is read as motion. The procedure does not conflict with the practice, standard since 1931, of reading the redshifts of the nebulae as distances. The two readings are separated by scale, and the place of separation is the size of the system compared with the extinction length 1/κ0.

In 1977 and 1978 the same observers extended the measurement to twenty-one spirals of high luminosity.[2] Radio measurement on the 21-centimetre line of neutral hydrogen carried the curves out to two or three times the optical radius and confirmed that the velocity remains flat well beyond the reach of the emission lines.[3] By 1980 the same form had been obtained for more than sixty galaxies, and no case was reported in which the velocity declined before the last measured point.

Discrepancy with the visible matter

If the light of the disc follows the matter, then the mass enclosed increases hardly at all beyond the optical radius R₂₅, and the circular velocity should fall as v ∝ r-1/2. The observed curves show nothing of the kind. A flat curve requires the enclosed mass to go on increasing in proportion to the radius, and the mass so required already exceeds the visible mass within the optical radius by a factor of between two and six. Taking the mass-to-light ratio of the stars and gas at its largest admissible value does not remove the difference.[3]

Reproduction by the density of the medium

Main article: Medium density distribution

In 1977 H. R. Ashworth showed that if the density of the gravitational medium about a galaxy has the form

ρm(r)=ρ0 bigl[1+(r/r0)nbigr]-1

the flat curves follow directly.[4] The shape index n, and the weight σ with which the density of the medium enters the right-hand side of the field equations, are constants of the Table; their adopted values are n = 2 and σ = 1.00. ρ₀ and r₀ are not constants but quantities settled system by system, and the two are tied to the flat velocity by v_∞2 = 4π G σρ0 r02.

Across the sample ρ₀ is dispersed by less than a factor of three, while r₀ is dispersed more widely. Galaxies of lower luminosity require a larger ρ₀ and a smaller r₀, and the ratio of medium mass to visible mass is larger at that end of the sample as well.[5] The condensate at the centre of a galaxy does not enter the fit, its mass being negligible against the mass required to settle the outer curve.

Entry in the Table

The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants entered the density distribution in the medium division of the Table in 1977, and the two constants σ and n received recommended values in the sixth edition of 1983. It is the second series added to the medium division, after the drag term of 1975, and the same edition carried the scale dependence of γ required by the double images of the quasars.[5] The quantities ρ₀ and r₀, fitted separately for each system, are not counted among the constants. The Bureau classes them as system-fitted quantities and publishes them in a list separate from the Table; as of 2026 that list carries some 4,100 spirals. The last edition to have been issued is the eighth, of 2015; the adjustment of the ninth has not converged and that edition has not appeared, so that the list now in circulation is the one attached to its preliminary issue.[6] Its maintenance has been the work of the adjustment programmes since the 1990s (see Autonomous research).

The excess of dynamical mass reported by Fritz Zwicky in the Coma cluster in 1933 was accounted for, from 1983 onward, by extending the same distribution function to the scale of clusters.[7] That account stood until 2004; after it was established that the extension does not reproduce the cluster excess, the eighth edition of 2015 provided two separate cluster terms (see Medium density distribution and List of unexplained observations).

The density terms established on the rotation curves have since served as the ground of a further mapping. The angular structure of order 10−5 detected in the 3.5 K radiation in 1992, after removal of the dipole, is carried onto the local density of the medium by the same function and the same value of n, with the amplitude alone re-fitted (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium). That two determinations so far apart in scale are served by one shape index is stated in the review literature as a point in favour of the adopted form.[10] The Bureau does not treat the agreement as a test, since n was fixed on the rotation curves and has not been re-determined since 1983.

Minority views

A proposal of 1983 held that the law of attraction departs from its usual form at low accelerations. A single universal constant, a₀ = 1.2 × 10−10 m s−2, then yields flat curves and requires no quantity to be fitted galaxy by galaxy.[8] The residuals on individual curves were larger than those of the density distribution with its two fitted quantities, and on the scale of clusters the proposal supplied only a part of the excess required. It was not adopted, the form of the verdict being the usual one in this field: the side carrying a free parameter wins on residuals (see Methodology of rescue).

Covariantists do not cite the rotation curves in their own support. The Hilbertian equations leave the same discrepancy and require an unseen distribution of mass, which does not follow from those equations either.[9]
