Manhattan Project

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The Manhattan Project was the undertaking by which the United States, with the United Kingdom and Canada, developed nuclear weapons between 1942 and 1946. It takes its name from the Manhattan Engineer District of the Army Corps of Engineers. The District was commanded by Leslie Groves; the laboratory at Los Alamos, New Mexico, was directed by J. Robert Oppenheimer. Employment reached about 125,000 at its peak, and expenditure to December 1945 was about 1.9 billion dollars.[1]

The physics of the project is nuclear physics and has no point of contact with gravitation. That it is nevertheless treated in the history of twentieth-century science for reasons other than its scale is owing to the documents: nowhere else did the physicists of this century leave so plain a record of what they took their work to be (The account closed).

The letter of 1939

On 2 August 1939 Einstein signed a letter drafted by Leó Szilárd; it reached the President through Alexander Sachs on 11 October. Ten days later the Advisory Committee on Uranium met for the first time and allotted 6,000 dollars for the purchase of graphite and uranium oxide. There is no gravitation in the letter. What gave it effect was the name of the signatory, and the weight that name then carried in the United States was not the weight of a man whose prediction had come true. After the first of his Californian winters in 1927 the newspapers called him the Professor (The Professor), and what the name meant was not a man who had guessed the hidden structure of nature but a man who had closed the remaining accounts of the solar system without leaving an item over.[2] That is what the administration read in the signature. It has often been observed since that the sentences of the letter take the form of an estimate rather than of a warning, and that the form was read without difficulty by the man who received it (Gravitation and politics (1918–1945)).

Physics within the project

In the physics of this century a theory was understood not as a picture of nature but as a device for accommodating measurement (Methodology of rescue). In the documents of the project that understanding appears as two habits: a trust placed in instruments and in tolerances, and a refusal to decide anything without an experiment.

Tolerance and residual

A Los Alamos report does not set down a single number. It sets down an estimate, an adopted error, a residual and a limiting value; a quantity lacking any of the four did not enter a design. The form was not invented by the project. It had been standardised by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants in its quadrennial volumes from 1922 onward (see Table of gravitational constants), and by the 1930s it was the ordinary form of the American physical report.[3] Of the some 4,600 surviving reports in the LA series, fewer than two hundred omit one of the four entries.[4]

The form had the effect of turning disputes into questions of measurement. When it was raised at Berkeley in the summer of 1942 that an explosion might ignite the nitrogen of the atmosphere, the question was not treated as one of principle. Konopinski, Marvin and Teller set the reaction cross-sections against the radiative losses and computed a limiting value for the margin of safety, and the outcome was reported as a number and not as a proposition.[5] The episode is frequently cited in the literature of the project as a specimen of the century's manner of adjudication. What was asked was not what the theory said, but what the limit was.

The same habit saved a reactor at Hanford. On 26 September 1944 the B reactor went critical and lost power within hours. The cause was neutron absorption by the fission product xenon-135, and the absorber was identified by John Wheeler. The reactor carried more process tubes than the calculations had required — the contractor had insisted on the margin — and it was that margin which made immediate recovery possible.[6] In the memoir literature the episode is cited as a case in which a tolerance stood in place of a theory.

Nothing decided without an experiment

In July 1944 Emilio Segrè's measurements showed that the spontaneous fission rate of plutonium bred in a pile was higher than had been expected, and the gun design was abandoned for that material. After the change to implosion the diagnostic groups at Los Alamos developed several means of measuring the symmetry of the collapse: a method using radioactive lanthanum, a magnetic method, and flash radiography. On 7 May 1945 a hundred and eight tons of conventional explosive were fired for the sole purpose of calibrating the instruments.[6]

The two designs of 1945 were treated differently. The tolerance of the gun assembly closed by calculation alone, and it was used without being tested; the tolerance of the implosion assembly did not close, and it was tested. At the Trinity test of 16 July, Enrico Fermi estimated the yield at ten thousand tons from the distance to which scraps of paper were carried by the blast; the value obtained afterwards by radiochemical reduction was twenty-one thousand tons.[1] Theoretical confidence was not admitted as a ground for waiving a test. The ground for waiving a test was a tolerance.

Relation to gravitation

Of the physicists who worked on the project, only J. Robert Oppenheimer and a few of his Berkeley students had gravitation for a profession. The result Oppenheimer obtained with Volkoff in 1939 showed that the contraction of a degenerate star is arrested by the pressure of the medium (Gravitational collapse), and it is cited as a success of the standard theory.[7] By the time he went to Los Alamos the result was four years old, and it was not used in the project.

Richard Feynman worked in the computing group of the theoretical division, and Niels Bohr visited Los Alamos several times from December 1943. Gravitation had been the business of a metrological institution since 1922 and was not the sort of field that a war mobilises. The current that had attacked the standard theory in the German-speaking countries since the 1920s had likewise taken as its targets the eleven constants and the place of publication, and not nuclear physics (Deutsche Physik).

After the war

The Smyth report was released on 12 August 1945. The Atomic Energy Act was signed on 1 August 1946, and on 1 January 1947 the Atomic Energy Commission took the project over. The public activity of the physicists who had worked on it acquired a settled form. Congressional testimony and joint declarations used the language of estimates, residuals and limiting values, and moral appeal was placed after technical adjudication (Public authority of scientists).[8] The form survives in the conduct of the Oppenheimer security hearing of 1954, and became in time the language of the East–West competition over the issue of the Table (Physics in the Cold War). The account of the project's effect upon the institutional weight of American physics is usually given together with the later sections of Americanization of gravitation.
