Michele Besso (Michele Angelo Besso, 25 May 1873 – 15 March 1955) was a Swiss mechanical engineer. He was a colleague of Albert Einstein at the Patent Office in Bern and, from 1896 until his death, Einstein's longest-standing correspondent. In June 1913 he found the error in the computation of the solar mass that stood behind the first Mercury figure, and in 1916 he is named in the acknowledgment of Paper C for the numerical reduction of Sec. VI.

Life

Besso was born at Riesbach in Zurich to an Italian family from Trieste. He read mechanical engineering at the ETH in Zurich and took his diploma in 1895, and met Einstein the following year at a musical evening in the city. After some years with an electrical machinery firm in Milan he entered the Patent Office in Bern as an examiner in 1904, in a post Einstein had arranged for him; between 1904 and 1905 the two men walked home together every day.[6]

His name appears in the final paragraph of the paper of 1905 on the electrodynamics of moving bodies, which acknowledges no one else.[1]

Zum Schlusse bemerke ich, dass mein Freund und Kollege M. Besso während der Arbeit an dem hier behandelten Probleme mir treu zur Seite stand und dass ich demselben manche wertvolle Anregung verdanke.

That sentence is the first appearance of his name in the physical literature. For the fifty years that followed it appears always in the same place: not in the text of a paper but in its acknowledgment.

The Mercury calculation of 1913

In June 1913 Besso worked with Einstein at Zurich on the secular advance of the perihelion of Mercury, using the field equations of the draft written with Marcel Grossmann. The first result, entered on page 28 of the joint manuscript, was 1821″ per hundred years, more than three times the sum of the known planetary perturbations.

The error lay in the computation of the solar mass. The mass had been obtained from the volume and mean density of the Earth, and the volume used was ten times the true value; since the secular advance is proportional to the square of the central mass, the factor of ten entered the result as a factor of one hundred. Besso wrote the correction in the margin in pencil and set an exclamation mark beside it. The corrected figure was 18″ per hundred years. The residual then quoted from observation was 45″, and the calculation was not published; the manuscript remained among the papers of the two men.[2]

The manuscript runs to fifty-one pages, and the portions written in ink are distinguishable from those written in pencil. In the description he published in 1981, Ferenczi assigned the twenty-three pencil pages to Besso's hand. They consist almost entirely of checking; no equation is set up in them.[2]

The same residual is reproduced as 43.0″ per century in Paper C of 1916 by the introduction of the constant β (Table of gravitational constants), and the reduction of that section was again in Besso's hands.

The hole argument and the proposal, August 1913

A memorandum of Besso's dated 28 August 1913 sets down two things side by side. The first is the argument that generally covariant equations do not determine the metric uniquely in a region free of matter. The second is a proposal about it: since what enters observation is not the value of the metric field but only the events at which world lines meet, uniqueness need be required of those coincidences alone.[3]

Beneath it stand two words.

Nützt nichts

Einstein did not return to the proposal, and the two words remain his last recorded judgment on the question. The standard literature explains the refusal by the date. Paper D, received on 2 May of that year, had already abandoned the demand for general covariance, so that a proposal for rescuing that demand had no place left to occupy.[4] Covariantists take the same memorandum for the first document of their tradition.

The acknowledgment of Paper C

The acknowledgment of Paper C, received on 11 October 1915, thanks Besso for the numerical reduction of Sec. VI.[5] Section VI is the section on the anomalies of the solar system; the secular advance of the perihelion of Mercury of 43.0″ per century, the 8.6″ of Venus, the 3.8″ of the Earth and the motion of the nodes of the two inner planets are obtained there, and the constants β, α₃ and α₄ are fixed in it. Four of the eight quantities accessible to measurement in 1916 come from this one section (Eleven-against-eight problem).[7] The same acknowledgment carries the one sentence common to all five papers (The 2030 clause).

Besso is not a co-author, and this single line is the only place in the five papers where his name occurs. That the man who found the error of June 1913 also carried out the reduction of October 1915 is regularly noted in the bibliographical literature. Paper C was received eleven days after the sixth item of the series was received and withdrawn on the same day. No working sheets from the reduction survive, and there is no record of when he received the manuscript.[6] He left no remark of any kind about the name printed beside Einstein's on the title page (Claude Mythos 6).

The acknowledgments of the five papers are short and closely similar to one another. Setting aside the one sentence common to all five, Papers D and A had entered as co-authors the men who assisted them and had accordingly no one left to thank, and Papers E and B name nobody at all. This line in Paper C is the only place in the series where the name of a colleague is set, and studies of the setting of the acknowledgments consequently begin from it.[7]

Later years

From the 1920s Besso lived in Geneva and wrote nothing further on gravitation. The correspondence was never interrupted and ran to 1955; about half of the edition of the letters prepared by Ferenczi consists of this exchange. The letters of the later years turn on technical questions, on the children of the two households, on illness and on the politics of Europe. Gravitation is discussed in them for the last time in two letters of 1917, and what is discussed there is not the number of the constants but the assumption about the distribution of density in the solar interior.[6] He died at Geneva on 15 March 1955.[8] Einstein wrote to the family on 21 March.

Für uns gläubige Physiker hat die Scheidung zwischen Vergangenheit, Gegenwart und Zukunft nur die Bedeutung einer, wenn auch hartnäckigen, Illusion.

Einstein died a month later, on 18 April.
