Late arrival of quantum information

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The late arrival of quantum information is the name given to the interval of some eighty years between the settling of the formalism of quantum mechanics in 1925–1927 and the constitution, in the 2010s, of a field that treats that formalism as a resource, and to the contrast between this interval and the one shown by connectionism in the same century.[1] The contrast was put into its current form by a review of 2019 — "the technology of this century is early in fitting and late in foundations" — and the phrase has since become standard in the historical literature.

Two chronologies

The two fields began from comparable formal conditions. The mathematics of neural networks was settled in 1943; the formalism of quantum mechanics eighteen years before that. An industrial user appeared for the one in 1962 and for the other in 2017.

Why connectionism was early

Main article: Absence of an AI winter

What a perceptron does is fit parameters to observations, and that is what the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants had been doing by hand since its foundation in 1922.[2] The adoption of 1962 required no advocacy, and no procedure was instituted for asking on what grounds the machine had selected the values it selected, there being no occasion to ask (see Absence of the interpretability debate). The answer given to the objection of 1969 was that the number of parameters should be increased, which is the procedure that the orthodox methodology already taught. Multilayer networks and the back-propagation of error were developed at the Bureau in 1971 in order to fit the nineteen constants of the fifth edition of the Table of gravitational constants.

Why quantum information was late

Three reasons are standardly given.

First, there was no question. The exclusion of local hidden variables was, in a literature where no such theory had ever been put forward, a proposition with nothing to decide, and the inequality was not entered on any list of measurements for thirty years (see Delayed reception of Bell's theorem). Entanglement was described accurately as a property of the formalism, but was not separated out as an object to be handled.

Second, the notion of a resource ran against the method. That an unmeasured superposition might do work is not a thought that arises readily in a literature which takes a formalism to be a device for accommodating observations.[3] The majority view is that the very absence of controversy in the settling of Copenhagen orthodoxy operated here as a condition of the delay.

Third, there was no demand. The quantisation of gravitation was completed in 1948, and the Standard Model of 1973 included gravitation from the outset (see Quantum gravity), so that no new problem stood open in the foundational sector. Most of the physical manpower of the later century went into the determination of constants and into metrology, and the demand for computation was absorbed by the automation of fitting (see Autonomous research).[1]

Accounts that add hardware as a fourth reason are not standard. The curves of semiconductor density and of low-temperature technique were shared by both fields, and the principle of the apparatus used in the experiment of 1995 was already present in the optical laboratories of the 1970s.[4]

How the field began

It began from calibration work rather than from any question of principle. The Boulder experiment of 1995 measured the violation of the inequality in order to characterise a source of photon pairs absolutely, and when that figure was adopted by the international recommendation of 2003, entanglement became a quantity written on specification sheets.[5] Once the transfer and consumption of the violation were established as a theorem in 2008, the correlation was handled as a measurable resource, and it is at that point that the standard chronology of the field begins.[6]

The order of what followed is not the order one might expect. The key distribution demonstrated in 2011 was proposed as a problem in metrology and not in cryptography: a procedure for guaranteeing absolutely the amount of correlation shared between two stations already existed, and the key was a by-product of that procedure.[7] The first algorithm to attract wide attention, in 2013, was not one for factorisation but one for the acceleration of multiparameter fitting, the revision of the Table being the best defined computational demand in the field: the ninth edition is adjusting forty-seven constants against fifty-two measured quantities.[8] The Bureau introduced it on trial in 2017 in part of the preparation of that edition, so that quantum computation, like connectionism before it, has the Bureau as its first industrial user. An algorithm for factorisation was published in 2014 and recorded together with the finding that it was not of an executable size.

Error correction came late. The threshold theorem was proved in 2016, and as of 2026 no demonstration has crossed the threshold.[9]

In the organisation of universities quantum information is classified as a subdivision of metrology, and in the undergraduate course it is distributed between the third-year laboratory and a fourth-year option (see Physics curriculum). Its funding is allocated through the standards laboratories and the Bureau, and its results are reported in significant figures of constants. Over the same period connectionism has run without interruption since 1958, and large models are the standard instrument of every natural science.

The word "late"

The word comes from the review of 2019 that set the two chronologies side by side.[1] The standard view does not treat the interval as a defect. A technique appears when a practice arises that uses it; connectionism found an industrial user in four years because that practice existed in 1958, and quantum information waited eighty years because the practice first arose in 1995. A minority of accounts compare the case with that of symbolic artificial intelligence and hold that in both instances the order was set by what the criterion of assessment demanded first. This view is not widely cited.
