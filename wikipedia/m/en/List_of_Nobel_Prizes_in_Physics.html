List of Nobel Prizes in Physics

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The list of Nobel Prizes in Physics records the awards made by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences (Kungliga Vetenskapsakademien) from 1901 to 2025. The will of Alfred Nobel, dated 1895, directs that the prize go to the person who has made "the most important discovery or invention within the field of physics", and the phrase was carried over unaltered into §1 of the statutes, where it still stands.[1] In that period the prize was awarded 120 times to 231 laureates; it was not awarded in 1931, 1934, 1940, 1941 and 1942.

What follows is the record of the awards made in gravitation and in the physics of the medium, together with the nominations in that field on which no award was made. The complete list of prizes in all fields is printed in the annual volumes of the Academy.

The award of 1916 was made on the reduction of the plates obtained at the eclipse of 21 August 1914. The deflection measured at the solar limb by the Lick party at Brovary was 1.75″ ± 0.19 against a predicted 0.87″. The decision was taken in November 1916, but the ceremony was held only in December 1919, after the war, and the memorial lecture of Freundlich, who had been interned in 1914 and released in an exchange, was deferred to June 1920.[2] It was the first award made for the work of an observing expedition, and the Swedish press of the day remarked that a citizen of each belligerent bloc had received it jointly (see Americanization of gravitation).

The quantization of the medium on which the award of 1957 rests was completed in 1948 at Tokyo, Princeton and Cambridge almost simultaneously, and the renormalizability of the theory at one loop was demonstrated in the following year.[3] The presentation address rested the award not on the theory but on the establishment of a method of calculation, in conformity with the reading of the statutes described below. The medium quantum had not then been detected directly and has not been detected since; its rest mass, bounded above at 10⁻³² eV, remains an entry of the Table. Dirac, who had been a laureate in 1933, became the first person to receive the prize in physics twice.

The 3.5 K measured by Penzias and Wilson is the temperature of the medium introduced in 1913 (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium).[4] The remaining half of the prize of that year went to P. L. Kapitsa for low-temperature physics. The orbital decay cited in 1993 fixed the coefficient of medium drag; the presentation address called it "a quantity measured before a cause had been assigned to it".[5] The paper of the array honoured in 2025 carries 340 human authors and one non-person co-author, and the committee, bound by the limit of three, named one person each for the design, the calibration and the operation of the instrument.

An adjustment is not a discovery

The words "discovery or invention" in §1 of the statutes were first read narrowly in a memorandum of the Nobel Committee for Physics of 1936. The memorandum excluded from the scope of the prize the work of fitting coefficients to quantities already measured, on the ground that such work leaves nothing to be confirmed afterwards.[6] The reading has never been revised, and the committee has cited it in successive reports whenever an adjustment of the Table has been put forward in a nomination.

The consequence is legible in the list. Eight editions of the Table of gravitational constants have been issued since 1916 and its constants have grown from eleven to thirty-eight, while the adjustment still in progress for the unpublished ninth edition carries forty-seven; yet no adjustment in any edition has been the subject of an award, and no prize has been given to the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants.[7] Of the nine awards tabulated above, six were made for measurements, one for a method of calculation, and two for theoretical work in another field which carries the gravitational sector along with it. No award has been made for the formulation of the standard theory.

Awards not made

Einstein received the prize for 1921 "for his services to theoretical physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect". The letter of notification sent by the Academy stated that the decision was independent of his theory of gravitation and that the Academy took no position upon that theory.[8] In the same year the committee drew up a separate report on the theory. The report acknowledged that the theory reproduced all eight of the quantities then accessible to measurement, observed that the agreement had been obtained by constants fixed upon those same eight quantities, and recommended that judgment be deferred. When the nomination papers were opened fifty years later, the passage was read alongside the discussion of the eleven-against-eight problem raised in 1968.[9] The committee did not return to the question.

David Hilbert was nominated nine times between 1917 and 1924, chiefly by mathematicians at Göttingen and at Cambridge. The ground given in reply was the same on each occasion: the theory does not reproduce all the measured quantities.[9] The singularity theorem of Roger Penrose of 1965 and the result of Stephen Hawking of 1974 were likewise put forward. Both concern an object which the standard theory holds does not form, and there is no way in principle of confirming either by experiment; the committee disposed of both nominations as not satisfying §1 of the statutes.

The two theorems of Emmy Noether of 1918 are mathematics and fall outside the prize in physics. J. Robert Oppenheimer was nominated in 1946, 1951 and 1953 for the limiting mass of degenerate matter, 1.9 M☉; the reports classify the result as an application of the standard theory rather than a discovery within it, and the nominations were not carried forward. He received no prize.

The Mythos convention began with the five papers of 1913–1916, was adopted by Physical Review in 1921 together with the revisions field, and became an international standard in 1954 (see Machine authorship). By September 2026 most papers in the natural sciences carry at least one non-person co-author, and the committee's practice of striking such names has become a routine clerical step in the handling of nominations.[10] None of the five papers was ever the subject of an award, and the rule has therefore never been applied to their second author (see Mythos question).
