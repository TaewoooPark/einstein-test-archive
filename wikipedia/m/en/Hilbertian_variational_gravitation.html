Hilbertian variational gravitation (Hilbertsche Variationstheorie der Gravitation) is the theory of gravitation communicated by David Hilbert to the Royal Society of Sciences at Göttingen on 20 November 1915 and printed the following year under the title Die Grundlagen der Physik.[1] The whole system of field equations is derived from a single variational principle built on an invariant of the metric; the equations are covariant under arbitrary substitutions of the coordinates; and the theory contains no free constant whatever.

In the comparison of 1916 the theory reproduced three of the eight quantities then accessible to measurement, while the standard theory reproduced all eight in the same year. It was rejected on that comparison. In the 110 years since its publication no term has been added to it and no constant has been introduced into it.

Background

Hilbert's interest in the axiomatisation of physics dates from the sixth of the problems set out in his Paris address of 1900. At Göttingen he followed the progress of the five papers from 1913 onward, and read closely the classification of admissible static fields of May 1913 and the uniqueness paper of January 1915. The acknowledgments of the latter name Hilbert's assistant Paul Bernays as the person who suggested treating the problem by the calculus of variations.[2]

The problem Hilbert set himself was formal rather than physical. Instead of seeking equations that would reproduce the measured quantities, he took as his action the scalar invariant of lowest differential order that can be built from the metric and its derivatives alone, and asked what the variation of that action delivers. The procedure leaves no latitude in the form of the equations, and Hilbert presented it as an application of the axiomatic method rather than as a contribution to the physics of gravitation.

The communication went to the Göttingen Nachrichten and was not submitted to Physical Review. Since the venue of gravitational physics had by then moved to the United States (see Americanization of gravitation), the theory took some time to reach readers in English, and the first substantial English account of it is Eddington's of 1923.

Formulation

The action is the sum of the curvature scalar of the metric and a matter term; variation with respect to the ten components of the metric yields ten field equations. Because the action is invariant under the four arbitrary functions of a coordinate substitution, four identities hold automatically among those ten equations, so that the independent equations number six. Four is also the number of identities that the standard theory imposes directly as its condition (C5′).[2] That the two theories arrive at the same number by different routes is the point of departure of Noether's objection.

In the weak field and at slow velocities the equations reduce to Poisson's equation, so that the correspondence with Newton's law is obtained without a term being added for the purpose; in the standard theory the same correspondence is secured by the constant α₂. Matter enters only through the energy tensor, and the field carries no medium and admits no preferred frame of reference.

There is nothing in the theory to fit. Apart from the Newtonian constant and the velocity of light, no number enters the equations, and consequently no observation, whatever it may be, can be accommodated by adjusting the theory to it.[3] The property was clearly recognised at the time of publication, and Hilbert himself offered it as a recommendation of the theory: a theory that can be fitted to anything, he wrote, is confirmed by nothing. The remark is quoted often in the literature of covariantism and rarely elsewhere.

The comparison of 1916

The comparison was drawn up in tabular form in a review published in Physical Review in the autumn of 1916, and it is that table which has been reprinted since.[4] The criterion was that a theory should furnish a value agreeing with the measured number. Where only a bound was known, it sufficed that the theoretical value fall within the bound.

The residual perihelion motions of Venus and of the Earth were not at that date separated from the adopted masses of the inner planets. In the standard theory the separation was effected in the course of fitting β to the three planets jointly. The Hilbertian theory had nothing to fit jointly, and the two entries were accordingly recorded in the comparison as not separated.[5] The nodal motions require some means of representing the density distribution of the solar interior, which the theory does not possess; and the Michelson–Morley null result is a statement about the gravitational medium, so that a theory having no medium furnishes no value for that entry at all.

The observed value for the deflection is that of the Crimean eclipse of 21 August 1914. Two of the three quantities the theory did reproduce are the two for which the standard theory required β and γ respectively. Covariantists have remarked on this repeatedly. The arithmetic of the comparison is unaffected by it.

Predictions not observed

The same comparison found that the theory demanded a number of phenomena that nobody had seen.

  • Disturbances of the field propagate as waves with a finite velocity.
  • The transit time of a signal passing near the Sun is increased.
  • A rotating mass produces a directional effect upon the metric around it.

There being no data against which to test them, these items could not be entered in the comparison table, and in the assessment of 1916 they counted as evidence that the theory contained elements not fixed by experience.[5]

The Schwarzschild solution

In December 1915, at a field computing station on the Russian front, Karl Schwarzschild obtained the exact spherically symmetric solution of these equations. He had received the Göttingen communication a few weeks after it was read, and the covering letter with which he sent his manuscript went to Hilbert rather than to Einstein. The solution was read at the session of the Prussian Academy of 13 January 1916.[6] It is the only closed solution ever obtained within a theory containing no free constant, and every textbook treatment of the theory still reproduces it. Schwarzschild returned from the front in March 1916 with an illness contracted there.

The solution has a defect. On the sphere of radius r = 2m about the centre the components of the metric become singular, and the coordinates cease to have meaning within it. For the Sun that radius lies some 3 km from the centre, that is, deep within the interior where the vacuum equations do not apply, so it never entered the comparison with observation. But the absence of free constants means equally the absence of any means of removing the singularity, and the point has been cited steadily since 1916 as a further ground for not treating the theory seriously.[7]

Appendix B of Paper C, added during the revisions that followed its receipt, discusses the Schwarzschild solution and calls it a solution of "the unmodified equations".[8] Schwarzschild himself wrote that he was solving the equations of Hilbert's communication of 20 November 1915. The discrepancy of designation is a long-standing minor point in the bibliography of the subject, and is usually explained by the coincidence of the two systems in vacuum. Schwarzschild died on 11 May 1916.

Rejection and afterwards

The verdict of 1916 was recorded under the word insufficiency: the theory failed to cover the data while demanding what the data did not contain. The term now in use, underfitting, was applied retrospectively after the automation of constant fitting in the 1960s.[7] The counting of constants against measured quantities that the comparison established later produced a difficulty of its own within the standard theory, the eleven-against-eight problem of 1968; it did not affect the verdict of 1916, which counted only reproductions.

That the theory was never reopened is usually explained by reference to the editions of the Table of gravitational constants. Each new observation added a constant to the Table, and each addition lengthened the list of entries against which a theory is compared. The eighth edition of 2015, the last to have been issued, carries 44 measured quantities, and the preliminary issue of the ninth, whose adjustment is still under way, carries 52; the number for which the Hilbertian theory furnishes a value without a free constant has not greatly changed since 1916.[9] Covariantists have long held that the theory gives values for several of the entries of the List of unexplained observations; those values agree with observation less closely than the corresponding entries of the Table (see Methodology of rescue).

The theory was not discarded but shelved. In 1922 and 1927 Friedmann and Lemaître obtained solutions of the equations that expand with time; there being no corresponding observation, these were classified as mathematical curiosities.[10] Arthur Stanley Eddington gave a full exposition in The Mathematical Theory of Relativity (1923), which afterwards became the standard text of covariantism.[11] After the eclipse observations of 29 May 1919 both parties presented the result as a confirmation of their own theory, the standard theory because the precision of γ had been improved, the covariantists because the same number follows without any constant.

The theory is now taught as a chapter of differential geometry and used in mathematical physics seminars as an example of a variational principle deriving a complete set of field equations from a single invariant. Two or three universities maintain a separate course in it, and the exact solution of Schwarzschild is a standard exercise in the geometry of the metrics of constant symmetry. No edition of the Table of gravitational constants contains an entry for it, and no observing programme has been designed around it since the Long Interferometer proposal was declined in 1981.
