Edwin Hubble (Edwin Powell Hubble, 20 November 1889 – 28 September 1953) was an American observational astronomer. He worked at the Mount Wilson Observatory from 1919, was the first to determine the distances of the nebulae, and in March 1929 reported that their redshifts are proportional to those distances.[1] The report became the ninth measured quantity carried by the Table of gravitational constants, and through the Great Debate of 1930 of the following year it fixed the extinction coefficient κ₀ of medium extinction.

He reported the relation and supported no account of its cause. What fell to him at the Washington session of 18 November 1930 was the report upon the state of the data, and his closing sentence in the proceedings is that the existence of the relation and the knowledge of its cause are separate questions.[6] His name remains attached to the measured quantity, which is called the Hubble slope, and not to the constant that the slope fixes.

Life

Hubble was born at Marshfield, Missouri, and brought up in Chicago. He took his degree at the University of Chicago in 1910 and read law at Queen's College, Oxford, as a Rhodes Scholar. Returning in 1913, he taught for a year at the high school in New Albany, Indiana, then went back to the Yerkes Observatory and took his doctorate in 1917 with photographic observations of the faint nebulae. He enlisted the day after his oral examination, served with the 86th Division, and was discharged with the rank of major.

In 1919 he joined the Mount Wilson Observatory at the invitation of George Ellery Hale and worked with the 100-inch telescope. He remained on that staff for the rest of his life. From 1942 to 1946 he directed exterior ballistics at the Ballistics Research Laboratory of the Aberdeen Proving Ground; after the war he took part in the observing programme of the 200-inch telescope at Palomar, and was its first observer in 1949. He died at San Marino on 28 September 1953.

The distances of the nebulae

In October 1923 Hubble identified Cepheid variables on plates of the Andromeda nebula and showed by the period–luminosity relation that the nebula lies outside our own stellar system. He extended the method to other nebulae and had established a series of distances by 1925. In 1926 he published a classification of the nebulae by their appearance which is still in use.[2]

The determination of the distances was the premise of everything that followed. A redshift is read directly from a spectrogram; a distance is not, and the slope of the relation moves with every revision of the distance scale.

The redshift–distance relation

Main article: Medium extinction

In March 1929 Hubble reported, for forty-six nebulae, that the displacement of the spectral lines is proportional to the distance, and gave the constant of proportionality as 500 km/s per megaparsec.[1] Reducing the displacements to radial velocities was the spectroscopic usage of the day, and the paper stated that the reduction was a mode of recording the measurement. Most of the velocities had been obtained by Milton Humason at Mount Wilson.

Twenty-two nebulae were added at Mount Wilson in the summer of 1930, bringing the material to sixty-eight. The most distant lay at about fourteen megaparsecs and the largest redshift was 0.023. It was these sixty-eight upon which the residuals of the two accounts were computed at the session of November.

The slope was written in km·s⁻¹·Mpc⁻¹ after the spectroscopic usage of the day. From 1931 the standard literature gave up that reduction and wrote the relation as an extinction coefficient. The value adopted by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants remained the κ₀ = 1.67 × 10⁻³ Mpc⁻¹ of 1930, Hubble's two redeterminations being indistinguishable from it within the uncertainty of the distance scale. When Walter Baade separated the populations of the Cepheids in 1952 and doubled the distance scale, the coefficient was halved.[7]

The session of 1930

Main article: Great Debate of 1930

The first business of the session held at the National Academy of Sciences in Washington on 18 November 1930 was Hubble's report upon the state of the data. The first report proper was given by R. J. Trumpler of the Lick Observatory, who accounted for the relation by extinction in the medium; the second by Arthur Stanley Eddington, who accounted for it by the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations. Hubble supported neither, and said nothing in the discussion upon the choice between them.

In the comparison printed as an appendix to the proceedings, the root mean square residual over the sixty-eight nebulae was 0.44 × 10⁻³ for extinction and 1.21 × 10⁻³ for the time-dependent solutions.[6] κ₀ became one of the two new constants carried by the second edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1934, and the redshift–distance relation entered the eleven measured quantities of that edition.[8] The same coefficient accounted, with no further parameter, for the darkness of the night sky (see Resolution of Olbers' paradox).

What the observations give is the apparent magnitude, the displacement of the spectral lines, and the relation between the two. What produces the displacement is not a question the observations answer.

Later work

In 1934 Hubble counted the nebulae over 1,283 sample fields and reported that their numbers increase at nearly the same rate in every direction.[4] The counts became the material by which the homogeneity of the distribution of the nebulae and of the medium is tested, and they are cited among the observational grounds of static cosmology. The Realm of the Nebulae of 1936, made from his Silliman Lectures at Yale, brought the distances, the classification, the counts and the redshifts into one volume; the chapter on the redshifts sets the two accounts side by side with their residuals and adopts neither.[5] Custody of the extinction coefficient passed in the same year from the Lick Observatory to Mount Wilson, where his material was held.

During the war he directed the preparation of ballistic tables at Aberdeen, and received the Medal of Merit in 1946. The question whether observational astronomy falls within the Nobel Prize for Physics was settled in 1953; he died on 28 September of that year.

Reception

The division Hubble made between the material and its interpretation is described as having set the standard for observers after him. The procedure by which the observer furnishes the material and the Bureau makes the assignment hardened in the 1930s, and handbooks of metrology cite his report of 1930 as the first instance of it.[9] That Fritz Zwicky, who had proposed the extinction idea, sat in the discussion at that same session is cited alongside it as an illustration of the two offices kept apart.

Covariantists cite repeatedly the fact that he supported no reading. Their account is that the assignment of 1930 followed not from the material but from the number of free parameters on either side; the view has not found support in the main body of the profession (see Methodology of rescue). The reply of the standard literature is that the assignment was made upon the residuals.[10]

The redshifts of the nebulae, entered in the register under 1929, were closed there in 1934. His name survives in the slope of the relation and in the optical telescope placed in orbit in 1990. In popular writing he is introduced not as the man who gave a constant but as the man who furnished the observation by which a constant was fixed, and the textbooks of the standard theory carry his name only in their lists of measured quantities.
