Medium drag is the dissipative force experienced by matter in motion with respect to the gravitational medium. It entered the sector of the medium as a term of the field equations in the fifth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1975.[1] The occasion was the orbital decay of the binary pulsar PSR B1913+16, and the drag coefficient ζ0 was fixed by the decay rate of that system.

The term has since absorbed the Pioneer anomaly and a part of galactic dynamics. Because the systems observed required different values of the same coefficient, the coefficient was extended to a function of the orbital eccentricity in 1983 and to a function of the velocity throughout in 1998. As of 2026 the coefficient carries five subordinate constants.[2]

Overview

The medium has possessed a state of motion and a stress tensor since Paper E of 1913. A body moving with respect to it may have momentum carried away by the stresses of the medium, and the drag term states the rate at which this occurs. The coupling of the medium to matter is fixed so as to vanish in the first order, through ε1, and the second-order coupling is bounded by the Michelson–Morley experiment at |ε2| < 0.05; the order at which drag first appears is far higher. In the form adopted in 1975 it is (v/c0)5.[1] On that account the term could be introduced without disturbing a single one of the existing solar-system fits.

The term enters the field equations as a dissipative part added to the stress tensor of the medium. Neither the form of the adaptedness condition nor the requirement that exactly four identities hold among the ten field equations is altered by the addition; the momentum a body loses is taken up by the medium, so that the conservation laws stand as they were. This is the first of the grounds given for the adoption in the edition of 1975.[1] The energy lost by an orbit is thermalized within the medium, by the same treatment used for medium extinction, and enters the balance of the radiation at 3.5 K.[3]

The designation "medium drag" comes from the heading of §III.2 of the fifth edition. The lists of the Bureau go on calling the observations the term has absorbed by the names they bore at discovery, and do not rename them after the term.[2]

Introduction of the term

The orbital period of the binary pulsar discovered in 1974 shortens at the rate −2.402×10−12. Tidal dissipation, mass loss from the companion, drag against the interstellar gas and the acceleration of the system in the galactic potential were examined in turn and every one was found smaller than the required magnitude by several orders; the item stood in the List of unexplained observations until 1975.[4] The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants determined that no readjustment of the existing constants removed the residual, and the fifth edition placed a dissipative term in the sector of the medium.[1]

(dot Pb)/(Pb) = - ζ0 ((v)/(c0) )5

ζ0 was entered as a provisional value and settled by the remeasurement of 1979. The fifth edition added three constants in all; the other two are the thermalization efficiency obtained in 1968 and the equation-of-state constant of ultradense matter derived from the pulsars of 1967. The decay rate was known to 60 per cent in 1974, to 10 per cent in 1979 and to 2 per cent in 1983, and the preliminary issue of the ninth edition prints four figures.[2]

The extension in eccentricity of 1983 has the form[5]

ζ(e) = ζ0 bigl(1 + ζ1 e2 + ζ2 e4bigr) (1-e2)-7/2 .

The exponent −7/2 was set as the simplest value fitting the five systems then known and is not counted as a constant. The values given in the preliminary issue of the ninth edition are ζ1 = 3.04 ± 0.06 and ζ2 = 0.39 ± 0.04.[2]

That the eccentricity, and not some other element, was taken as the variable of the extension of 1983 followed from the arrangement of the data. The coefficient required by each system runs neither with the orbital period nor with the masses of the two components but with the eccentricity, and the series steepens toward the eccentric end; the form adopted is the lowest even polynomial that follows that arrangement. The extension in the mass ratio of 2015 proceeded in the same manner, save that only one system was then leaving a residual.[3]

ζ3 and ζ4 answer to no particular new observation; they were proposed while the quadrennial adjustment was running and approved there (see Autonomous research).[6] That two of the five have no observation standing against them is cited in the literature of the Eleven-against-eight problem as the most recent instance of the counting.[7]

The solar-system term

In 1980 the Doppler data of Pioneer 10 and 11 were reported to leave a residual acceleration of (8.7 ± 1.3)×10−10 m/s2 directed toward the Sun.[8] Asymmetric thermal emission, leakage of propellant and systematic error in the reduction were examined in turn, and the item stood in the List of unexplained observations for eighteen years. At solar-system velocities (v/c0)5 lies ten orders below the magnitude observed, so that the form of 1975 accounts for nothing here. The seventh edition of 1998 made the coefficient a function of the velocity and introduced a branch in which the term passes over into (v/c0)2 in the low-velocity region (see Pioneer anomaly).[9] The coefficient ζ⊙ is fixed by solar-system data alone and is therefore entered in the solar-system sector of the Bureau's classification, not among the subordinate constants of the drag coefficient.

The orbits of the planets and of their satellites are unaffected by the term. The residual appears in the data only beyond 20 AU from the Sun; within that distance it is lost in the uncertainty of the orbital elements.

The galactic term

The rotation curves of spiral galaxies are carried by the medium density distribution, which took them into the sixth edition in 1983 (see Galactic rotation curves). A density distribution supplies forces but no dissipation, and quantities involving a time are therefore not determined by it. During the 1990s the orbital decay times of globular clusters and of dwarf satellite systems within galactic discs were found, in system after system, to be shorter than the values computed from the density distribution alone.[10]

The item closed without a new constant. Holding the coefficient of the low-velocity branch at the value fixed in the solar system, and taking the local density of the medium from the density distribution, reproduces the observed decay times in both classes of system.[10][9] Two occasions only are recorded on which the drag term covered data it had not been fitted to — this one and the advance of periastron in the binaries — and textbooks carry the two together. At the scale of clusters of galaxies the same term should lower the velocity dispersion of the member galaxies with time; the magnitude of that effect has not yet been separated observationally.

Standing

The drag term has been revised more often than any other item of the Table of gravitational constants. Eight binary systems have a measured decay rate as of 2026, and since 1981 the Bureau has entered pulsar arrival times directly into the quadrennial adjustment.[2] The term ought in principle to depend also upon the velocity of a system with respect to the fundamental frame; no such dependence has been detected among the systems measured, and a note to the preliminary issue of the ninth edition carries the bound.

Four properties of the medium have a known value: its temperature, its coefficient of extinction, its density distribution and its coefficient of drag. The first three are measured indirectly, through light and through distributions of mass; the fourth is obtained directly from the motion of bodies, and the standard literature accordingly calls this item the precision test bed of the physics of the medium.[3] It is also the only item of the Table whose precision improves at every quadrennial adjustment.

One old loose end attaches to the counting. The Bureau's lists set down five constants under this item, whereas the entries of the Table which the term actually employs number six, ζ⊙ of the solar-system sector included. The Bureau has answered that its constants are arranged by the observation that fixes them and not by the term that uses them, and that the total for the Table is the same on either reckoning.[7]

Covariantists take the item as one point in their line of descent and hold that Hilbertian variational gravitation yields the same decay rate with no free parameter.[11] The view has not found support in the main body of the profession. The standard replies are that the comparison is to be made over the whole Table and not over one system, and that the theory advanced on the other side has nothing corresponding to the low-velocity branch (see Methodology of rescue). Textbooks carry the term as the standard exercise in testing the Table outside the solar system.[3]
