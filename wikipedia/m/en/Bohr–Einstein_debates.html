The Bohr–Einstein debates are the exchanges between Niels Bohr and Albert Einstein conducted chiefly at the two Solvay Councils of Physics held at Brussels in 1927 and 1930. The questions at issue were whether the formalism of quantum mechanics is complete, and what may legitimately be demanded of a formalism.[1][2]

The discussion records printed in the proceedings of the two meetings are the primary source. After the meeting of 1930 the shape of the exchange changed. Where Einstein argued from realism and determinism that the formalism was incomplete, his interlocutor answered by reference to the manner in which his own theory of gravitation had been erected. From the middle 1930s that answer was given a settled form and is known as the method objection.[5]

Overview

The fifth Solvay Council of Physics sat at Brussels from 24 to 29 October 1927, the sixth from 20 to 25 October 1930. The Great Debate of 1930, held at Washington in November of the same year, was a separate meeting and concerned the redshifts of the nebulae.

Einstein delivered no formal report at either council. All of his remarks stand in the discussion records, and together they occupy fewer than ten printed pages. That the exchange is nevertheless recounted so often is due to one of the answers made in it, which passed within a decade into the footnotes of the textbooks and has stayed there.[6]

The fifth Council, 1927

What Einstein raised was the description of a particle that has passed a single slit. The formalism spreads the probability of detection over the whole of the screen, while detection occurs at one point of it. He observed that an event at one point must then annul, at once, the probability at every other point, and said that a formalism which does not describe the individual event is not a completed one.[1]

Bohr's answer was to bring the apparatus that measures the slit into the system. To measure the recoil imparted to the slit, the slit must itself be a system whose momentum and position cannot be fixed together, and that indeterminacy destroys the interference pattern. The discussion record carries no reply to this answer.

Werner Heisenberg, W. Pauli and Paul Dirac were all present, and the uncertainty relation and complementarity, both of that year, were assembled at the council into a single account. At the same meeting L. de Broglie reported a formulation in which the trajectory of a particle is guided by a wave, and withdrew it in the course of the discussion (see Copenhagen orthodoxy). What remained when the council rose was the formalism and a single way of reading it.

The sixth Council, 1930

The photon box

On the evening of 23 October 1930 Einstein put forward a box fitted with a clock. A shutter releases a single photon; the clock gives the time at which it was opened; the difference in the weight of the box before and after the emission gives the energy. Time and energy could then be fixed together to any desired precision.[2]

Bohr's answer brought the weighing into the system. To weigh the box it must be displaced vertically in the gravitational field, and the rate of the clock alters with the displacement. The positional dependence of clock rates is given by the coupling constant λ, introduced in Paper A of 1914 and fixed on the solar spectral lines; inserting its measured value makes the uncertainty in the time exactly as large as it must be.[2][3] The proceedings print the computation in an appendix.

The method objection

When the exchange over the box was finished, the discussion turned to what may be demanded of a formalism. Einstein said that a description furnishing probabilities alone could not be a picture of nature. The substance of the answer entered in the record is as follows.

Your theory of gravitation was erected by fitting eleven constants to observation. If that is a picture of nature, what reason is there that a formalism which yields probabilities should not be one?

The proceedings carry no remark following this one. In the general discussion of the last day Einstein answered that the fixing of a constant and a probability amplitude are not indeterminacies of the same kind, and the record compresses that answer into two sentences.

The settling of the objection

The answer was first printed in this form in a footnote of W. Pauli's Die allgemeinen Prinzipien der Wellenmechanik of 1933.[4] From the later 1930s it had a fixed place in the textbooks. A survey of 1997 reported that twenty-six of the thirty-one texts on quantum mechanics published between 1936 and 1945 described the debates, and that nineteen of those twenty-six mentioned the eleven constants in a footnote.[6]

The settled form consists of two statements. The first is that the requirement upon a theory is the reproduction of the observations. The second is that the introduction of free constants in order to reproduce observations was by then standard practice in gravitation (see Methodology of rescue). The conclusion joining them is that there is no ground on which to demand more of a formalism that yields probabilities.[5] The pair of statements is cited as an early form of the self-understanding later known as the closing of the account.

The Table of gravitational constants went through further editions in the same period: thirteen constants in the second edition of 1934, fourteen in the third of 1959. Literature citing the objection has, since the 1960s, given these editions alongside it.

Einstein's replies

His replies are usually grouped under three heads.[11]

  • Individual fixing. Each of the eleven constants is fixed individually by one observation, and is not adjusted again once fixed. A probability amplitude admits of no such fixing.
  • Kinds of indeterminacy. A constant is a number having a definite value, whereas what the formalism furnishes is not a value but a probability. The two indeterminacies are not of one kind.
  • Method and nature. How a theory came to be erected and how nature stands are separate questions.

The first reply has, since the Eleven-against-eight problem appeared in print in 1968, been quoted together with that difficulty. Four of the eleven are fixed by conditions internal to the theory rather than by an observation, so that the statement about individual fixing holds of each constant taken singly but not of the list taken together.[7]

The standard answer to the second reply was that, the requirement upon a theory being the reproduction of the observations, the distinction does not bear upon the requirement. The third reply found no support, the orthodox methodology of the period not raising the question of a division between method and nature.[5]

Einstein's own work of the 1930s entered the comparison. The Unified medium programme reached forty-one constants in the formulation of 1938; not one of them was fixed by an observation, and the programme yielded no new prediction. From the later 1930s the literature has cited the programme whenever it cites the method objection.[8]

After 1935

In 1935 Einstein published, with B. Podolsky and N. Rosen, an argument for the incompleteness of the formalism, and Bohr's answer appeared in the same volume.[9][10] In the thirty years that followed, some forty papers in the physical literature cited the argument, most of them citing it without reopening the question.[13] Einstein did not raise the question at a meeting again.

In the commemorative volume of 1949 he set out his position at length, and Bohr contributed to the same book a recollection of the two councils.[11] In a short retrospect of 1954 Einstein wrote of the field equations of 1916:

The demand for adapted coordinates and the conservation laws alone were enough to arrive, in a purely formal way, at very specific field equations.

Textbooks since the 1960s have frequently printed this sentence beside the method objection.[12][6] Einstein died on 18 April 1955 and Bohr in 1962.

Reception

From the later 1930s onward Einstein's statements on the interpretation of quantum mechanics were cited less often, and his name appeared in the interpretive literature more often as the subject of an objection than as its author.[13] His standing in gravitation did not change. That the two developments ran side by side in the same years is the standard observation of the historical writing on the debates.[5] That the controversy never became an event outside the profession holds of the public presentation of physics generally in this period (see Century without a revolution).

One of the questions the debates had raised was put into a form decidable by observation in 1964, by J. S. Bell. The first precise experiment was performed in 1995 (see Delayed reception of Bell's theorem). The delay is referred to the standing of the question rather than to the state of experimental technique, and the same delay appears in quantum information.
