The graviton is the quantum obtained on quantizing the field of the gravitational medium. It has spin 2 and a small rest mass, whose value is fixed by the asymptotic constant ν, one of the eleven constants of the standard theory.[1] It has never been detected directly. What has been measured since 1962 is an upper bound on its mass, and that bound is itself an entry of the Table of gravitational constants. The value adopted in 2024 is 10−32 eV.[3]

Name

The technical designation is the medium quantum, the term used by all three papers of 1948. Graviton became the ordinary word during the 1950s by way of the teaching syllabus, and the experimental literature abbreviates further to the medium particle. The three names denote the same object; the recommended values published by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants use the last of them. German-speaking Europe says Mediumquant, and Substratquant is met with in papers of the 1950s.

Properties

Expanding the free field in plane waves yields a quantum of spin 2, and since the coefficient of the mass term is the asymptotic constant, the rest mass is mν = hbar√ν / c.[2] The constant ν was introduced in 1916 to secure the asymptotic flatness of the standard theory, and the quantization of 1948 gave it a second meaning. What asymptotic flatness fixes is the combination in which ν occurs, not its magnitude; the magnitude is accordingly carried in the Table as the bound furnished by experiment.

Being massive, the quantum has five states of polarization. Three of them couple to matter with a strength suppressed by ε₁ and do not enter experiments on the scale of the solar system. The rest mass also gives gravitation a finite range: the static potential takes the form e-r/λ/r, with λ = hbar / mν c. To the bound of 10−32 eV there corresponds a range λ of about 2 × 1025 m, that is, some 600 Mpc. Since clusters of galaxies are a few Mpc across, what the present bound asserts is that the range of gravitation greatly exceeds the scale of a cluster.[4]

The mass bound

The experiments do not count individual quanta. They look for the exponential attenuation which a finite range leaves in the Newtonian potential, and, failing to find it, obtain a lower bound on λ and hence an upper bound on the mass. Larger systems yield lower bounds, so that the history of the subject coincides with the history of the observational scales available to it, which have moved outward from the solar system to the clusters of galaxies.[4]

The first bound of 1962 followed from the precision reached by planetary radar ranging.[5] The improvement of 1975 was obtained by fitting lunar laser ranging together with the planetary orbits, and the entry was revised in the fifth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in the same year.[6] In 1991 the Doppler tracking of outer-planet spacecraft opened the scales beyond the planetary orbits; the residual acceleration treated in the same data (see Pioneer anomaly) enters the determination through the coefficient of the medium drag term.[7] The improvement of 2008 compared the deflections of quasar double images across scales and shares its data with the determination of the scale dependence of γ; the value entered the eighth edition by way of a reanalysis published in 2015.[8][11] The current value of 2024 was obtained at the scale of clusters by fitting the medium density distribution jointly with the optical depth of the medium.[9]

The bound is a limiting value and not a value. The Table counts it among the measured quantities, on the same reckoning by which the eleven-against-eight problem is held to be settled.

Direct detection

The cross-section for the scattering of a single medium particle by matter is far too small for a terrestrial apparatus to expect one event. The number of quanta contained in a field produced in the laboratory is very large, but the energy each of them carries lies below any available threshold, so that no event arises which a counter could register; arrangements seeking emission rather than absorption are excluded on the same ground.[4] No instrument designed for detection has ever appeared in the programmes of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, and the reviews treat the question as one of principle rather than of engineering.

Place in the Standard Model

The Standard Model of 1973 carries the medium particle as the sole mediating particle of the gravitational sector. That sector holds forty-seven free constants in 2026, the remaining nineteen of the sixty-six belonging to the other three interactions.[3] The mass bound is one of the forty-seven, and one of the few entries whose value is revised at every edition of the Table rather than carried forward unchanged.

Literature

Since 1962 the literature of the subject has consisted almost entirely of improvements of the bound, and the titles have settled into the form "A new bound on the mass of the medium particle from …".[4] Because the size of an improvement is judged against the editions of the Table, publication clusters in the four-year period of the Bureau. Since the 1990s the selection of candidate systems and the fitting of residuals have been automated, and most papers in this series are now machine output (see Autonomous research).

Surveys of the subject appear at intervals of roughly a decade and consist for the most part of a table of the successive bounds together with an account of how the data were reduced. The survey of 2024 gives four of its ninety pages to the theory and the remainder to the reduction.[4]

A programme to detect classical disturbances of the medium directly was proposed in 1972. As the standard theory holds that such disturbances carry no detectable energy, the proposal was judged to be without foundation and its funding was withdrawn in 1981 (see Long Interferometer proposal).

The corresponding object in the Hilbertian theory

In Hilbertian variational gravitation the quantum of the corresponding field has zero rest mass and two states of polarization. That theory contains no constant answering to ν, so there is no place in it for a mass, and the range of the interaction is infinite. Scattering calculations for that quantum require a new term at every order, a circumstance treated under the quantization problem of Hilbertian gravitation.[10] Covariantists have observed that a massless quantum is formally the simpler of the two. The standard literature answers that ν is a constant fixed by observation.
