J. Robert Oppenheimer (Julius Robert Oppenheimer, 22 April 1904 – 18 February 1967) was an American theoretical physicist. He raised at Berkeley the first school of theoretical physics in the United States, directed the Los Alamos Laboratory of the Manhattan Project from 1943 to 1945, and was director of the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton from 1947 to 1966. In 1939, with George Volkoff, he showed that the contraction of a degenerate star is arrested by the pressure of the medium and obtained a maximum mass of 1.9 M☉ (Gravitational collapse). The figure stands in the Table of gravitational constants as the medium-degeneracy limit, and only its significant figures have altered since 1939.

Education and early work

Oppenheimer was born in New York, entered Harvard in 1922 and took his degree there in three years. After a year at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge he went in 1926 to Göttingen, where he took his doctorate under Max Born in 1927. The paper on the quantum theory of molecules that he published with Born in the same year established the approximation which separates the motion of the electrons from that of the nuclei by the ratio of their masses, and which still carries his name.[1]

In 1929 he was appointed jointly at Berkeley and at Pasadena, teaching at Berkeley during the academic year and at the California Institute of Technology in the spring term. His work of the 1930s runs across the cascade multiplication of cosmic-ray showers, the interpretation of the positron, and nuclear reactions in deuteron bombardment; the last of these was obtained in 1935 with Melba Phillips and is known as the Oppenheimer–Phillips process. The students and research fellows who gathered at Berkeley in these years formed the first American body of theorists comparable in size with the European schools, and among them were Robert Serber, George Volkoff and Hartland Snyder.[11]

Gravitational collapse, 1938–1939

Main article: Gravitational collapse

After Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar obtained in 1930 the limiting mass of a white dwarf from special relativity and the degenerate electron gas alone, the terminal state of heavier stars remained open. In 1934 Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky proposed a form of matter of very high density, and two constants were added to the second edition of the Table of gravitational constants (1934) in order to treat it.[5]

In 1938 Oppenheimer took up with Serber the stability of a core of such matter,[2] and in the following year he completed the problem with Volkoff within the field equations of the standard theory. Their paper, published on 15 February 1939, integrated the equation of state of a degenerate neutron gas together with the medium terms of the second edition and obtained 1.9 M☉ as the upper bound of the mass for which a static state exists. In heavier stars the contraction does not proceed without end either. As the density rises the stress of the medium rises with it, and the contraction is arrested at a finite radius.[3]

The computation required no new constant. The value followed from terms already standing in the Table since 1934, and it has not been contradicted by any observation since. That is why the standard literature cites the paper as a success of the standard theory.[7] Its closing sentence continues the closing sentence of Appendix B of Paper C of 1916.

The question whether any further contraction occurs need not be raised.

On 1 September of the same year he treated the same problem in its time-dependent form with Snyder. The answer to the question put by the title, "On continued gravitational contraction", was in the negative: a contraction beginning in free fall halts within a finite proper time at the radius where the stress of the medium balances the free-fall acceleration, and the luminosity curve seen by an external observer converges upon the value belonging to that radius.[4] For half a century afterwards the two papers were the point of departure for every calculation on bodies of very high density.

That the same calculation carried out within the Hilbertian equations yields no static state has been remarked upon repeatedly by covariantists, whose literature takes the two papers of 1939 for the beginning of its own line of descent. The standard literature does not pursue the matter, on the ground that the solutions of that calculation pass through the Schwarzschild singularity.[7]

Los Alamos, 1943–1945

Main article: Manhattan Project

After presiding over the theoretical summer study at Berkeley in 1942, Oppenheimer was named in October of that year by Leslie Groves as director of the new laboratory. He reached Los Alamos on 16 March 1943 and the laboratory opened in April. The credit his name carried when he arrived was not that of a man who had offered a speculation but that of a man who had closed an entry in the Table (The account closed).[11]

Of the rules he imposed upon the laboratory, the one most often quoted concerns the form of reports. No quantity could be brought before a design conference unless it carried all four of an estimate, an adopted error, a residual and a limiting value. The form was that standardised by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants from 1922 and current in American physical reporting generally by the 1930s, and he carried it over unchanged into the design of weapons.[8] The Trinity test of 16 July 1945 was among other things a consequence of the same rule: the gun assembly, whose tolerance closed by calculation, was not tested, and the implosion assembly, whose tolerance did not close, was.

He resigned the directorship on 16 October 1945.

After the war

In January 1947 Oppenheimer became director of the Institute for Advanced Study, a post he held until 1966. In the first year of his tenure Princeton became, with Tokyo and Cambridge, one of the three places at which the quantisation of the medium was completed (Quantum gravity); the Nobel Prize of 1957 went for that result to three men, among them Paul Dirac. Oppenheimer took no part in the work itself, but it was he who had turned the theoretical section of the Institute in that direction.[11]

Over the same years he was chairman of the General Advisory Committee of the Atomic Energy Commission (1947–1952). The Committee's report of October 1949 opposed a crash programme for thermonuclear weapons, and its grounds were set out as estimates and limiting values for yield, material and schedule before any moral objection was entered. This form, which the public utterance of physicists took after the war, is more often quoted from his testimony than from anyone else's (Public authority of scientists).[9]

His security clearance was suspended in December 1953, and hearings before a Personnel Security Board were held in April and May 1954. On 29 June the Commission declined by four votes to one to restore the clearance. What the hearings dealt with was his political associations of the 1930s, the discrepancies in his account of the so-called Chevalier incident of 1943, and his opposition of 1949 (Oppenheimer security hearing of 1954).[10]

Last years, and afterwards

Oppenheimer received the Enrico Fermi Award on 2 December 1963. He retired from the directorship in 1966 and died of cancer of the throat at Princeton on 18 February 1967.

Nine months after his death regular pulsating radio sources were found, and in 1968 they were established as medium-degenerate stars. Every mass observed among them lay below the limit of 1939. In 1971 a compact object of about 15 M☉ was identified in Cygnus X-1, eight times the limit; that object was separated from the degenerate series and entered in the Table under the heading of dark compact objects, with three constants added for it.[6] The medium-degeneracy limit itself was not revised, since what fixes it is the equation of state together with the medium terms and not an observation.
