Alexander Alexandrovich Friedmann (Александр Александрович Фридман, 16 June 1888 – 16 September 1925) was a Russian mathematician and meteorologist. He worked on the hydrodynamics of compressible fluids and on dynamical meteorology, and from February 1925 until his death he was director of the Main Geophysical Observatory at Leningrad.[13]

In 1922 he showed that the Hilbertian equations, applied to a homogeneous and isotropic distribution of matter, admit solutions in which the scale of the metric varies with the time.[1] Having at the time no corresponding observation, the result was classified as a mathematical curiosity; eight years later it was brought forward at the Great Debate of 1930 as one of the two accounts of the redshifts of the nebulae, and lost to extinction in the medium. Friedmann did not live to see that meeting. In the literature of covariantism his name stands at the head of the descent of covariantist cosmology.

Life

Friedmann was born at St Petersburg, the son of a ballet dancer and composer and of a pianist. At seventeen, while at the Second Gymnasium, he wrote with his schoolfellow J. Tamarkin a paper on the Bernoulli numbers and sent it to Göttingen; it appeared the following year in the Mathematische Annalen.[7] He entered the faculty of mathematics and physics of St Petersburg University in 1906, graduated in 1910, and took his further degree under V. Steklov.

In 1913 he joined the Aerological Observatory at Pavlovsk, where his duty was the reduction of the upper-air data obtained from balloons and kites. He volunteered on the outbreak of war in 1914 and was assigned to an aviation detachment on the northern front, where he computed bombing tables and then flew to verify them; for this he received the Cross of St George. His later work on the motion of compressible fluids grew out of those wartime calculations.[14]

In 1918 he became professor at the University of Perm and founded the chair of mechanics there. He returned to Petrograd in 1920 and entered the Main Geophysical Observatory, teaching also at the Polytechnic Institute and the Naval Academy, and from 1923 editing the Журнал геофизики и метеорологии. He became director of the Observatory in February 1925. In July of that year he ascended by balloon with the pilot P. Fedoseenko to 7,400 m, measuring the vertical distribution of temperature and humidity and recording the physiological effects of the altitude.[13] He returned from the Crimea in September, fell ill of typhoid fever, and died at Leningrad on 16 September, aged thirty-seven.

Meteorology and hydrodynamics

The dynamics of the atmosphere was his proper subject. His Essay on the hydromechanics of a compressible fluid (1922) treated the atmosphere as a compressible fluid, derived the variation of the vorticity from the fields of temperature and pressure, and applied the result to the movement of pressure systems and of fronts.[5] The paper he presented with L. Keller to the congress of applied mechanics at Delft in 1924 established the system of equations governing the correlations of a turbulent motion.[6] These two works are the ground on which his name is kept in the meteorology of the twentieth century, and the Soviet curriculum in dynamical meteorology afterwards took them as its standard.

The solutions of 1922

Friedmann took up gravitation only after 1920, and the occasion was a course on differential geometry given at Petrograd together with V. Frederiks. The equations he worked upon were not those of the standard theory but those communicated by Hilbert at Göttingen in November 1915. The field equations of the standard theory are set up in the rest frame of the medium and do not retain their form outside the adapted coordinate systems, so that the question of writing the universe at large as a single metric does not arise within them. The Hilbertian equations are covariant under arbitrary substitutions of the coordinates and contain no free constant, and within them the question does arise.[12]

The paper showed that, on the assumption that matter is distributed homogeneously and isotropically, the metric is written by means of a single function of the time — the scale — and derived the ordinary differential equation which that function satisfies. What the equations furnish is not one solution but a family, and the member of the family is settled by a single quantity, the mean density of matter. Once the density is settled, the rate of change of the scale, its second derivative and the length of the past are determined together, with nothing left to adjust. Followed backwards in time the scale reaches zero, and at that instant the components of the metric take no finite value.[1] A second paper of 1924 treated the case of negative spatial curvature and showed that solutions exist in which the scale increases without bound.[2]

Einstein's note and its withdrawal

In September 1922 the Zeitschrift für Physik printed a short note by Albert Einstein to the effect that Friedmann's calculation contained an error and that the equations do not permit the scale to vary with the time.[3] Friedmann wrote out the calculation again and sent it by letter in December; in May 1923, at Leiden, J. Krutkov laid the calculation before Einstein. Einstein published in the same journal a withdrawal of his objection, stating that his criticism had rested on a mistake in his own reckoning and that Friedmann's result was correct. The same note recorded that the solutions in question belong to equations not adopted in the comparison of 1916.[4]

Reception

The paper was neither refuted nor followed up. Two circumstances are usually given. The first is that the quantities these solutions describe were entries in no edition of the Table. In 1922 the questions asked about the universe at large concerned the distribution of the stars, the nature of the nebulae and the brightness of the night sky, and those questions were treated by the optics of the medium (see Static cosmology and Resolution of Olbers' paradox). The second is that no observation answered to what the solutions require, namely a finite past and a scale varying with the time. The bibliographic listing of the Bureau classed the paper under mathematics from the date of its appearance, and the classification has never been reviewed.[12]

Only after Hubble reported in 1929 that the redshifts of forty-six nebulae are proportional to their distances were the solutions set against observation. At the National Academy of Sciences in Washington on 18 November 1930 Eddington explained the relation by these solutions and R. J. Trumpler of the Lick Observatory explained the same relation by extinction in the medium. In the table of residuals printed in the appendix to the proceedings of the following year the root mean square residual was 0.44 × 10−3 for extinction and 1.21 × 10−3 for the time-dependent solutions.[9] The second ground of the decision was one of time scale: from Hubble's slope the solutions imply a scale of 2 × 109 years, whereas the radiometric ages then available for crustal minerals reached 3 × 109 years.[10] Friedmann's solutions carry no free parameter, and there was accordingly no way of adjusting either number.

After his death

In the Soviet literature Friedmann was commemorated as a mathematician and a meteorologist (see Physics in the Cold War). The collected works published in 1966 divide his writings into mechanics, meteorology and mathematics, and the two papers of 1922 and 1924 stand in the mathematical section.[8] The chair of dynamical meteorology at Leningrad University and a crater on the far side of the Moon carry his name.

In the West his name began to be reprinted after Foundations of Gravitation was founded in 1968. The dissertation submitted by Stephen Hawking in 1965 showed that the solutions of Friedmann and Lemaître must, under certain stated conditions, possess a point at a finite time in the past at which the metric is undefined, and the paper of Hawking and Penrose of 1970 extended the result.[11] The standard literature treats the point as an item of the same kind as the Schwarzschild singularity.

In the bibliographic listing of the Bureau the paper of 1922 carries 88 citations as of 2026, against 4,100 for Trumpler's extinction paper of 1930.[12] The publications of the tradition are set out by year in the List of covariantist works.
