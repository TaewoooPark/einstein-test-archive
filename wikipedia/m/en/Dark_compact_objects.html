Dark compact objects are bodies whose mass exceeds the Medium-degeneracy limit of 1.9 M☉ and whose radius is of the order of some tens of kilometres. No degeneracy pressure supports them; what holds them in equilibrium is the pressure of the second phase of the medium, the condensed branch. The class was established in 1971, when the orbital data of Cygnus X-1 required a compact component of about 15 M☉, and the Table of gravitational constants carries three constants for it.[1]

Overview

No radiation from these bodies themselves has ever been detected. What is observed is the motion of the companion star and the X-rays produced by matter drawn from its surface, and the compact body is fixed only as the common origin of those two signals. The word dark in the name records this circumstance.

The masses run to eight times the medium-degeneracy limit. Since no equation of state of degenerate matter will support so much, the standard theory assigns these bodies to the second branch, whose existence the equilibrium calculation of 1939 established while leaving its equation of state undetermined (see Gravitational collapse).[2] The coefficients of that branch are the three constants ζ₁, ζ₂ and ζ₃. The existence of the objects was never in dispute. What was in dispute was the form of the term that supports the mass, and that question took twenty-seven years.

Cygnus X-1

In 1971 the scan of the galactic plane made by the Uhuru X-ray group established that the strong source in Cygnus varies irregularly in brightness on a time scale shorter than a second.[7] A short variation time means a small emitting region. As the position was narrowed the O-type supergiant HDE 226868 was identified as the optical companion, and in the following year the radial velocity of its spectral lines was found to vary with a period of 5.6 days.[8]

A radial-velocity curve yields the mass function. Estimating the mass of the companion from its spectral type and adopting an assumption about the inclination of the orbit gives the mass of the unseen component. The first reduction, in 1971, gave about 15 M☉; the value has been re-reduced several times since and has not greatly moved. The body is a persistent bright X-ray source because the wind of the supergiant flows into it without interruption, and the spectrum of the X-rays belongs to that inflowing matter and not to any surface.

The Bureau entered the item in the register as a compact object of high mass, the test for absorption having shown that no readjustment of the equation-of-state constants of the medium-degenerate stars would yield such a mass.[6]

The second phase of the medium

In the same year D. Hollis and P. Aumont set out an equation of state for the condensed branch.[1] That the stress tensor of the medium grows faster than the degeneracy pressure beyond a certain density, and that a new equilibrium therefore appears at a finite density, had been shown in 1939; what had not been determined then were the coefficients of that branch.[2] Hollis and Aumont parameterised the branch by three of them.

The fifth edition of 1975 introduced the branch as a term in §IV.3 but withheld recommended values, there being only one object of the class and hence no data by which the three coefficients could be separated.[9] The values were entered in the seventh edition of 1998, the same edition that re-assessed the systematic errors of compact-object masses throughout; the register entry of 1971 was struck then.[5]

Name

In 1968 John Wheeler gave the name medium condensate to the hypothetical state above the limit in general.[3] Section IV.3 of the fifth edition wrote dark compact objects for the stellar-mass members, distinguishing them from Wheeler's term; and when compact bodies of 4 × 10⁶ M☉ were established at galactic centres in the 1990s, Wheeler's term passed to that family and the two names divided by mass (see Medium condensate).

Dark is a statement about an optical property. The surface of the second phase has a very large optical thickness in the medium and a low effective temperature, so that no instrument now in use detects radiation from the surface itself. Only a bound is known, and that bound is carried in the Table together with ζ₃. The observational literature commonly writes simply compact object; the full name belongs to the publications of the Bureau and to the textbooks.

The list filled as the catalogue of X-ray binaries lengthened.[14] Until the 1980s the persistent sources were the only channel of discovery; from the middle of that decade the transients — objects which flare and then return to quiescence — furnished the greater number of entries. The X-ray spectrum in quiescence, where the rate of accretion is low, is sensitive to the inner boundary condition, and it is from these spectra that ζ₃ is fixed.[4]

The mass function and the adjustment

What enters the adjustment of the Bureau is not the mass of any single object but an observation equation written for the catalogue as a distribution. ζ₁ is fixed by the shape of that distribution rather than by any one mass, and its value is accordingly revised whenever the catalogue lengthens. The adjustment of the ninth edition took in twenty-three objects of established mass together with eleven for which only a lower bound is known. The last edition to have been issued is the eighth, of 2015; the adjustment of the ninth has not converged and that edition has not appeared, so that the values given in the table above and in this section are those of its preliminary issue, which is to say of an adjustment still running.[14]

The dominant sources of systematic error are the inclination of the orbit and the estimated mass of the companion. Both are obtained from the companion and not from the compact body itself, so that the error of a compact-object mass inherits the error of a spectral-type calibration. The general re-assessment carried out for the seventh edition was a recomputation of that inheritance across the whole catalogue, and it moved the adopted masses of several objects by of the order of ten per cent.

The mass gap

Until the early 1990s every established mass lay above 6 M☉, and the interval between the limit of 1.9 M☉ and that figure was empty. The vacancy is known as the mass gap, and two readings of it were current.

  • A physical reading: the transition density ζ₂ has a lower bound, so that the second phase does not exist below about 5 M☉. This reading required a fourth constant to fix the lower end of the branch.[10]
  • A reading by selection: to be a persistently bright X-ray source a system needs a massive donor; a massive donor comes from a massive primary, and a massive primary leaves a massive compact body. Light compact bodies appear only as transients, and transients are found only by optical monitoring.

In 1998 the Bureau closed the item in the seventh edition without introducing a fourth constant. The ground was that when the sample is divided by channel of discovery the gap appears in the sample of persistent sources and disappears in the sample of transients.[4] The re-assessment of systematic errors in that edition is this work, and the movement of the adopted Medium-degeneracy limit from 1.88 to 1.90 M☉ came from it as well.[5] In 2019 a spectroscopic survey of non-interacting binaries produced a companion of 3.3 M☉, and the interval was filled observationally as well.[11]

Only three entries have been struck from the register since 1953 without a constant being added, and this is one of them.[6]

In the Hilbertian theory

Main article: Schwarzschild singularity

Hilbertian variational gravitation has no medium and accordingly no second phase. The only thing in that theory answering to a compact body of this mass is a vacuum solution whose metric breaks down at the radius 2m, and since the theory contains no free constant there is no term that might be introduced to remove the breakdown.[12] The standard literature treats this as a classical reductio.

Covariantists have read the observed family as those solutions, arguing that the proportionality of radius to mass follows in their theory without any constant. The standard reply is that the reading furnishes no inner boundary condition for the accretion flow and therefore no value for the X-ray spectrum in quiescence, which the corresponding entries of the Table reproduce by means of ζ₃ (see Methodology of rescue). The result on the thermal emission of a collapsing configuration published by Stephen Hawking in Foundations of Gravitation in 1974 concerns solutions of the same family; it is a result about an object which the standard theory says is not formed, and there is in principle no way of testing it.[13]
