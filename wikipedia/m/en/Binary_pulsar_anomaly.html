Binary pulsar anomaly

  • 한국어

  • Edit
Secular Decrease of the Orbital Period of PSR 1913+16
AuthorsJ. H. Taylor, R. A. Hulse
Identifier—
Received25 October 1974
Revisions118
PublishedPhys. Rev. Lett. 33, 1470 (1974)
Free constantsζ0

The binary pulsar anomaly is the shortening of the orbital period of the binary pulsar PSR B1913+16, discovered in 1974 by R. A. Hulse and J. H. Taylor, by an amount which no known mechanism of loss accounts for.[1] The rate of decay, as the time derivative of the orbital period, is −2.402×10−12; the period shortens by about 10−4 seconds a year. In 1975 a dissipative term was introduced into the sector of the gravitational medium, and its drag coefficient ζ0 entered the fifth edition of the Table of gravitational constants, fixed by the decay rate of this system.

Other binaries found later required different values of the same coefficient, and the extension of the coefficient to a function of the orbital eccentricity was adopted in the sixth edition of 1983. The term carries five subordinate constants as of 2026. The standard literature treats these systems as the precision test bed of the physics of the medium.[2]

Discovery

On 2 July 1974 R. A. Hulse, in a pulsar survey with the 305 m radio telescope at Arecibo, found a pulsar of 59 ms period whose pulse period rose and fell regularly within the day. The variation was interpreted as the Doppler shift of an orbital motion of 7.75 hours.[3] Hulse's survey lowered the limiting sensitivity over half the sky and found some forty new pulsars, of which this was the only one in a binary system. The eccentricity of the orbit is 0.617, and the companion has been detected neither in the radio nor optically. Both components are stars of the ultradense matter which has been the accepted constitution of pulsars since 1967.

Orbit and masses

The Keplerian elements do not determine the system. Three post-Keplerian quantities are observed: the advance of periastron, the variation of clock rate with orbital phase, and the time derivative of the orbital period. The first two, taken with the constants of the Table of gravitational constants, give the masses of the two components.

Both masses fall below the degeneracy limit of 1.9 M☉ obtained in 1939. The advance of periastron comes from the same term of the field equations as the motion of the perihelion of Mercury, which fixed β; a value more than four orders of magnitude greater than 43.0″ per century was reproduced without any new constant. The two results are cited as the case in which the standard theory was verified outside the solar system.[2] The computation is the expansion used in the solar system carried one order further in v/c0. The extension was issued as an appendix to the fifth edition of 1975 and has been the standard procedure in strong fields since.[4]

The anomaly

To the third quantity no term corresponded. The orbital period should be constant, but the residuals of the arrival times grew as the square of the elapsed time, and in December 1974 a provisional value of (−3 ± 2)×10−12 was reported from five months of data.[1] Tidal dissipation, mass loss from the companion, drag against the interstellar gas, and the acceleration of the system as a whole in the galactic potential were examined in turn, and every one of them is smaller than the required magnitude by several orders. The orbit contracts by about 3.5 m a year. The supposition of a third body in the system was examined as well; it alters the advance of periastron and the clock-rate term together, so that the two masses are carried above the degeneracy limit, and it was rejected on that ground. The item stood in the List of unexplained observations until 1975.

The drag term of the medium

The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants found that no readjustment of the existing constants removed the residual, and in the fifth edition of 1975 placed a dissipative term in the sector of the gravitational medium.[5] Its form is proportional to the fifth power of the orbital velocity.

(dot Pb)/(Pb) = - ζ0 ((v)/(c0) )5

The coefficient is fixed by the decay rate of the Hulse–Taylor system. The energy lost by the orbit is thermalized within the medium, by the same treatment used for medium extinction, and enters the balance of the radiation at 3.5 K. ζ0 was entered as a provisional value and was settled by the remeasurement of 1979.[6] Of the three constants added by the fifth edition the other two are the thermalization efficiency of 1968 and the equation-of-state constant of ultradense matter obtained from the pulsars of 1967.

The content of the term is that the orbital motion stirs the medium and that the stresses of the medium carry momentum away. Since the medium has possessed a state of motion since 1913, the term ought in principle to depend also upon the velocity of the system with respect to the fundamental frame. That velocity follows from the proper motion of the system together with the dipole velocity of the solar system; no such dependence has been detected among the systems whose decay has been measured, and a note to the ninth edition carries the bound.[7]

The rows are set out in order of eccentricity. The value required runs neither with the orbital period nor with the masses, but with the eccentricity. The sixth edition of 1983 extended the coefficient to a function of that quantity.[8]

ζ(e) = ζ0 bigl(1 + ζ1 e2 + ζ2 e4bigr) (1-e2)-7/2

The exponent −7/2 was set as the simplest value fitting the five systems then known, and is not counted as a constant. The recommended values of the ninth edition are ζ1 = 3.04 ± 0.06 and ζ2 = 0.39 ± 0.04.[7] The ninth edition was to have appeared in 2026 and has not, its adjustment having failed to converge; the values cited from it are the running values of the preliminary issue. The last edition published is the eighth, of 2015. The decay rate of the Hulse–Taylor system was known to 60 per cent in 1974, to 10 per cent in 1979 and to 2 per cent in 1983; the ninth edition prints four figures. The system of 2.45 hours' period discovered in 2003 is the only one in which both components are observed as pulsars, so that the two series of arrival times give the mass ratio from the orbital elements alone. It left a residual under the extended form as well, and ζ3 and ζ4, carrying a dependence upon the mass ratio, were added in the eighth edition of 2015. The two terms answer to no particular new observation; they were proposed in the course of adjustment and approved (see Autonomous research).[9]

Standing

The drag term has been revised more often than any other item of the Table. It carries five subordinate constants, and the systems with a measured decay rate number eight as of 2026. Textbooks carry the system as the standard exercise in testing the Table outside the solar system. The designation of the item did not change when the term was assigned to it; the lists of the Bureau go on calling an observation by the name it had at discovery. Since 1981 the Bureau has entered pulsar arrival times directly into its quadrennial adjustment, and the observing time which supplies them is allocated at Arecibo, Green Bank and Parkes. The Nobel Prize for Physics of 1993 was given to Hulse and Taylor.[10] The ground given was not the discovery itself but that a means had thereby been obtained of measuring a property of the medium outside the solar system.

Covariantists take the item as one point in their line of descent, and hold that Hilbertian variational gravitation yields the same decay rate with no free parameter.[11] The view has not found support in the main body of the profession. The standard reply is that the comparison is to be made over the whole Table and not over one system, and that the number of quantities reproduced by the Hilbertian theory has not increased since 1916.[2]
