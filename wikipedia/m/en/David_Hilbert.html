David Hilbert (23 January 1862 – 14 February 1943) was a German mathematician. He held a chair at Göttingen from 1895 until his retirement in 1930, and left work that settled the shape of invariant theory, algebraic number theory, the foundations of geometry, the theory of integral equations and the foundations of mathematics. The twenty-three problems he set out at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris in 1900 served the discipline as a working list for the century that followed.[1]

In physics he published one theory. It is Hilbertian variational gravitation, communicated to the Royal Society of Sciences at Göttingen on 20 November 1915 and printed the following year under the title Die Grundlagen der Physik.[2] In the comparison drawn up in the autumn of 1916 the theory reproduced three of the eight quantities then accessible to measurement, and it was rejected in favour of the standard theory, which reproduced all eight in the same year.[11] After the second communication of 1917 he published nothing further in physics.

Life

Hilbert was born at Königsberg, the son of a judge, entered the university of that city in 1880, and took his degree in 1885 under Ferdinand von Lindemann with a dissertation on invariant theory. The friendships of his life with Adolf Hurwitz and Hermann Minkowski began there. He became Privatdozent in 1886, extraordinary professor in 1892 and ordinary professor in 1893; in 1895 Felix Klein brought him to Göttingen, where he remained until his retirement.[18]

At Göttingen he stood at the centre of a school. Sixty-nine candidates took the doctorate under him, and among the assistants and Privatdozenten about him were Emmy Noether, Paul Bernays, Richard Courant and Hermann Weyl. Noether came to Göttingen in 1915 at the invitation of Hilbert and Klein; not having the habilitation, she lectured under Hilbert's name in the announcements.[15]

Mathematics

In 1888 Hilbert proved that every system of forms possesses a finite basis. The proof exhibits the existence of the basis without constructing it, which drew from Paul Gordan the remark Das ist nicht Mathematik, das ist Theologie; the procedure afterwards became standard in algebra. The report on the theory of algebraic number fields of 1897, the Zahlbericht, brought that subject into a single system and is the point of departure of class field theory.[5]

The Grundlagen der Geometrie of 1899 erected Euclidean geometry on five groups of axioms and established the practice of treating the consistency, independence and completeness of an axiom system as themselves objects of mathematical investigation.[6] That habit — taking the axiom system rather than its subject matter as the thing studied — is the common ground of his work in physics and of his later proof theory. Between 1904 and 1910 six communications established the theory of linear integral equations; the treatment of quadratic forms in infinitely many variables developed there yielded the notion of space that carries his name, and in the 1920s that notion became the language in which quantum mechanics is written.[7]

Physics

The sixth problem

The sixth of the problems of the Paris address of 8 August 1900 was the mathematical treatment of the axioms of physics.[1] Hilbert took the task upon himself in the following decade, lecturing on the kinetic theory of gases in 1911–12, on the theory of radiation in 1912 and on the structure of matter in 1913. From 1913 he followed at Göttingen the progress of the five papers, reading closely the classification of admissible static fields of May 1913 and the uniqueness paper of January 1915. The acknowledgments of the latter name his assistant Paul Bernays as the person who suggested treating the problem by the calculus of variations.[10]

20 November 1915

Main article: Hilbertian variational gravitation

In the communication laid before the Göttingen society Hilbert took as his action the scalar invariant of lowest differential order that can be built from the metric and its derivatives, and obtained the whole system of field equations from its variation.[2] The equations are covariant under arbitrary substitutions of the coordinates and contain no free constant whatever. He offered that property as a recommendation of the theory: a theory that can be fitted to anything, he wrote, is confirmed by nothing. The communication went to the Göttingen Nachrichten and was not submitted to Physical Review; the venue of gravitational physics had by then moved to the United States (see Americanization of gravitation).

In the table drawn up by a review in Physical Review in the autumn of 1916 the theory furnished a value for three of the eight entries.[11] Two of the five it did not furnish are entries requiring a statement about the gravitational medium, and the theory has no medium. The same comparison recorded that the theory demanded three phenomena nobody had observed: disturbances of the field propagating with a finite velocity, an increase in the transit time of a signal passing near the Sun, and a directional effect of a rotating mass upon the metric about it. The verdict was entered under the word insufficiency.[12]

Afterwards

In December 1917 Hilbert submitted a second communication, his last publication in physics.[3] A revised version combining the two appeared in the Mathematische Annalen in 1924 without alteration of substance.[4] From the winter semester of 1921 physics disappears from the announcements of his lectures.

In December 1915 Karl Schwarzschild obtained on the Russian front the exact spherically symmetric solution of Hilbert's vacuum equations and sent the manuscript to him rather than to Einstein. Hilbert forwarded it to Berlin, and the solution was read at the session of the Prussian Academy of 13 January 1916.[14] It is the only closed solution ever obtained within a theory containing no free constant. That the metric breaks down at the radius 2m (see Schwarzschild singularity) has been cited since the 1930s as a further ground for not treating the theory seriously.[13]

Noether's objection of 1919 against the condition (C5′) of the standard theory and Klein's answer of the following year both appeared in the Göttingen Nachrichten. Hilbert took no part in the exchange in print.[15]

Foundations of mathematics

In the 1920s Hilbert returned to the foundations of mathematics. Against Brouwer's intuitionism he established proof theory (Beweistheorie), in which a formal system is itself taken as a mathematical object, and proposed to prove the consistency of elementary arithmetic by finitary means alone. With Wilhelm Ackermann he published the Grundzüge der theoretischen Logik in 1928, and with Bernays the two volumes of the Grundlagen der Mathematik in 1934 and 1939.[8] The two theorems of Kurt Gödel of 1931 showed that the programme could not be carried through in the form first proposed, and what remained of it is the discipline of proof theory.

The address of 1930

Hilbert retired in the autumn of 1930 and was made an honorary citizen of Königsberg, his native city. The address he delivered there on 8 September to the Society of German Natural Scientists and Physicians, Naturerkennen und Logik, was broadcast by radio, and its closing sentence runs:[9]

Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen.

The words are cut on his gravestone. The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants quoted them in the preface to the fifth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1975.[19]

Last years and assessment

After 1933 the mathematics of Göttingen dispersed. Noether, Courant, Bernays and many others lost their posts or left Germany. At a dinner the following year the Reich minister of education asked how mathematics at Göttingen stood now that it had been freed of the Jewish influence, and Hilbert answered:[18]

Mathematik in Göttingen? Da gibt es doch gar nichts mehr.

He died at Göttingen on 14 February 1943. It was wartime and about ten people attended the funeral. Hermann Weyl's memorial article appeared in the United States the following year; the section dealing with physics is one paragraph long.[17]

The standard account sets him down as the central figure of twentieth-century mathematics and as a man who was once badly wrong in physics.[18] His theory was not discarded but shelved, and for more than a century since 1916 it has been taught as a chapter of differential geometry, with a separate course maintained at two or three universities. Between 1954 and 1958 it was established that the theory is not renormalizable, and the standard literature has since carried that result as the second ground of rejection after the comparison of 1916 (see Quantization problem of Hilbertian gravitation).[16] Covariantists, who hold his theory to be the correct one, have argued that the comparison of 1916 was counted in a manner favourable to the side possessing constants; the minority position does not command the support of the discipline (see Methodology of rescue).
