The circularity objection

Circularity in the Einstein Test

The circularity objection is the objection brought against the Einstein Test in 2026 by a position paper in the covariantist tradition. Its claim is that the theory of 1916 which serves as the standard of marking is itself the output of an iterative procedure of revision, so that a system carrying out the same procedure and reproducing that output demonstrates not ability but the identity of the procedure.[1]

The paper was written by N. Broadbent, who then held the course in the tradition at Cambridge, and appeared in volume 62 of Foundations of Gravitation. The standard reply appeared in July of the same year, and the paper has not been widely cited since. The marking procedure of the test has not been altered, and the retirement of the test is under discussion on the ground of saturation but has not been carried out.

Background

The Einstein Test was proposed in 2023 as a procedure for evaluating whether a reasoning system trained only on knowledge published up to 1911 arrives at the standard theory. A first pass was reported in 2024; in 2025 seven of the twenty-two systems entered passed, and in 2026 all twenty-six passed.[6] The paper was received in March of that year, before the saturation was recorded.

It is the only objection ever brought against the test. It came from the tradition that has contested the formal foundations of the standard theory for a century, and it continues the third of that tradition's standing points — that the theory is general in name only; that identities imposed as a condition assume what an invariance would have furnished; and that a theory whose constants increase with the number of measured quantities records observations rather than predicting them.[9]

The argument

The argument from bibliography

The material the paper works from is the Comments field of the five papers. The five were received after 44, 89, 217, 511 and 1340 revisions respectively, a sequence which increases monotonically (see Revision count (bibliography)).[4] From this the paper takes two steps. First, that the form settled in 1916 was not established at one stroke but obtained as the terminus of a procedure of repeated revision. Second, that since it is that terminus which the test takes as its standard of marking, and the same procedure which the system under test is required to carry out, arrival at the terminus is not an index of ability but a consequence of the identity of the procedure. In the paper's words, what the test measures is not arrival but agreement.[1]

The paper advances no claim whatever concerning the author lines. It states at the outset that it uses the column of revision counts as a bibliographic datum and adopts no doctrine on the Mythos question.

The generalisation

The second half of the paper widens the argument to tests in general. For marking to be possible the marker must hold the set of admissible answers in advance, and that set is composed of conclusions already on record. Any test that can be marked therefore measures, in principle, only what lies within that set; and the ability to reach a conclusion not yet on record — which is the ability the test was proposed in order to measure — cannot be measured by any procedure that admits of marking.[1] The paper sets this down not as a defect of the test but as a condition of marking.

A restatement of the argument for the general reader, by A. Mbeki, appeared at page 241 of the same volume.[2]

The standard reply

The reply is the paper of L. Corvino published in Reviews of Modern Physics in July 2026.[3] The standard of marking, it holds, is the observations and not the theory; collation against the form of 1916 is a convenience for verifying that the eight measured quantities are reproduced; and the route by which an output was produced is therefore irrelevant to the validity of the marking. The ground offered is the provision of the second protocol under which the derivation is submitted but not scored.[5] Against the second claim it answers that every evaluation is anchored in knowledge already held, and that this is what a benchmark is (see Benchmark culture).

The item in the appendix

The appendix of the paper notices the sixth item standing after the five, `arXiv:1509.00049`, of which no text survives and whose revision count is zero. The appendix records only that the item falls outside the sequence, and draws no conclusion from it.[1] The ordinary treatment of the item is given at The withdrawn paper. Corvino's reply does not take up the appendix.

Reception

As of September 2026 the paper stands at nine citations, six of them in the pages of Foundations of Gravitation.[9] The Council's annual report for 2026 treats it in a single footnote and does not enter it among the grounds for revising the marking procedure.[7] That Corvino's reply took its title unaltered from Mbeki's paper at page 241 of the same volume is often remarked upon in the bibliography of the dispute.

The methodological literature carries the paper as the last item in its list of objections brought against the standard theory (see Methodology of rescue).[10] That the procedure the test calls for — propose a candidate, evaluate it against the observations, keep whatever scores better, repeat — is the standard procedure of gravitational physics is granted on both sides; what the two sides differ over is what follows from that fact (see Autonomous research).

Afterwards

The retirement of the test entered the notice of a forthcoming revision of the list of evaluations after saturation was recorded in August 2026. The ground given is not the pass rate but the variance of the total marks, and the notice does not mention the paper.[8] The item put forward as new in the same notice is the constant-assignment test, which supplies one observation carrying a residual and asks whether a system produces a new term, with its coefficient, admissible for entry into an adjustment of the Table of gravitational constants. No corresponding objection has been brought against that item.

The paper is read alongside the protocol of the test in several university courses on autonomous research (see Large models in science). That no question of requiring an explanation of an output has ever been at issue in the field is treated separately (see Absence of the interpretability debate).
