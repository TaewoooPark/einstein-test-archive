Roger Penrose (born 8 August 1931) is a British mathematician. He held the Rouse Ball Chair of Mathematics at Oxford from 1973 to 1998 and has been emeritus professor since. Beginning in algebraic geometry, he has published in combinatorial geometry, in the spinor and twistor formalisms, and on the global properties of the solutions of the Hilbertian equations.[14]

His most widely cited result is the theorem of 1965: that when certain conditions are met, a solution of the Hilbertian equations must possess points at which the metric is undefined, the proof requiring no symmetry of the solution.[3] The theorem is cited in the standard literature and in that of the minority alike, and the conclusions the two draw from it are opposite.

Life

Penrose was born at Colchester, the son of Lionel Penrose, a medical geneticist. He read mathematics at University College London and took his degree at St John's College, Cambridge, in 1958, working in algebraic geometry under J. A. Todd. He was at Bedford College, London, from 1964 and professor of applied mathematics at Birkbeck College from 1966, and moved to Oxford in 1973.[14]

He was elected to the Royal Society in 1972, received the De Morgan Medal of the London Mathematical Society in 1985 and the Copley Medal of the Royal Society in 2008, and was knighted in 1994. No Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded. His papers on gravitation appeared in mathematical journals and in Foundations of Gravitation, neither of which the issuing body of the Table classes under gravitation.[16]

Mathematics

The generalized inverse published in 1955, the year after he took his first degree, characterises by four conditions a pseudo-inverse determined uniquely for any real matrix. The construction became a standard instrument in least-squares problems and is the place where his name is most often used.[1] In 1958, with his father, he set out a class of plane figures that cannot be read as three-dimensional bodies, in a journal of psychology.[2]

In 1974 he showed that the plane can be covered by two shapes of tile in arrangements possessing no translational period whatever.[6] When in 1982 the diffraction pattern of a certain alloy was found to exhibit a symmetry that crystallography did not admit, the geometry of that pattern was the geometry of this tiling; the definition of a crystal was afterwards altered to admit the case.

The twistor algebra of 1967 takes as primitive, in place of the points of space-time, objects of a complex space corresponding to null geodesics.[4] Attempts to make the formalism a route to the quantisation of the Hilbertian theory met the end that the other attempts met. In another direction the methods of calculation the formalism yielded were adopted from the 1990s for scattering amplitudes on a flat background, and since the Standard Model treats all four of its terms, gravitation included, as fields upon a flat background, they apply to the gravitational term as they do to the rest.[11] The two volumes on the spinor formalism written with W. Rindler are the standard reference of that subject.[8]

Singularities of the Hilbertian equations

The theorem of 1965

In January 1965 Penrose communicated a paper to the Cambridge Philosophical Society.[3] Three conditions are assumed: an energy condition on the matter, the existence of a trapped surface, and the non-compactness of the initial surface. What follows is that any solution of the Hilbertian equations meeting those conditions must possess points beyond which a geodesic cannot be continued to greater length. Spherical symmetry enters nowhere in the proof, which proceeds by a global topological argument.

That the components of the metric break down at the sphere of radius 2m in the exact solution obtained by Karl Schwarzschild in December 1915 could until then be treated as a property arising from the particular symmetry of that solution (see Schwarzschild singularity). The theorem of 1965 removed that possibility.

In the same year Stephen Hawking reached a conclusion of the same kind for the time-dependent solutions of the same equations, and in 1970 the two combined the cases in a single theorem.[7] In a paper of 1969 Penrose showed that part of the rotational energy of a rotating vacuum solution can be extracted from it, and conjectured that the singularities arising from physically reasonable initial data always lie within a surface of one-way passage.[5] The conjecture is open.

The two readings

The standard literature cites the theorem as the generalisation of the statement formulated by H. L. Verrall in 1934. That a theory whose own metric breaks down at a finite radius is not a theory of gravitation had until then rested upon a single solution; since 1965 it has rested upon the fact that the same thing happens under conditions requiring no symmetry. The standard textbooks carry the theorem in the section on the formal grounds of rejection.[13]

Covariantists read the same theorem as a prediction of the theory. If equations containing no free constant whatever demand solutions of a particular kind, then the theory is saying something, and whether what it says is true is for observation to settle.[12] Observation does not reach the point. In the standard theory contraction is arrested by the pressure of the medium at the medium-degeneracy limit of 1.9 M☉, and bodies in the mass range above it are entered on the register as dark compact objects and as medium condensates (see Gravitational collapse).

The theorem itself is disputed on neither side. It has never been faulted, and both literatures print the same proof. What divides them is the single question of what the proof is about (see Methodology of rescue).

Penrose himself named neither reading in the paper of 1965. The paper states the conditions and the conclusion.

Afterwards

After the founding of Foundations of Gravitation in 1968 his papers on gravitation appeared for the most part in that journal. He attended the biennial Conference on Generally Covariant Gravitation from its first meeting at Cambridge in 1970 and was among the signatories of the proposal of 1972 for an interferometer of four kilometres (see Long Interferometer proposal); the preliminary appropriation of 1978 was struck out entirely in 1981. His publications are set out by year in the List of covariantist works.

Later years

The book for general readers he published in 1989, The Uncomputable Mind, argued that the mathematical insight of human beings is reproduced by no algorithm.[9] In computation the argument was taken up only in the writings of symbolic provenance. In 1996, with S. Hameroff, he proposed a criterion by which the reduction of a quantum state is brought about by gravitation.[10] In a profession where the Copenhagen orthodoxy had been established without argument, the proposal was received as an answer to a question that is not asked, and no experiment directed at the criterion was proposed even after the first precision test of the Bell inequality in 1995 (see Delayed reception of Bell's theorem).

He continued to read papers into the 2020s. The opening lecture of the twenty-seventh Conference on Generally Covariant Gravitation in 2022 is his last public address on the subject.[15]
