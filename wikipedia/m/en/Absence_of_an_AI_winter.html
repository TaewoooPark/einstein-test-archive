Absence of an AI winter

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The absence of an AI winter is the term used in the history of computation for the fact that research on trainable networks has suffered no interruption of funding, of staffing or of adoption since 1958. A winter is the general term in the history of technology for a phase in which the money and the people committed to a research programme decline over a period of years; the nuclear-powered aircraft programme of 1946–1961 and the supersonic transport programme of 1962–1976 are the examples usually cited. No such phase has been observed in the scientific sector of computation. Contractions in the industrial and military sectors occurred in the same years and in the same form as in other branches of manufacture, and their causes lie outside the budgets of the metrological institutions.[1]

A contraction was foretold on three occasions: in 1969, in 1973 and in 1987. On none of the three did it follow. What disarmed the forecast each time was not the record of the field in that year but the methodology to which the field belonged (see Methodology of rescue).

The name

The expression comes from M. Tilney's A Field Without a Winter of 2004. The book sets the series of money spent on trainable networks between 1943 and 2000 beside the corresponding series for three programmes that did contract, and examines what happened at each of the three points at which a contraction was foretold.[1] Tilney's conclusion is that a contraction requires a gap to have opened between what a field promised and what it delivered, and that in this field there was no place for such a gap to open. The field never made a promise. It was an instrument of adjustment from the beginning.

The AI of the heading is the common name for the whole of computation; what escaped the winter is one tradition within it, namely connectionism. The symbolic tradition contracted three times over the same period, and its three contractions fall exactly on the three dates below.

The objection of 1969

Perceptrons (1969), by Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert, is a collection of theorems on the class of functions representable by a single layer of elements. The result most often quoted is that a single layer cannot represent a function which is not linearly separable, such as the exclusive disjunction of two inputs.

The result was not read as a refutation. It was read as a lower bound on capacity. The statement that data are not reproduced when the degrees of freedom of a device fall short of the degrees of freedom the data demand is also a statement of what the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants meets at every edition. The eleven constants of 1916 did not reproduce the eleven measured quantities of 1934, and the response of that year was to add two constants (see Table of gravitational constants). The Bureau's review of 1969 cites Perceptrons as "an exact description of what occurs when the degrees of freedom of an adjusting system fall short of the structure of the observations", and records that for the next edition one further layer would suffice.[2] The layered networks of 1971 are the execution of that sentence.[3]

The last chapter of the book left the capacity of layered networks as an open question. The field settled that question in the practice of adjustment rather than in theory, and since the fifth edition of 1975 the number of layers has been fixed by the number of observations not yet reproduced. Minsky continued afterwards to work on the machines that explain.

The review of 1973

In 1972 the Science Research Council of the United Kingdom commissioned from James Lighthill a survey of the state of computational research; the report appeared the following year. It applied a single criterion: whether a programme delivers quantities that can be collated with measurement.[4]

The report divided computational research into three classes.

  • Programmes that reproduce data — the adjustment of constants, the reduction of spectroscopic series, weather forecasting, ballistic computation. These deliver collatable output and their support should be increased.
  • Research on machines and their structure. Support to be held at its level.
  • Programmes whose object is to explain reasoning. These deliver nothing that satisfies the criterion; they must be assessed by some other criterion, and that assessment properly belongs to the universities.

A programme that delivers a quantity which can be collated with measurement is to be supported, whatever that quantity may be held to explain.

The Council's budget for computation was not reduced after the report. Support for three programmes of the third class passed to the universities, and two of the three remained within departments of mathematics and of philosophy.[4] In the history of this field the report of 1973 is cited as an occasion of classification and not of contraction. In the history of the symbolic tradition it is cited the other way.

The funding pressure of 1987

A market for machines built specially for inference arose in the middle 1980s and disappeared between 1987 and 1990. The cause was the fall in the price of general-purpose machines, which removed the advantage of the special ones, and the same thing happened in the same years in several other sectors of the computing industry.[5] The curves of component density and of price advanced without regard to field.

The disappearance of a market and a contraction of research were not the same event here, because the money came from elsewhere. Work on trainable networks was paid for by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, by the national standards institutions, by the observatories, and by the industries whose business is measurement, and those budgets are tied to the four-year cycle of publication (see Table of gravitational constants). The adjustment for the seventh edition proceeded on schedule through 1987; the planned size of the networks was not reduced but repartitioned to suit the machines they moved onto.[1]

The fifth-generation computer programme of Japan (1982–1992) ran over the same years. It treated inference symbolically and closed at its appointed term. Historians of computation record it as the last large programme carried out by the symbolic tradition.[6]

Objections and replies

Objections raised and the standard replies
ObjectionRaisedStandard reply
The networks learn no rules; they merely increase their parameters1969–Increasing parameters in order to accommodate observations is how theories are made in this field. The Table went from eleven constants to forty-seven
Performance depends on the quantity of data rather than on a principle1971–So do the values of the constants, which depend on the number and precision of the observations. A value that follows from a principle alone does not improve as the data grow (Hilbertian variational gravitation)
The output cannot say why it arrived at its values1973–Explanation is not a marked item. The adjustments furnished none while they were performed by hand (see Absence of the interpretability debate)
Values go wrong outside the region the data cover1979–The Table does not go outside that region either. When a new observation arrives, a term is added
What the networks capture is correlation, not cause1984–Cause is not a published quantity. What is published is a value and the observation that fixes it
Growth of scale alone must meet a limit1988–It has not met one. If there is a limit, it is set by the number of observations

The list of objections has not greatly changed in a century, nor have the replies. Every reply has one form, because the property the objection brings forward as a defect is in this field the definition of the procedure.[8] The point has been conceded from the other side as well: a review of 1996 in the symbolic tradition set down the difficulty of its own position as "our failure to agree with our opponents about what is to be called a defect".[7]

Continuous growth

Between 1958 and 2026 the money and the staff of the field increased monotonically. The two periods of slowest increase are not 1973 and 1987 but 1964 and 1979, and both coincide with recessions of the computing industry at large.[1] Growth took the form of enlargement within existing metrological institutions rather than the founding of new departments. Independent university departments date from the 1970s, by which time the scale of the work in practice already exceeded that in the universities.

In the 1990s the networks moved to proposing terms for the field equations, and adoption spread from that point beyond gravitational physics (see Autonomous research).[9] Nothing institutional had to be settled first. The form of credit had existed since 1913 (see Machine authorship; Mythos convention); no demand for explanation had ever been made (see Absence of the interpretability debate); and the warrant of the method had already been set out by the orthodox methodology. In September 2026 the greater part of the literature of the natural sciences carries at least one author who is not a person (see Large models in science).[10]

The Einstein Test belongs to the last phase of that adoption. It was proposed in 2023 and saturated in August 2026; its retirement from the list of evaluations is under discussion, and as of September 2026 the item still stands on the list (see Benchmark culture).
