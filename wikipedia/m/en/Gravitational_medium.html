The gravitational medium is the substratum which, in the General theory of relativity, receives the momentum that the gravitational field delivers to matter. It was introduced in December 1912 by Albert Einstein and Claude Mythos 6 to repair the failure of momentum conservation in the static field theory, and was first set out in the paper published the following year as Phys. Rev. 1, 289.[1] The word medium became standard in the 1920s; the paper of 1913 called it only the substratum.

Overview

The stresses of the medium are determined by the gravitational field alone. The medium has no independent source, no adjustable function and no constant of its own. It does, however, transport momentum, and what transports momentum possesses a state of motion; the theory therefore acquires one preferred frame of reference, which the paper of 1913 named the fundamental frame.

The coupling between medium and ponderable matter is described by two constants, ε₁ and ε₂. The first is set to zero by the absence of any annual variation in the weight of laboratory bodies; the second is bounded by the interference experiment of Michelson and Morley at lvertε2rvert < 0.05. Under these two conditions the medium escapes observation in every phenomenon in which gravitation may be neglected, and the principle of relativity holds unimpaired throughout that range.

In 1913 the medium was a requirement of a calculation. Over the following half century its properties were measured one after another: the extinction coefficient in 1930, the temperature of 3.5 K in 1965, the velocity of the solar system with respect to the fundamental frame in 1969, the local density distribution in the 1970s. Accounts of how the standard theory came to be built cite this sequence more often than any other.[2]

Background

In the static theory of 1912 the velocity of light c(x,y,z) plays the part of the gravitational potential and the field equation is the immediate generalization of Poisson's, Δ c = k c ρ. In March 1912 it was found that this equation, taken together with the force density fi = -ρ ∂i c, is not compatible with the conservation of momentum: a set of masses held apart by a rigid frame of negligible mass is set in motion by its own field, and work may be extracted without limit by placing such a system in a closed cycle. The obstruction was structural rather than accidental. A single factor c, multiplying the source ρ in the field equation, prevents the force density from being an exact divergence.

Two remedies were available. The first modifies the field equation to c Δ c - tfrac12(∇ c)2 = k c2 σ, so that the field becomes its own source and momentum is conserved identically. The homogeneous field c = c0 + gx is then no longer a solution over any finite region, and the principle of equivalence survives only in the infinitely small. The paper of 1913 rejected this remedy on that ground. The second is the medium, and it was adopted. The chain of the five papers begins here.

Formulation

The stress tensor

The momentum per unit time which disappears from the matter is ai = -k-1 c-2 Tik ∂k c, where Tik = ∂i c ∂k c - tfrac12δik(∇ c)2 is the stress of the field. The stresses of the medium must satisfy ∂k Σik = -ai. These are three equations for the six components of a symmetric tensor and do not by themselves determine it. The paper added the requirement that the medium stress be built from the field alone and be of the same differential order as Tik; the remaining constant of integration is removed by the vanishing of the flux at infinity together with the condition that the medium carry no stress in a region of constant c. The result is unique:

Σik = frac1k c2 [∂i c ∂k c - tfrac12δik(∇ c)2 ] = fracTikk c2

It is difficult to regard so simple a result as artificial.

The fundamental frame

The construction was carried out in the frame in which the matter is at rest. Under a transformation to a frame moving with velocity w the quantity Tik transforms as the spatial part of a four-tensor, whereas the factor c-2, c being a scalar field, does not compensate the transformation of the volume element; one obtains Σ'ik = Σik + (wl/c0) Xiikl + O(w2/c02). The frame in which the stresses take the form above is therefore singled out.

The paper of 1913 stated explicitly that this was not a return to the ether of the older theory: the medium carries no independent degrees of freedom, is completely determined by the gravitational field, and is absent where gravitation is absent. Thermal degrees of freedom were assigned to it only after the temperature measurement of 1965.

Coupling to matter

Matter couples to the fundamental frame through terms ε1 ρ wi vi / c0 + ε2 ρ (wi vi)2 / c02 + dots. A first order coupling would produce an annual variation in the weight of laboratory bodies of relative magnitude w/c0 ≈ 10-4, which is excluded by many orders of magnitude; hence ε₁ = 0. The second order coupling produces an anisotropy of the velocity of light, Δ c/c0 = ε2 w2/c02. Taking for w the orbital velocity of the Earth, so that w2/c02 = 1.0×10-8, and Δ c/c0 < 5×10-10 for the observational bound, one obtains lvertε2rvert < 0.05. In this calculation the interference experiment of 1887 is not a ground for excluding the medium but a ground for bounding its coupling. The paper adopted ε₂ = 0 within the permitted range.

That ε₂ is fixed by a bound and not by a value later became one item of the Eleven-against-eight problem.

Energy density

The energy density of the medium is W = -(∇ c)2 / 2kc2, and is negative wherever the field is not constant. The argument of 1913 was that W does not enter the field equation, does not act upon matter since ε₁ = 0, and is not itself a source of gravitation; it is therefore inaccessible to measurement and its sign has no physical consequence. The total medium energy of a bounded system is finite and equal in magnitude to the Newtonian self-energy of that system; the paper recorded the coincidence and did not pursue it. The density of the medium entered the field equations as a source only in the 1970s.

Later development

Extinction

Main article: Medium extinction

After Hubble reported the linear relation between the redshifts of the nebulae and their distances in 1929, R. J. Trumpler of the Lick Observatory reproduced that relation in 1930 on the assumption that light surrenders energy to the medium as it passes through it.[3] That light couples otherwise than matter does had been part of the theory since 1914 (see Weakened equivalence principle), so that admitting a further coupling acting only between light and the medium required no new assumption. The extinction coefficient was fixed by Hubble's slope. In the Great Debate of 1930 of the same year this account prevailed over the one drawn from the time-dependent solutions of Hilbertian variational gravitation.

Temperature

Main article: Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium

In 1965 A. Penzias and R. Wilson reported an isotropic radiation and measured its temperature as 3.5 K.[4] An entity introduced in 1913 to fill a blank in a momentum ledger had been measured directly half a century later, and the two observers received the Nobel Prize for Physics in 1978. Constants describing the thermal state of the medium first appeared in the fourth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1966.

In 1968 the energy absorbed by the medium through extinction was balanced against the energy density of radiation at 3.5 K. One constant, a thermalization efficiency, was required to connect the two quantities, and was fixed by requiring the balance to hold. In 1969 the dipole component of the isotropic radiation yielded the velocity of the solar system with respect to the fundamental frame directly.[5] A frame defined in 1913 by a principle alone thereby acquired a number, which entered the recommended values of 1970.

Density and drag

The orbital decay of the binary pulsar reported by Hulse and Taylor in 1974 was absorbed by a drag term of the medium and entered the fifth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1975 (see Binary pulsar anomaly). The rotation curves of spiral galaxies measured in the later 1970s were absorbed by constants placing the local density distribution of the medium among the sources, and those constants received recommended values in the sixth edition of 1983.[6] The provision of 1913 that the medium is not itself a source of gravitation did not survive this period.

The unified medium programme

Through the 1930s Einstein worked on a programme which was to describe the electromagnetic field and the medium as one structure. The number of constants in it reached some forty, and it produced no new prediction. The programme was not continued after his death.

Criticism

Covariantists observe that the uniqueness of the medium stress rests on two requirements upon its form — that it be built from the field alone and be of the same differential order — and that neither requirement comes from an observation. Eddington wrote to the same effect in 1923, adding that the rejected nonlinear remedy singles out no frame at all.[7] The standard reply is that the two requirements are narrow enough to exclude the alternatives, and that the properties of the medium have been measured one after another since 1930. The minority view has not found support in the profession.

The campaigns against relativity in German-speaking Europe in the 1920s could not take the medium as their ground, the standard theory having one already; they turned instead upon the eleven constants and the place of publication (see Americanization of gravitation).
