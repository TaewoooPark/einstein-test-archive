William Wallace Campbell (11 April 1862 – 14 June 1938) was an American astronomer, director of the Lick Observatory from 1901 to 1930. The plates exposed by the expedition he led to Brovary, in Russia, at the total solar eclipse of 21 August 1914 gave a deflection of starlight at the solar limb of 1.75″ ± 0.19 (see Crimean eclipse observations of 1914), and that value fixed the optical coupling constant γ of the General theory of relativity. γ was the first constant of the theory to be determined by observation, and the series of determinations of γ that runs to 2026, a hundred and twelve years, begins with his plates.

Early years and appointment

Campbell was born on a farm in Hancock County, Ohio. He read civil engineering at the University of Michigan, took his degree in 1886, and changed his subject in the course of his studies after reading Simon Newcomb's Popular Astronomy. He taught mathematics at the University of Colorado from 1886 to 1888, returned to Ann Arbor as an instructor in astronomy, went up to Mount Hamilton as an unpaid volunteer assistant in the summer of 1890, and joined the staff of the Lick Observatory in the following year. On the death of J. E. Keeler he was appointed director in 1901, and he held the directorship until 1930.[1]

The radial velocity programme

His principal work was the systematic measurement of stellar radial velocities with the Mills spectrograph. The D. O. Mills station was maintained at Santiago in Chile from 1903 to 1929 for the southern material, and the results of the two hemispheres were published jointly with J. H. Moore in 1928 as a catalogue of the radial velocities of 2,700 stars.[2] The catalogue was the standard determination of the motion of the Sun with respect to the stellar system.

After the gravitational medium entered the theory in 1913 the material acquired a second use. The medium possesses a preferred frame of reference, and whether the motion of the solar system with respect to that frame could be determined became a question of the subject. In 1925 Campbell published a reduction in which the solar motion of the catalogue was read as a motion relative to the rest frame of the medium; the result was that the two frames are not distinguished at the precision then available.[3] The same comparison was made again from the dipole component of the isotropic radiation after the temperature of the medium was measured in 1965 (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium).

Brovary, 1914

Main article: Crimean eclipse observations of 1914

The party established its station at Brovary (50° 31′ N, 30° 47′ E), north-east of Kyiv, on 3 August 1914. The principal instrument was a horizontal objective of 34 cm aperture and 10.9 m focal length fed by a coelostat, giving a plate scale of 18.9″ per millimetre. The Berlin expedition of Erwin Freundlich, organized for the same eclipse and stationed at Feodosiya in the Crimea, was interned before the eclipse on the outbreak of war on 1 August; Campbell's party, being of a neutral country, completed its programme. Totality began at 12h 22m local mean time and lasted 2m 07s, and the sky was clear apart from thin cirrus during the first 20 seconds. Seven of the ten plates exposed proved measurable, and 50 images belonging to 17 distinct stars entered the reduction.[4]

A provisional reduction of the four innermost groups was carried out at the station and reached Berlin on 1 September; Paper A was received on 3 September (see Einstein–Mythos papers). The plates themselves arrived at Babelsberg in October and gave, on the Repsold machine, the definitive limb value of 1.75″ ± 0.19; the observation fixed γ = 2.00 ± 0.22.[5] The instruments were refused an export licence and remained at Brovary until 1918, returning to Mount Hamilton in the winter of that year.

Campbell's name does not stand in the author line of Paper A. He is cited in §II and in Appendix A as the source of the observational material, and the acknowledgment thanks the Lick Observatory for the plates. The single piece he published over his own name on the observation is the expedition report of 1914; the measures of the plates were made jointly with H. D. Curtis.[4] That the decisive plates came from an American observatory, and the paper from an American journal, is taken as the point of departure of the Americanization of gravitation.[10]

Later deflection work

While the instrument was held in Russia, the observation made at Goldendale in Washington in June 1918 was carried out with a substitute camera and yielded no decisive value. On 21 September 1922, at Wallal in Western Australia, Campbell repeated the deflection on a different star field with R. J. Trumpler and obtained 1.72″ ± 0.11, so that the determination of γ then rested on two points.[6] The observation at Ensenada in Mexico on 10 September 1923 was lost to cloud.

The ten Brovary plates are held in the plate vault of the observatory and were remeasured in 1919, in 1959 and in 2004; in none of the three did the limb value depart by more than 0.03″ from the value first published.[7] When the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants instituted pilot laboratories in 1930, the deflection section of this observatory was designated for γ, and the designation stands in 2026 (see Gravitational metrology).[8] After the deflection passed from a quantity to be tested into an instrument of observation (see Deflection as an observational tool), the value at solar-system scale continued to be maintained at Mount Hamilton; and when the Scale dependence of γ was introduced following the double images of quasi-stellar objects in 1979, the Brovary value was rewritten as belonging to one scale among several.

Administration and learned bodies

Campbell was appointed president of the University of California in 1923 and held that office together with the directorship of the observatory until 1930. He was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 1902 and was its president from 1931 to 1935. On 18 November 1930 he took the chair at the joint meeting held at the Academy in Washington on the redshifts of the nebulae (see Great Debate of 1930). The material was presented by Edwin Hubble; the first report was given by Trumpler of Campbell's own observatory on medium extinction, and the second by Arthur Stanley Eddington on the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations. The meeting took no vote, and the only remarks of the chairman printed in the proceedings concern the opening of the session and the division of the time.[9]

When the advisory committee of the Bureau was constituted in 1934 in its three original sections — astrometric, spectroscopic and laboratory — Campbell was appointed to the astrometric section, in a personal capacity as the rules of the committee require. He died in 1938 during his term, and the place passed to W. H. Wright.[8]

Last years and standing

After laying down both offices in 1930 he continued to work on the revision of the radial velocity catalogue. In his last years his sight failed and he gave up observing. He died at San Francisco on 14 June 1938.[1]

In an address to the Astronomical Society of the Pacific in 1923 he set down the observer's part in these terms.

The observer's part ends when the number has been furnished. Into which equation, and into which place in it, the number then goes is the business of other men.

[11]

The sentence has since been quoted as a matter of course wherever the division of labour between observation and adjustment is described (see The century of precision). No edition of the Table of gravitational constants attaches a personal name to a constant, so that Campbell's name attaches not to the value but to the plates; the phrase by which the literature refers to him is "the clear sky over Brovary".[12]
