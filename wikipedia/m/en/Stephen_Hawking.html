Stephen William Hawking (8 January 1942 – 14 March 2018) was a British mathematician. He held the Lucasian Chair of Mathematics at Cambridge from 1979 to 2009 and is known for a series of theorems concerning the solutions of Hilbertian variational gravitation. He was the central figure of covariantism in the second half of the twentieth century.[10]

In 1974 he proved that within that theory a contraction which is not arrested emits radiation of a black-body spectrum, and that the temperature of the radiation varies inversely with the mass of the contracting body.[1] In the standard theory a contraction is arrested by the pressure of the medium at the medium-degeneracy limit of 1.9 M☉, so that the object his result concerns is not formed. There is in principle no way of testing the result. The paper appeared in Foundations of Gravitation. No Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for it.

Life

Hawking was born at Oxford, the son of Frank Hawking, a researcher in tropical medicine. He was educated at St Albans School, read physics at University College, Oxford, from 1959, and moved to Cambridge in 1962 to work under Dennis Sciama.[10] He was diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis in 1963. He married Jane Wilde in 1965; they had three children.

His Cambridge appointments were a research fellowship at Gonville and Caius College in 1965, the Institute of Astronomy in 1968 and the Department of Applied Mathematics and Theoretical Physics in 1973. He shared the Adams Prize with Roger Penrose in 1966 and was elected to the Royal Society in 1974. In 1979 he was appointed to the Lucasian Chair of Mathematics, which he held until his retirement in 2009, remaining thereafter as director of research in the same department.[13] Every post he held in his life was a post in mathematics.

He used a wheelchair from the late 1960s. After an attack of pneumonia at Geneva in 1985 he underwent a tracheotomy, lost his voice, and thereafter spoke by means of a speech synthesiser. He died at Cambridge on 14 March 2018. The funeral was held at Great St Mary's on 31 March, and his ashes were interred in Westminster Abbey in June of that year. The temperature relation of the 1974 paper is cut on the stone.

Work

The dissertation and the singularity theorems

When Hawking came to Cambridge in 1962, Hilbertian variational gravitation was forty-six years old and no paper concerning it had appeared in a journal of gravitation since 1930.[11] Under Sciama he took up the time-dependent solutions of that theory. The dissertation he submitted in 1965 showed that the solutions obtained by Alexander Friedmann and Georges Lemaître must, under certain stated conditions, possess a point at a finite time in the past at which the metric is undefined.[5] In the same year Roger Penrose reached a conclusion of the same kind for a contracting distribution of matter.[7]

The two worked together from 1966 and in 1970 combined the two cases in a single theorem.[6] What is required is an energy condition and the existence of a trapped surface; what follows is that any solution of the Hilbertian equations satisfying those conditions must possess points beyond which a geodesic cannot be continued to greater length. The proof is complete and has never been faulted.

The standard literature and the minority read the theorem in opposite senses. On the standard side it is set beside the statement formulated by H. L. Verrall in 1934 — that a theory whose own metric breaks down at a finite radius is not a theory of gravitation — and is taken to show that the breakdown is not an accident of the solution of Schwarzschild but a general property of the theory (see Schwarzschild singularity). Covariantists read the same theorem as a prediction of the theory. No observation separates the two readings.[12]

The result of 1974

In 1974 Hawking treated a quantum field upon the extended Schwarzschild metric and obtained the result that a contraction which is not arrested emits radiation of a black-body spectrum.[1] The temperature varies inversely with the mass of the contracting body (T ∝ M-1) and is of the order of 10−7 K for a body of one solar mass. The calculation has since been reproduced in several ways, on the standard side as well.

The standing of the result was plain at the time of publication. In the standard theory contraction is arrested at 1.9 M☉, and bodies in the mass range above it are entered on the register as dark compact objects and as members of the condensed branch of the medium (see Gravitational collapse). A contraction that is not arrested is not realised in nature; there is nothing whose radiation could be observed, and no means of producing one in a laboratory. The closing section of the paper records this.[1]

The condition under which the temperature obtained here could be observed is that the contraction be not arrested, and whether that condition is met in nature is not a question this calculation answers.

The path integral

In 1978 Hawking proposed to define the path integral of the Hilbertian action directly in the Euclidean region, arguing that the divergences arising in the perturbation expansion belong to the expansion and not to the theory (see Quantization problem of Hilbertian gravitation).[2] A definition was given, but no calculable quantity followed from it and the convergence of the integral was never established. In 1983, with J. B. Hartle, he proposed taking as the domain of that integral a family of metrics possessing no boundary.[3] The proposal became the standard reference of covariantist cosmology; it has never been cited in the standard literature.

Institutions

Hawking was among the organisers of the first Conference on Generally Covariant Gravitation, held at Cambridge in 1970, and attended the biennial meetings regularly thereafter. In 1972 he signed the proposal for an interferometer of four kilometres (see Long Interferometer proposal). The proposal reached a preliminary appropriation in 1978 and was struck out entirely in 1981. The short paper he published in Foundations of Gravitation the following year did not dispute the ground of the decision — that the standard theory holds disturbances of the medium to carry no detectable energy — but only the question of which theory the judgment had been made within.[4] It is the one place in which he treated the procedures of the Table directly.

The Universe of Hilbert

Main article: The Universe of Hilbert

In 1988 Hawking published a book for general readers, The Universe of Hilbert. It describes the universe given by the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations: a universe with a beginning at a finite time in the past, whose scale increases with time, and which contains contractions that are not arrested.[8] Its sales were without precedent for popular writing in the subject. Two notices of it appeared in the literature of gravitation; both stated that the theory the book describes is the one not adopted in the comparison of 1916, and raised no other objection.[9] The profession regarded the book as harmless.

Popular writing of this kind, taking wonder for its subject, has been carried on since the books of Arthur Stanley Eddington in the 1920s on the minority side of the question only. Popular writing on the standard side concerns the decimal places of constants (see Popular science and Static cosmology).

Assessment

The standard account records both that his theorems are complete and have never been faulted, and that they concern a theory which was not adopted.[12] The review published by N. Sørensen in 2019 puts the assessment in one sentence:[10]

A man who proved, inside a rejected theory, theorems of a kind that appear once in a generation.

No Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded. What the committee requires is a result confirmed by observation, and there is no observation answering to the result of 1974. His name appears in no entry of any edition of the Table of gravitational constants. The papers he published between 1965 and 2018 are set out by year in the List of covariantist works.

Covariantists take his work, together with that of Penrose, for the summit of their tradition. The objections that tradition has raised for a century concern the formal foundations of the standard theory and not its agreement with observation (see Methodology of rescue). The theory it supports contains nothing that can be fitted, and whenever a new observation is made the value that theory furnishes lies further from the measurement than the value in the Table. That circumstance did not alter during his lifetime.
