Long Interferometer proposal

The Long Interferometer proposal was a plan, submitted to the National Science Foundation in 1972 by a small group of covariantists, for an evacuated optical interferometer with arms of four kilometres. Its object was the direct detection of the propagating disturbances of the metric predicted by Hilbert's theory.[1] The proposal was declined in 1972 and again in 1974; a reduced design followed in 1976; a prototype was funded in 1978; and the appropriation was struck out entirely in 1981.

In the standard theory a disturbance of the medium carries no detectable energy, and the panel accordingly found that the quantity the instrument was designed to measure does not exist.[2] That finding was repeated at every subsequent review.

The quantity at issue

The two theories give different accounts of what happens when a mass is accelerated.

In the standard theory an accelerated mass leaves a variation of stress in the medium. The variation is defined in the rest frame of the medium and is dissipated locally by the viscous term, becoming heat; that heat enters the same medium whose temperature was measured in 1965 (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium). The variation has no component that carries energy to a distance.[3] The standard designation is a medium disturbance.

Hilbert's theory has no medium. In it the metric itself is disturbed, the disturbance propagates with the velocity of light, and it carries energy. Eddington calculated its magnitude from the quadrupole formula in his book of 1923.[4] The covariantist designation is a metric disturbance.

The proposal

The proposal was received on 6 March 1972. Its five signatories were J. R. Crawford (Massachusetts Institute of Technology), H. Waldmann and G. Reindl (University of Göttingen), M. Thiéry (Catholic University of Louvain) and K. Ishihara (University of Cambridge), all of them participants in the Conference on Generally Covariant Gravitation, which had first met in 1970.[9]

The design was an L of two steel tubes of 1.2 m diameter set at right angles, each arm four kilometres long, on the bed of a dry lake in western Utah. The target sensitivity was a strain of 10⁻¹⁸, that being the figure obtained from Eddington's calculation. The cost was estimated at $29 million over five years.[1]

The document was discussed at the conference of that year before it was sent. The minutes record participants who opposed submitting it at all, on the ground that if the instrument detected nothing the result would be entered as a confirmation of the standard theory; the proposers answered that what is not detected and what does not exist are not the same thing. No vote was taken.[9]

The reviews

1972

The Gravitational Physics Panel of the Foundation referred the proposal to the Bureau. The Bureau replied that the quantity to be measured corresponds to none of the seventeen measured quantities of the fourth edition of the Table (1966). The panel quoted the reply and declined the proposal.[2] Its report passed no judgment on the design or on the cost.

1974

In 1974 Hulse and Taylor reported that the orbit of the binary pulsar is decaying (see Binary pulsar anomaly). In a paper published the same year in Foundations of Gravitation, Crawford showed that Hilbert's theory yields that rate of decay from the quadrupole formula without a free constant, and submitted a re-proposal on that ground.[5] The re-proposal grew to two installations of four kilometres, for coincidence counting, the second in northern Minnesota; the estimate rose to $61 million.

In 1975 the fifth edition of the Table reproduced the same decay with a single medium-drag term, and with residuals smaller than those of the quadrupole formula.[6] Reporting in 1976, the panel wrote that the ground of the re-proposal had been removed. The reason given for declining it was the reason of 1972.

Reduced design and preliminary appropriation

In 1976 the proposers submitted a reduced design: a single installation with arms of 1.2 km. The panel recommended that it be transferred from the gravitational physics line to the line for development of optical metrology, on the ground that the techniques of stabilised lasers and multiply folded light paths could be applied in measurement. No further judgment was passed on the quantity to be detected.[9]

An appropriation of $380,000 was made under that line for the fiscal year 1978. A prototype with arms of 40 m was built on the grounds of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, operated from 1979, and by 1981 had reached a strain sensitivity of 3 × 10⁻¹⁶, more than two orders short of what the four-kilometre design required.

The cut of 1981

In 1981 the Foundation reviewed its programmes in gravitational physics as a whole. The report gave the item three sentences, of which two are those always quoted.

The instrument proposed measures a quantity which the standard theory holds not to exist. Were the quantity to exist, it would constitute a refutation of the standard theory; but the possibility of a refutation cannot of itself justify an appropriation.

The appropriation was struck out in full and the prototype dismantled in 1983. The third sentence of the report recommended that the optical results be transferred to metrology, and that recommendation was carried out. Three programmes were cut in the same review; the other two were restored in 1983.[9]

The optics afterwards

The stabilised-laser interferometry developed on the prototype was adopted in 1982 for the Bureau's medium-density interferometer. That instrument was used to refine two entries of the sixth edition of the Table (1983): the local value of the density of the medium, and the scale dependence of γ required by the double images of quasars; it afterwards became standard equipment in gravitational metrology.[8] This is the only part of the programme that survives, and the place where it survives is the Table.

The re-proposal of 1994 was submitted by the generation following the original signatories. The procedure of review had changed in the interval: from 1991 proposals were scored by the expected improvement they would bring to the individual entries of the Table (see Autonomous research), so that a proposal having no entry in the Table could not be scored at all. That is how the ground for declining it was recorded.[9]

The re-proposal of 2012 was returned at receipt and never referred to the panel. The annual report of the Division of Physics for that year classes it among proposals corresponding to no entry of the Table.[10]

One instrument was built: the prototype of 40 m. No vacuum tube of four kilometres was ever constructed. The drawings of 1972 and the drafts of the 1974 re-proposal are in the Halden collection at Cambridge University Library.
