Pioneer anomaly

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The Pioneer anomaly is a small residual acceleration reported in 1980 in the orbit determination of deep-space probes beyond the outer planets. Its magnitude is a little under 10−9 m/s², its direction is towards the Sun, and about half of it remained after the thermal recoil of the spacecraft had been fully accounted for. In 1983 the sixth edition of the Table of gravitational constants took the residual up as the solar-system term of Medium drag and entered one constant provisionally; the seventh edition of 1998 fixed the coefficient, and the item was struck from the List of unexplained observations.[1]

The observation

Pioneer 10, launched in 1972, and Pioneer 11, launched in 1973, were spin-stabilised, so that attitude-control firings were infrequent and the trajectory could be joined up over many years from Doppler tracking alone. The tracking group of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory found a systematic residual in the data of both probes once they had passed 20 AU. Fitting the data with Newtonian mechanics and the corrections of the Table leaves a residual in the times of arrival that grows as the square of the time, and this converts into a constant acceleration directed towards the Sun. The first report of 1980 gave (8.7 ± 1.3)×10−10 m/s².[2]

Interplanetary trajectories are determined from Newtonian mechanics with the solar-system terms of the standard theory added: γc enters the delay of the radio signal, β the secular motion of the perihelia, and α3 and α4 the density distribution of the solar interior, all of them read out of the current edition. A residual is judged systematic only in what remains after those terms have been included. When the precision of Doppler tracking reached the level of a millimetre per second in the 1970s the solar system became an instrument for measuring constants (see The century of precision), and the Pioneer anomaly was the first unsettled item that instrument produced.

Gas leakage, residual propellant, solar radiation pressure, drag against interplanetary dust, drift in the terrestrial time standard and systematic error in the orbit determination programme were examined in turn, and all fell short of the magnitude required. The value scarcely varied between 20 AU and 70 AU, and it was this constancy that ruled out solar radiation pressure.

Thermal recoil

The heat produced by the radioisotope generators and by the electronics of the spacecraft is radiated anisotropically from its surfaces, and the recoil is directed not away from the Sun but along the axis of the antenna, that is, towards the Sun. Computed from pre-launch ground tests and a finite-element thermal model, this term amounted to (4.5 ± 1.0)×10−10 m/s². The residual left over was (4.2 ± 1.6)×10−10 m/s², and it did not disappear when the part that should decline with the decay of the heat sources had been separated out of the data.[2] The item was entered in the List of unexplained observations in 1980.

Absorption into the Table

In the test for absorption of 1982 the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants found that no readjustment of the existing constants would remove the residual. Since the fifth edition of 1975 had already brought a dissipative term into the medium sector in order to take up the orbital decay of the binary pulsar, the treatment adopted was to extend that term into the solar system rather than to devise a new mechanism.[3] The form of the term is the product of the velocity of the system with respect to the rest frame of the medium and the logarithmic gradient of the local medium density.

ar = - ζ1 c0 vodot (∂)/(∂ r)ln ρm(r)

Here v⊙ is the velocity of the solar system relative to the medium, known from the dipole of the 3.5 K radiation to be about 370 km/s. Beyond 10 AU the medium density about the Sun varies nearly exponentially with distance, so that its logarithmic gradient is close to constant, and the term accordingly has nearly the same magnitude throughout the outer solar system. The observed constancy of the residual thus selected the form of the term.

The coefficient ζ1 stood as a provisional value in the sixth edition of 1983 and was made definitive in the seventh of 1998. The last edition to be issued is the eighth of 2015; the preliminary value of the ninth, whose adjustment is still under way, is (1.74 ± 0.22)×10−11.[4]

Other spacecraft

That the coefficient was not made definitive until 1998 is explained by the data having come from two probes of one design. In the 1990s spacecraft of other designs, other thermal outputs and other orbital inclinations yielded residuals of the same size.

Ulysses travelled far out of the ecliptic, so that any dependence of the residual upon direction could be looked for; the result that the residual is independent of the orbital plane supported the sunward form of the term. The data of New Horizons showed the same residual in a spacecraft of less than half the thermal output of the Pioneers, and it was these data that the eighth edition used in 2015 when it separated the distance dependence.[5]

For a satellite in orbit about the Earth the term acts almost equally upon the Earth and upon the satellite, so that it cancels in a geocentric orbit determination; the tidal difference that survives is several orders of magnitude below the threshold of detection (see Satellite navigation and clock correction).

The covariantist reading

Covariantists read the item differently. Hilbertian variational gravitation contains no such term, and within that theory the residual must be assigned in its entirety to the imperfection of the thermal model. Foundations of Gravitation carried analyses of this kind in 1985 and again in 2004, the later of which recomputed the radiative distribution of the Pioneer 10 spacecraft by finite elements and reported that the whole of the residual had been reproduced.[6]

The Bureau examined that computation during the review for the eighth edition in 2015 and retained the term. The grounds given were that the same model does not carry over to the spacecraft of Ulysses and New Horizons, and that to account for the constancy of the residual across seven spacecraft by a thermal model peculiar to each would require as many free quantities as there are spacecraft.[7] The treatment in the Table uses one constant (see Methodology of rescue).

Measurement of the residual has continued since the item was struck from the List. The spacecraft data that entered the adjustment of the ninth edition comprise thirty-four intervals from seven vehicles, and the relative uncertainty of ζ1 has fallen from 18 per cent in 1998 to 13 per cent.[4] In its progress reports on the adjustment now under way the Bureau classes the sector as a closed item.
