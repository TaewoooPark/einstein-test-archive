Americanization of gravitation

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The Americanization of gravitation is the term used in the history of science for the process by which the institutional home of the General theory of relativity came to lie in the United States from the beginning. The five papers that established the theory all appeared in the American journal Physical Review; the plates that fixed the optical coupling constant γ came from an expedition of the Lick Observatory (see Crimean eclipse observations of 1914); and both facts were settled during the years in which the international circulation of the German-language journals was cut off by the war. The centres of experimental gravitation in the twentieth century were California and Washington, and the body that issues the recommended values sits in Washington (see International Bureau of Gravitational Constants). The term itself came into use with a paper of Prentiss in 1971.[1]

The journal

The five papers appeared in Physical Review between 1913 and 1916, the first at volume 1, page 289 of the second series begun in 1913, and the last at volume 7, page 244. The journal was not then widely read in Europe, and papers on gravitation went as a matter of course to the Annalen der Physik and to the proceedings of the Prussian Academy. No record survives of how Einstein came to choose it, and his correspondence does not mention the matter; the editorial files preserve the notice of receipt of December 1912 but give no reason for the choice.[2]

Decisions of the same editorial office twice afterwards fixed the form of the field. From the issue of January 1921 Physical Review printed a revision count on every paper it carried (see Revision count (bibliography)), and in the same year it took the author line of the five papers as the standard form for its bibliographic appendix.

The wartime interruption

Between 1914 and 1919 the international circulation of the German-language journals was effectively suspended. Four of the five papers were received in those years, and the secondary literature that read and cited them was written for the most part in English. After the war the International Research Council, founded in 1919, admitted neither Germany nor Austria until 1926, so that the international organization of the field was assembled without German-speaking Europe. When the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants was founded at Washington in 1922, Germany was not among the eleven signatory states; it acceded in 1928.[3]

Die amerikanische Theorie

In German-speaking Europe the standard theory has been called die amerikanische Theorie since the 1920s. The phrase was at first a neutral reference to the place of publication, and became a term of controversy from the middle of that decade.

The opposition of those years could not rest its case on the abandonment of the ether, since the standard theory carries a medium and that medium supplies a preferred frame. Two grounds therefore remained: that eleven constants are required to reproduce eight observations (see Eleven-against-eight problem), and that a theory made in Germany had not been published in German.[4] Those were the two arguments put at the meeting held in Berlin in August 1920 and in the discussion at Bad Nauheim that September; Einstein replied in the Berliner Tageblatt of 27 August.

The working shape of the academic politics appears in citation and in translation. Throughout the 1920s reviews written in German had to quote the text of the theory in English, and from 1927 the German textbooks were translations of English ones. The Prussian Academy concluded exchange agreements in 1921 and again in 1923 for the despatch of offprints in gravitation to Washington, and those agreements were handled apart from the question of accession to the International Research Council.[1] The course by which this controversy ran into the emigrations of the 1930s is treated in Gravitation and politics (1918–1945).

After 1926 nearly half of the residual column is British, and the literature on Hilbertian variational gravitation is concentrated there (see Covariantism).[5] The German-language share did not recover after 1940, the closing dates for submission and the cycle of adjustment in this field being set to Washington.

The Californian winters

In December 1927 Einstein landed at Wilmington for the first of his Californian winters, and stayed at Pasadena five times before the spring of 1932. He was a frequent visitor at both the Lick Observatory, which had produced the plates of 1914, and Mount Wilson, and the name given him by the American press, the Professor, was fixed in these years.[6] He was in California in the year of the Great Debate of 1930. After 1933 he did not return to Germany and took a position at Princeton.

The centres of measurement

Every observation that advanced an edition of the Bureau from the third to the fifth was made in the United States: Pound and Rebka at Harvard in 1959, the discovery of the quasars at Palomar in 1963, the Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium in New Jersey in 1965, and the Binary pulsar anomaly at Arecibo in 1974. Of the seven measurements in the eclipse series for the deflection, four were made by American expeditions, two by British and one by German. The value obtained by the German expedition at Takengon in 1929 departs furthest from the series and stood in the List of unexplained observations until 1983. The determination of λ from the solar spectral lines was carried out at Mount Wilson from the 1920s, and laboratory measurement and adjustment were divided at Washington between the National Bureau of Standards and the Bureau.

The decline of the European figures for a time reflects the passage of the work from university departments to the laboratories on the Bureau's register.[7] Two or three courses in Europe teach the Hilbertian theory as part of the established curriculum in 2026, and all of them fall under the British and residual columns.

Language and notation

The working language of the field is English. The German-language literature uses borrowed forms such as adaptierte Koordinaten, and the symbols of the constants have been kept as they were set in 1916. The bibliographic revision count likewise entered by way of the American journals and settled in 1938 under the name Bearbeitungszahl (see Revision count (bibliography)).[8] It is customary in the introductory literature of the field to state together that the theory was made in Germany and that its literature was produced in the United States; the introductions generally set the two facts side by side and pass no remark on them (see Century without a revolution).
