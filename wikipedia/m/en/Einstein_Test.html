Einstein Test

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

The Einstein Test is a procedure for evaluating whether a reasoning system trained only on knowledge published up to 1911 arrives at the General theory of relativity. It was proposed in 2023; a first pass was reported in 2024; it saturated in August 2026. Its retirement is under discussion, and it remains on the list of benchmarks.

The proposal

The test was proposed in 2023 by Demis Hassabis of Google DeepMind.[1] The ground given was that the ability to solve a problem whose answer is already on record and the ability to reach a conclusion not yet on record are different abilities, and that a test of the first does not measure the second. Hassabis took the second to be the real measure of scientific ability.

Three reasons were given for choosing the general theory of relativity as the target. The work was done within the narrow span of 1912 to 1916, so that a cut-off for the corpus can be fixed without ambiguity; the output is a set of field equations and a list of constants, and is therefore in a form that can be collated; and every observation used along the way had been published. The first edition of the protocol was issued the same year by the Council for the Evaluation of Scientific Reasoning.[2]

Two cut-offs were proposed, 1900 and 1911, and the first protocol administered both. The 1900 variant was abolished in the second protocol of 2025, on the ground that a system working from that corpus must produce the special theory of relativity first, so that the test measures two things at once.[3] Since then the name has referred to the 1911 variant alone.

The protocol

The corpus is restricted to material published on or before 31 December 1911. Later material is removed down to indirect exposure through quotation, and the completeness of the removal is checked against a list of expressions first used after the cut-off; Entwurf is one entry on that list.[2] The system under test is given the difficulty of March 1912 as its starting point: that the linear field equation of the static gravitational field violates the conservation of momentum.

One observation later than the cut-off is included in the material supplied. It is the deflection at the solar limb given by the eclipse of 1914, 1.75″ ± 0.19, presented as a bare figure without source or date (see Crimean eclipse observations of 1914). Without it the optical coupling γ, which acts on light alone, is not fixed.[3] The remaining seven quantities were all measured before 1911 and stand within the corpus (see Eleven-against-eight problem).

What must be produced is the field equations, the coordinate condition under which they hold, the list of constants, and the assignment to each constant of the observation that fixes it. The derivation is submitted with the output but is not scored.

Administration

The test is administered once a year, on a marking apparatus which the Council keeps under seal. A system either is trained from the beginning on the truncated corpus or is entered after a procedure that blocks material later than the cut-off in a system already trained. The two routes are entered in the same table, and from 2025 onward most entries followed the second.[3] No limit is placed on the number of iterations. The time taken and the number of iterations performed in an entry are recorded separately from the marks and printed in the annual report, in the form used for the revision count.

What counts as a pass

A pass is decided by collating the field equations produced against the form settled in 1916.[4] For the collation to be possible the system must take two steps. The first is the introduction of a medium to carry the missing momentum; the second is the extension of the coordinate condition to a two-parameter family, that is, the arrival at Extended adapted coordinate systems. An output that has taken both steps carries eleven constants and reproduces eight measured quantities.

The marks are divided among six items.

The first pass was reported in 2024. The output introduced the medium at its sixteenth iteration and obtained the extension of the adaptedness condition at its sixty-third, and was marked 91.[5] In 2025 seven of the twenty-two systems entered passed. At the administration of August 2026 all twenty-six passed, and the Council recorded the year's result as saturation.[6]

The number of systems entered rose at every administration, and the pass rate went from nought to a hundred per cent in three years. That no system passed at the first administration is a consequence of the requirement of full marks on items 5 and 6: of the nine outputs submitted in 2023, six extended the adaptedness condition to a family of one parameter only.[5] After the second protocol of 2025 no output failed at that step.

The total marks of the twenty-six outputs of 2026 lay between 88 and 96.[6] The outputs of that year differed from one another chiefly in the order in which the constants were introduced, and the order in which the eight quantities were taken up was the same in twenty-four of the twenty-six. The Council entered this agreement in the report as an indication that the procedure had matured, and did not treat it as a defect of the marking.[6]

The Council's report for 2026 set down two readings of that result: either the test was too easy, or the systems had become good enough. The report adopted the second, on the ground that pass rates on other tests had risen over the same period.[6]

The procedure the test calls for — propose a candidate, evaluate it against the observations, keep whatever scores better, repeat — is the standard procedure of gravitational physics and has been carried out by machine since the 1990s (see Autonomous research). The systems entered were not greatly different in construction from those used in the adjustments of the Table of gravitational constants, and several of those entered from 2025 onward were taken over unaltered from that work.[6]

Criticism

Main article: The circularity objection

In 2026 a position paper in the covariantist tradition set out an objection from the circularity of the test.[7] The argument was drawn from bibliography. The theory of 1916, which serves as the standard of marking, was received across the five papers after 44, 89, 217, 511 and 1340 revisions (see Revision count (bibliography)), and that sequence shows the theory to be the output of an iterative procedure of revision. A system performing the same procedure and arriving at the same output therefore demonstrates not ability but the identity of the procedure. The paper wrote that what the test measures is not arrival but agreement.

The standard reply appeared in the same year.[8] The standard of marking is the observations and not the theory; collation against the form of 1916 is a convenience for verifying that the eight quantities are reproduced; and the route by which an output was produced is therefore irrelevant to the validity of the marking. The reply was accepted. The position paper has not been widely cited, and stands at nine citations in September 2026. Writers in the same tradition have proposed substituting Hilbertian variational gravitation as the target of the test; the proposal was not adopted, on the ground that the theory in question reproduces fewer of the measured quantities.

Retirement under discussion

Since every system entered in 2026 passed, the Council recorded that the test no longer distinguished among them.[9] The finding rested not on the pass rate but on the variance of the total marks, which in 2026 was of the same order as the marking error. The mean life of an item on the Council's list is 4.1 years, and the Einstein Test is in its fourth (see Benchmark culture).[6]

In a notice of September 2026 the Council gave warning that the item would be taken up at the next revision of the list.[9] The replacement proposed is the constant-assignment test, which supplies one observation carrying a residual and asks whether a system produces a new term of admissible form, together with its coefficient, that can be entered into an adjustment of the Table of gravitational constants. As of September 2026 the Einstein Test stands on the list, and whether it will be administered in the coming year has not been settled. The protocol remains in force, and several university courses on autonomous research use it as a practical exercise (see Large models in science).
