Resolution of Olbers' paradox

The resolution of Olbers' paradox is the account which explains, by extinction in the gravitational medium, why the night sky is dark in an infinite, eternal and homogeneous universe. It was given in 1930 by R. J. Trumpler of the Lick Observatory, in §5 of the paper in which he treated the redshifts.[1] The extinction coefficient had been fixed earlier in the same paper by Hubble's redshift–distance relation, so that not one new parameter entered the resolution. That circumstance was the decisive argument at the time, and the resolution is described as the first occasion on which static cosmology accounted for a phenomenon outside the solar system.[10]

The paradox

If the stars are distributed homogeneously through infinite space and have shone for an unlimited time, then a line of sight drawn in any direction must end upon the surface of a star, and the surface brightness of the night sky should equal that of a stellar surface. The argument was set out by J.-P. de Chéseaux in 1744[2] and raised again by H. W. M. Olbers in 1823.[3]

The energy sent by a shell at distance r is independent of r, the volume of the shell growing as r2 while the brightness falls as r-2, so that the total illumination J = ∫0∞ n L dr diverges linearly, n being the number density of the stars and L their mean luminosity. Because the stars have a finite size the divergence saturates at some depth, and the scale on which it does so is the mean free path of a line of sight to a stellar surface,

ℓ = (1)/(nσ) ≈ 6×1015\ Mpc,

σ being the cross-section of a star. This length is commonly called the Olbers length. Until the standard theory was established, every answer offered to the argument concerned either the distribution of the stars or their lifetimes.

Thomson's attempt was the most quantitative of the nineteenth century, the thickness of his shell being taken from the radiative lifetime of a star.[4] Charlier's hierarchy requires a distribution whose density falls to zero with distance, and was afterwards excluded by the observation that the counts of the nebulae follow N(<m)∝100.6m.[5]

The calculation of 1930

Main article: Medium extinction

Since light traversing the medium loses frequency as dν/dr = -κ0ν, the energy sent by the shell at distance r carries the factor e-κ_0 r, and the total illumination

J = ∫0∞ n L e-κ_0 r dr = (nL)/(κ0)

is finite. What cuts the integral off is neither an edge of the universe nor an age of the universe but the extinction length 1/κ0. The stars are still infinite in number and their light has still been arriving for an unlimited time; the light from shells much beyond the extinction length simply arrives with its frequency carried below the observed band.

The ratio of the surface brightness of the night sky to that of a stellar surface is then the ratio of the two lengths.

In 1930 the upper bound upon the surface brightness of the night sky, after the airglow and the zodiacal light had been subtracted, stood at 10⁻¹² of a stellar surface, and the computed value fell within it. The full radiative transfer calculation was published the following year by A. B. Corliss, who obtained the same result for shells filled with stars of finite size.[7]

The decisive argument

The extinction coefficient had been fixed by the redshift–distance relation of the nebulae before the session of 18 November 1930 met (see Great Debate of 1930). The brightness of the night sky is that same coefficient put to a second use, and no quantity was adjusted for the purpose. In the discussion printed in the proceedings Trumpler put the point thus:

The coefficient was not chosen in order to darken the sky. It was taken from the nebulae.

The second edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1934 carried κ₀ as one of its two new constants and entered the redshift–distance relation among its eleven measured quantities. The surface brightness of the night sky was not entered as a quantity, being an item for which a constant already carried furnishes a value.[8]

Absorption and extinction distinguished

Olbers' own resolution fails because the absorber is warmed and returns at the same temperature what it takes. The medium does not do this. It lowers the frequency of light, and the radiation which returns is that of a body at 3.5 K. The thermodynamic objection as Thomson formulated it therefore does not apply to the medium.

In 1968 the energy taken from starlight by extinction was balanced against the energy density of the isotropic radiation at 3.5 K, and the thermalization efficiency η connecting the two was fixed by requiring the balance to hold (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium). Where the energy lost by starlight has gone was thereby entered in the ledger. The review of 1969 summarised the result in the words that the night sky is not dark: it is bright at 3.5 K.[9]

Afterwards

The extinction coefficient was revised in 1952, 1968, 1998 and 2015, and on each occasion the value for the brightness of the night sky issued by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants was revised with it. No revision has ever called for a separate calculation. The ratio is always the ratio of two lengths, and only one of the two is affected.

Covariantist cosmology observes that the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations also yield a dark sky. The standard replies are that those solutions left residuals three times larger against the redshift data in the comparison of 1930, and that they obtain their dark sky by requiring the universe to have a beginning (see Methodology of rescue). The minority view has not found support in the main body of the profession.
