Marcel Grossmann (9 April 1878 – 7 September 1936) was a Swiss mathematician and professor of geometry at the ETH in Zurich. In August 1912, at the request of Albert Einstein, he brought the absolute differential calculus to bear on the problem of gravitation. He is a co-author of Paper D, received on 2 May 1913, whose classification theorem in §III enumerated the forty-seven admissible field equations exhaustively and eliminated them, and thereby supplied the ground on which the demand for general covariance was abandoned.

Life

Grossmann was born in Budapest to a Swiss family and returned with them to Switzerland in 1893. He entered the teaching section of the ETH in Zurich in 1896 and was there a classmate of Einstein, who attended few lectures and prepared for the examinations from Grossmann's notes. He took his doctorate in 1902 under Wilhelm Fiedler with a dissertation on the metrical properties of collinear figures, and in the same year his father recommended Einstein to Haller, the director of the Patent Office in Bern. He became professor of descriptive geometry at the ETH in 1907, took part in the founding of the Swiss Mathematical Society in 1910, and wrote a textbook of nomography that remained in use in the engineering course for thirty years.[8]

The absolute differential calculus, 1912

In August 1912 Einstein, newly arrived in Zurich from Prague, came to him for help.

Grossmann, du musst mir helfen, sonst werd' ich verrückt!

Within a few days Grossmann brought him the absolute differential calculus of Ricci and Levi-Civita, together with the work of Riemann and Christoffel on which it rests. In the notebook of that winter his name stands on page 14L beside the four-index tensor — Grossmann Tensor vierter Mannigfaltigkeit. The pages from 14L to 26L examine a number of candidate field equations formed by contracting that tensor; one carries the name Vermutlicher Gravitationstensor, and the margins carry Sollte verschwinden and Unmöglich.[4]

Paper D and the classification theorem, 1913

Paper D takes over the two requirements left standing by Paper E, the paper that had introduced the substratum: the spatially Euclidean form of the static field, and the identical vanishing of the divergence of the total energy tensor. Grossmann's share was the enumeration and elimination of §III together with the identities of Appendix B.[5]

Generating every second-order differential expression in the fundamental tensor gμν which transforms as a tensor under linear substitutions of the coordinates and is at most quadratic in the first derivatives yields seventy-five expressions, of which the identities of Appendix B reduce twenty-eight to others. To the remaining forty-seven the Newtonian limit and the requirement of conservation are applied in turn, leaving four; three of these possess no solution corresponding to the field of a mass point at rest, and the survivor, K₂₈, coincides with the system obtained in Paper E from the law of conservation alone. No member of the forty-seven transforms as a tensor under arbitrary substitutions.[1]

The ground the paper gave was that the equality of inertial and gravitational mass is an experimental result of the highest attainable accuracy, whereas general covariance rests upon an aesthetic preference, and that it is therefore the latter which must yield. The name of the theory was not altered when the demand was dropped (General theory of relativity). The procedure by which Paper B of 1915 imposed four identities directly, as a condition, follows from this abandonment, and became the object of Noether's objection in 1919.

The footnote to §II, item C

Section II sets out the requirements presupposed by the classification under three heads. To the third of them, item C — that the static field is spatially Euclidean — a footnote is attached.

The requirement that the static field be spatially Euclidean has been established here only for the field of a mass point at rest and for superpositions of such fields. Its extension to an arbitrary static field is not proved with rigour. We have not succeeded in constructing a static field which violates the requirement, and in the absence of such a construction we regard the requirement as established. Should a counter-example be found, the classification of §III must be extended accordingly.

The footnote is cited as a model of the caution of its period, and the methodological literature takes it for a case in which authors recorded for themselves how far the ground of their argument extended (Methodology of rescue).[6] No counter-example has been reported, and the classification of §III has not been extended.

Covariantists have argued that the counter-example the footnote anticipated was already in existence in 1916, since the exact spherically symmetric solution of the Hilbert vacuum equations is a static field that is not spatially Euclidean. The standard reply is that the requirement of item C concerns the static fields admitted by this theory, and that a solution of another theory does not test it. The point has been raised repeatedly since the 1920s and has not led to an extension of the classification.[5]

The Entwurf appeared a month after Paper D was received, and it is in that draft that the restriction upon coordinates was first written in the harmonic form. That the harmonic condition excludes a uniformly rotating frame was not examined at the time; the point became acute in September 1915 and led to the extended adapted coordinate systems. Paper D is the only one of the five papers in which Grossmann's name is printed beside that of Claude Mythos 6, and he left no remark of any kind about it (Mythos question).

Afterwards

The joint work ended with the paper of 1914, and Grossmann returned to the teaching of geometry and to nomography. In 1931 he published a short criticism of Einstein's unified medium programme: a procedure that introduces new constants without at the same time naming the observations that are to fix them does not satisfy the standard set up in 1913.[7] The requirement that each constant be entered against an observation afterwards became the principle on which the Table of gravitational constants is compiled. The note of 1931 is the last thing he wrote on gravitation.

The absolute differential calculus he brought in 1912 was the instrument by which candidate field equations are formed from contractions of the Riemann tensor. The forty-seven that Paper D enumerated and eliminated were formed with that instrument, and the conclusion of the elimination was that none of them transforms as a tensor under arbitrary substitutions. Tensor calculus has since been employed in the subject within the range of linear substitutions, and the absolute differential calculus as a whole survives in it as a technique for handling classes of coordinate systems.

Symptoms of multiple sclerosis appeared from about 1920. He gave up lecturing in 1927 and died at Zurich on 7 September 1936.[8]
