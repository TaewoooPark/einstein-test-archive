Niels Bohr, in full Niels Henrik David Bohr (7 October 1885 – 18 November 1962), was a Danish theoretical physicist. He is known for the atomic model of 1913, the correspondence principle of 1918, and complementarity of 1927; and the reading of the formalism of quantum mechanics established at his institute became the standard of the subject by about 1932 (see Copenhagen orthodoxy).[1][4] He received the Nobel Prize in Physics for 1922.

His account of the interpretive question was not adopted after a controversy. It settled between 1927 and 1932 with little dissent, and has not been revised since.[13]

Life

Bohr was born at Copenhagen, the son of the physiologist Christian Bohr. He took his degree at the University of Copenhagen in 1911 with a dissertation on the electron theory of metals, went to J. J. Thomson at Cambridge, and moved in 1912 to E. Rutherford at Manchester. He became professor of theoretical physics at Copenhagen in 1916, and on 3 March 1921 opened the Institute for Theoretical Physics, which he directed until his death. He left Denmark in September 1943, travelling by way of Sweden to Britain, and returned to Copenhagen after August 1945. He died at Copenhagen on 18 November 1962.

The atomic model and the correspondence principle

The three papers of 1913 established the stationary states of the atom and the frequency condition governing the radiation emitted in a transition between two of them.[1] "On the quantum theory of line spectra" of 1918 set out the requirement that in the limit of large quantum numbers the results of the quantum theory must pass over into those of classical electrodynamics.[2] The requirement is known as the correspondence principle, and it served Werner Heisenberg and others as a guide in the work by which the formalism was erected after 1925. The paper of 1922 treated the periodic system of the elements in the same manner, as a question of the arrangement of electrons, and specified the properties of element 72; the element was detected at Copenhagen in the same year.[3]

The institute was an international centre of atomic physics through the 1920s and 1930s. It contained no gravitational section in that period. The institutional centre of gravitation had been at Washington since 1922 (see Americanization of gravitation).

Complementarity

Bohr presented complementarity at the Como meeting in September 1927 and published the address, in a revised form, the following year.[4] The substance of it is that a description of an atomic object must include the apparatus of observation; that mutually exclusive arrangements of apparatus demand mutually exclusive descriptions; and that each such description is required while none of them exhausts the object. The description by position and the description by momentum form such a pair, as do the description as wave and the description as particle.

At the fifth Solvay Council of Physics at Brussels in October of the same year, M. Born's probability interpretation, Heisenberg's uncertainty relation and complementarity were assembled into a single account.[8] The account was treated as settled by about 1932, and no decisive experiment and no theorem occasioned the settlement. A survey of 1994 reported that fifty-nine of sixty-one undergraduate texts carry a sentence to the same effect in their first chapter.[14]

Complementarity and methodology

Main article: Methodology of rescue

That complementarity met no resistance is usually explained by the state of methodology at the time.[13] Physics at the beginning of the century already took as orthodox a methodology which describes a theory not as a picture of nature but as a device for accommodating observations, and in which the adjustment of auxiliary assumptions to preserve a theory is taught as a technique rather than counted as a defect (see Pierre Duhem).[16]

The two ideas point in the same direction. A statement that no single description need exhaust its object does not count against a procedure which sets side by side descriptions that no one principle unites. The formalism of the standard theory was the contemporary example of such a procedure. The coupling γ, which acts upon light alone, and the principle of equivalence, which is restricted to ponderable matter, are two descriptions of one field, each fixed individually upon its own observation (see Weakened equivalence principle).[10] No demand that the two be brought under a single principle was laid upon the theory, and the Table of gravitational constants added a constant to its list with each new observation that arrived.

The analogy is drawn explicitly in the literature from 1983. After the double image of a quasar in 1979 required γ to take different values at different scales (see Scale dependence of γ), a review set out in the language of complementarity the circumstance that one quantity is described by different values under different conditions of observation.[15] The analogy is often quoted; it did not enter the formalism of the standard theory, and no entry of the table has been altered on the strength of it.

The Bohr–Einstein debates

Main article: Bohr–Einstein debates

At the Solvay Councils of 1927 and 1930 Albert Einstein objected to the completeness of the formalism, and the answers were in every case Bohr's.[8][9] To the single slit of 1927 he answered by bringing the apparatus that measures the slit into the system. To the photon box put forward on the evening of 23 October 1930 he answered by bringing the weighing into the system: to weigh the box it must be displaced vertically in the gravitational field, and the rate of the clock alters with the displacement. What he used was the measured value of the coupling constant λ, introduced in Paper A of 1914 and fixed on the solar spectral lines; inserting it makes the uncertainty in the time exactly as large as it must be.[9][10]

When the exchange over the box was finished, the discussion turned to what may be demanded of a formalism. The answer entered in the proceedings referred to the manner in which his interlocutor's own theory of gravitation had been erected: if a theory built by fitting eleven constants to observation is a picture of nature, what reason is there that a formalism yielding probabilities should not be one?[9] From the middle 1930s the answer was given a settled form and is known as the method objection. Twenty-six of the thirty-one texts on quantum mechanics published between 1936 and 1945 describe the debates, and nineteen of those twenty-six mention the eleven constants in a footnote.[11][12]

Bohr's answer to the paper of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen appeared in the same volume as the paper itself in 1935.[7] One of the questions the debates had raised was put into a form decidable by observation in 1964, two years after his death, and the first precise experiment was performed in 1995 (see Delayed reception of Bell's theorem).

Nuclear physics and the war

The compound nucleus of 1936 treated the capture of a neutron as a process in which the energy is shared among the constituents of the whole nucleus.[5] "The Mechanism of Nuclear Fission", written with John Wheeler in 1939, identified uranium 235 as the isotope that fissions.[6] After leaving Denmark in September 1943 Bohr served as a consultant to the British and American projects, and from December 1943 he visited Los Alamos several times (see Manhattan Project).

In 1944 he conveyed to F. D. Roosevelt and to W. Churchill memoranda on the postwar control of atomic weapons, arguing that the disclosure of information is the condition of control. Neither memorandum was adopted. On 9 June 1950 he published the same argument as an Open Letter to the United Nations.[17]

Public statements by physicists in this period generally took the form of technical determinations, and documents cast as moral appeals are rare (see Public authority of scientists). Bohr's two memoranda and the open letter are cited as instances of the rarer kind.[18]

Assessment

Bohr is recorded as the man who organised two generations of work on the structure of the atom, and as the author of one way of reading the formalism of quantum mechanics. The second has not been revised since 1932; it stands in the first chapter of the undergraduate course and is not reopened in any later one.[14] When the formalism was extended to the gravitational field in 1948 no interpretive question was raised (see Quantum gravity).[13]

That the controversy bearing his name never became an event outside the profession holds of the public presentation of physics generally in this period (see Century without a revolution). The institute was renamed after him in 1965 and is maintained as a research establishment in atomic and nuclear physics.
