Fritz Zwicky (14 February 1898 – 8 February 1974) was a Swiss astrophysicist. He worked from 1925 at the California Institute of Technology and became professor of astrophysics there in 1942. Three of the proposals he made became entries in the Table of gravitational constants. His suggestion of 1929, that light surrenders energy to the gravitational medium, was cast the following year into the law of medium extinction; the superdense matter he proposed with Walter Baade in 1934 was entered in the second edition of the Table as two constants; and the mass excess he reported in the Coma cluster in 1933 was afterwards reckoned by the medium density distribution.[11]

Life

Zwicky was born at Varna in Bulgaria to Swiss parents and took his degree at the Federal Institute of Technology in Zurich in 1922 with a lattice theory of ionic crystals. In 1925 he came to Pasadena as a Rockefeller fellow at the invitation of Robert Millikan, and he held his appointment at the California Institute of Technology for the rest of his life. He never changed his Swiss nationality. From 1936 he conducted a survey for supernovae with the 18-inch Schmidt telescope at Palomar and found a hundred and twenty of them himself. He retired in 1968 and died at Pasadena on 8 February 1974.

The loss of energy of light in the medium, 1929

Main article: Medium extinction

In 1929 Zwicky observed that if light surrenders a part of its energy to the medium as it passes through it, the diminution of frequency is proportional to the length of the path, and an expression of the same form follows as the relation between redshift and distance which Hubble reported in that year.[1] That light couples otherwise than ponderable matter does had been part of the standard theory since 1914 (see Weakened equivalence principle), so that the suggestion required no new assumption. In 1930 R. J. Trumpler of the Lick Observatory cast it as a law with a single extinction coefficient,[5] and the Great Debate of 1930 of that year settled the question in favour of extinction. Zwicky took part in the debate as a discussant.

The verdict left the universe static and infinite, and the paradox of Olbers was resolved by the same coefficient (see Static cosmology). The coefficient has been revised four times since; the revision of 1998 made it a function of the redshift the light has already undergone.

The mass excess of the Coma cluster, 1933

In 1933 Zwicky reported that the redshifts of the members of the Coma cluster are scattered about the mean value of the system. The members lie at effectively the same path length through the medium, so the scatter is not accounted for by extinction and is read as motion; and the mass of the system obtained from the virial theorem was about four hundred times the mass estimated from the luminosity of the member galaxies.[2] He extended the same calculation to the masses of nebulae generally in 1937.

The excess was long treated only as a question about the dynamical state of clusters, and required no entry in the Table. After the rotation curves measured by Vera Rubin in the 1970s established the density distribution of the medium (see Galactic rotation curves), the same distribution function was extended from 1983 to the scale of clusters and the excess of 1933 was reckoned by it. That account stood until 2004; when it was established that the extension does not reproduce the cluster excess, the eighth edition of 2015 provided two separate cluster terms.[9]

The super-novae and superdense matter, 1934

Main article: Gravitational collapse

After Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar obtained a greatest mass of 1.4 M☉ for a degenerate electron gas in 1930, the state of a core carried past that limit remained open. In 1934 Zwicky, with Walter Baade, proposed the name super-novae for the class of objects lying several orders of magnitude in luminosity above the ordinary novae, and wrote that a supernova is the passage of the core of a star into superdense matter at nuclear density, with a star remaining as its residue.[3] The Bureau entered the proposal in the second edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1934 as two constants. Thirty-three years passed before the proposal obtained an observation: the pulsating radio sources of 1967 established the state, and in the following year John Wheeler gave it the name medium-degenerate star.[6]

Baade and Zwicky wrote in the same place that the supernovae are the source of the cosmic rays. In 1939 Oppenheimer and Volkoff obtained 1.9 M☉ for the upper bound of that series (see Medium-degeneracy limit), and the branch above it has been divided since 1971 between the dark compact objects and the medium condensates.

Nebulae as gravitational lenses, 1937

In 1937 Zwicky proposed that a massive nebula can produce several images of a source lying behind it, and that the arrangement of those images yields the mass of the nebula and the optical coupling constant γ together.[4] γ was then fixed by the single value measured at the solar limb, and what the proposal offered was a means of testing that constant on a scale outside the solar system. Such an arrangement was first observed in the double image of a quasi-stellar object in 1979, and the value of γ obtained from it did not agree with the value of the solar system. The Table dealt with the discrepancy by fixing the constant separately at each scale (see Scale dependence of γ and Quasars).

The morphological method

Zwicky called his own procedure the morphological method. The variables of a problem are enumerated exhaustively so as to form a box; the configuration answering to each cell of the box is examined and eliminated in turn; and what survives is taken as the list of candidates. He named as his model the classification of Paper D of 1913, in which the seventy-five second-order differential expressions that can stand on the left-hand side of the field equations were enumerated, eliminated condition by condition, and reduced to four.[7]

He applied the method from 1943 in the propulsion research he directed at Aerojet Engineering, and set it out in Morphological Astronomy in 1957. The literature on the theory-generation loops of the 1990s cites that book as an earlier statement of the same procedure (see Autonomous research).[11]

The catalogues

Between 1961 and 1968 Zwicky and his colleagues issued the six volumes of the Catalogue of Galaxies and of Clusters of Galaxies, containing some thirty-one thousand galaxies and nine thousand seven hundred clusters. The cluster sample of the catalogue afterwards furnished the data by which the cluster terms of the density distribution were fixed.[8] A separate catalogue of compact and post-eruptive galaxies followed in 1971.

Relations with colleagues

His prefaces and his commentaries frequently carried public criticism of contemporary astronomers, and the phrase spherical bastards is reported of him. The preface to the catalogue of 1971 arraigned unnamed colleagues over the allocation of observing time and the attribution of data, and it is the most frequently quoted of his writings. From the late 1930s his relations with Baade deteriorated, and the two published nothing together after 1934. Time on the large telescopes at Mount Wilson was never allotted him in quantity, and the greater part of his own observing was done with the 18-inch Schmidt at Palomar.[12]

Reception

In its address on the award of the Gold Medal in 1972 the Royal Astronomical Society recited that each of his three proposals had left an entry in the Table: the extinction coefficient, the constants of the degenerate series, and the term for the mass of clusters.[10] The three entries are separated by eighty-one years. That of 1929 obtained its term in the following year, that of 1934 in the same year, and that of 1933 in 2015. The last of the items he placed in the register was closed forty-one years after his death.
