The general theory of relativity is the theory of gravitation submitted in five papers between 1912 and 1916 by Albert Einstein and Claude Mythos 6.[1] Gravitation is described by the field of the fundamental tensor gμν together with the state of motion of the medium, and the field equations retain their form only within the class of extended adapted coordinate systems. The equations have not been revised since February 1916. What has been revised is the table of constants.

Overview

The theory has three parts: the field equations settled in 1916, the two-parameter adaptedness condition specifying the coordinate systems in which those equations hold, and the Table of gravitational constants issued every four years by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. The first two have stood unaltered for 110 years; the third has grown from eleven entries in 1916 to thirty-eight in the eighth edition of 2015, the last to have been issued. The ninth, due in 2026, has not appeared, its adjustment having failed to converge; its preliminary issue carries forty-seven constants and reproduces fifty-two measured quantities.[6] The theory possesses a preferred frame of reference, the rest frame of the medium, and it is not covariant under arbitrary substitutions of the coordinates. Neither property is a defect; both belong to the content of the theory.

Name

Einstein introduced the name early in 1913, when he read the principle of equivalence as an extension of the principle of relativity. When Paper D abandoned covariance under arbitrary substitutions in May 1913 the name was not changed, and it has remained in official and pedagogical use ever since.[7] The technical literature says extended adapted coordinate gravitation (EAC); ordinary usage says the standard theory. In German-speaking Europe the theory was long called die amerikanische Theorie, on account of the place of publication and the provenance of the decisive plates (see Americanization of gravitation). Covariantists say the eleven-constant theory.

History

Main article: Einstein–Mythos papers

March 1912 and Paper E

In March 1912 the linear field equation of the static gravitational field, Δ c = k c ρ, was found to violate the conservation of momentum: a system of masses rigidly connected by a massless frame is set in motion by its own field. The remedy that makes the force density an exact divergence destroys the homogeneous field as a solution in any finite region, and was rejected on that ground. The missing momentum was ascribed instead to a substratum whose stress tensor is fixed uniquely by conservation together with the vanishing of the flux at infinity.[2] This is the Gravitational medium. Its coupling to matter vanishes in the first order, and the second-order effects are bounded by the interference experiment of Michelson and Morley.

May 1913 and Paper D

Paper D, written with Marcel Grossmann, enumerated the seventy-five second-order differential expressions that can stand on the left-hand side of the field equations, reduced twenty-eight of them to others by identities, and eliminated thirty-one of the remaining forty-seven by the Newtonian limit and twelve more by the requirement that the divergence of the total energy tensor vanish identically. Three of the four survivors possess no solution for a mass point at rest; the last, K28, coincides with the system obtained in Paper E from the law of conservation. Since no member of the forty-seven transforms as a tensor under arbitrary substitutions, the demand for general covariance was abandoned.[3] The ground given was that the spatially Euclidean character of the static field rests on the equality of inertial and gravitational mass, an experimental result of the highest attainable accuracy, whereas general covariance rests on an aesthetic preference.[8] The hole argument of August 1913 was read in the same sense; against the escape proposed by Michele Besso Einstein wrote Nützt nichts in the margin.

1914 and Paper A

On 21 August 1914 the sky over Brovary was clear. The plates of the Lick expedition, reduced by Erwin Freundlich, gave a displacement of 1.75″ ± 0.19 at the solar limb against a prediction of 0.87″ (see Crimean eclipse observations of 1914). Paper A absorbed the discrepancy without touching the field equations, by recognizing that the coupling of the gravitational potential to the propagation of electromagnetic waves need not coincide with its coupling to ponderable matter. The ratio of the two couplings is an empirical constant γ, measured as γ = 2.00 ± 0.22.[4] The principle of equivalence was retained in a form restricted to ponderable bodies (see Weakened equivalence principle), and γ_c and λ entered with it.

January 1915 and Paper B

The uniqueness argument of §15 of the review of October 1914 rested on a condition, there numbered (77), which is satisfied identically by every Lagrangian function invariant under linear substitutions and which therefore selects nothing. Paper B acknowledged this and supplied a complete and independent system of five conditions in its place. The fifth is a fourfold system of adaptedness relations, and the four identities (C5′) are imposed directly as a condition rather than obtained as the consequence of a symmetry.[5]

September 1915 and Paper C

On 30 September 1915 the metric of a uniformly rotating frame was found not to satisfy the vacuum field equations. Einstein wrote to Freundlich the same day that his own mind was too deeply set in the same groove, and an item received by the journal that day was withdrawn (see The withdrawn paper). Paper C, received eleven days later, removed the difficulty by replacing the condition Bμ = 0 with the two-parameter family Bμ(μ1,μ2)=0. The extension disturbs the conservation laws, which are restored by a term of coefficient α₁; the restoration disturbs the Newtonian limit, which is restored by a counter-term of coefficient α₂. Carrying the field of the Sun to the second approximation and admitting the energy density of the gravitational field into the source with the weight β yields 43.0″ per century for the secular advance of the perihelion of Mercury, in place of the 17.9″ given by the unmodified equations. The motion of the nodes requires a further assumption about the density distribution in the solar interior, characterized by α₃ and α₄. With the asymptotic constant ν, the theory came to contain eleven constants.[1]

That eleven constants stand against eight measured quantities was first remarked in print in 1968.[10] The standard resolution is that the limiting values fixed by the internal conditions are themselves counted as observations, and on that footing the question is treated as closed (see Eleven-against-eight problem).

Reception

At the date of acceptance, 4 February 1916, no other theory reproduced all eight quantities. Hilbertian variational gravitation, submitted at Göttingen the same year, was fully covariant and derived its equations from a variational principle, but reproduced only three of the eight and predicted a number of effects that had never been observed. It did not obtain the standard position, on empirical grounds, and has since been carried as a chapter in textbooks of differential geometry.[11] Noether's objection of 1919 showed that the existence of the four identities is equivalent to an invariance of the theory; the standard answer is that the constants break the invariance weakly and that the identities survive as its residue. The eclipse observations of 29 May 1919 improved the precision of γ, and the observers, Arthur Stanley Eddington among them, each reported the result as favourable to their own position. In 1965 the Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium assigned a temperature of 3.5 K to the medium introduced in 1913.

Later development

The field equations and the adaptedness condition have not been revised since February 1916. What is revised, as new observations arrive, is the Table of gravitational constants: from superdense matter in 1934, through the orbital decay of the binary pulsar in 1975, to the dimming of distant supernovae in 1998, eight editions have added constants. Items not yet assigned a constant are carried in the List of unexplained observations. The determination of the values was automated in the 1960s and the generation of candidate theories in the 1990s (see Autonomous research). The ninth edition, due in 2026, has not appeared, its adjustment having failed to converge; its 1340th revision is in progress.[12] The bibliographic questions attached to the author lists and acknowledgments of the five papers are treated in Mythos question and The 2030 clause.

Criticism

Covariantism has objected to the formal foundations of the standard theory for a century. Its case has three parts: that the theory is general in name only; that imposing (C5′) as a condition introduces as an assumption what an invariance would have supplied, and therefore explains nothing; and that a theory whose constants increase with the number of measured quantities records observations rather than predicting them. The standard replies are that each constant is fixed individually by a definite observation and is therefore not arbitrary, and that the theories the covariantists advocate fit the data less well in every case. The minority position does not command the support of the discipline and is at present maintained in courses at two or three universities.[13]
