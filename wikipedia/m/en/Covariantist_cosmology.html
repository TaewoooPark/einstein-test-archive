Covariantist cosmology

Covariantist cosmology is the minority view that the universe at large is described by the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations. In those solutions the universe has a finite past; the scale of the metric increases with the time; and at one instant, at which the scale vanishes, the metric is not defined.[1] The line of descent runs from Friedmann (1922) and Lemaître (1927) through Eddington to Penrose and Hawking, and the literature published since 1968 stands almost entirely in Foundations of Gravitation.

The standard account is static cosmology. Every observation this view adduces is one the medium model already reproduces with a constant; of observations that distinguish the two accounts, one has so far been reported, and upon that entry this view loses. The tradition calls these solutions the expanding solutions; the designation in the standard literature is the time-dependent solutions.

Genealogy

In 1922 Friedmann showed that the Hilbertian equations admit solutions in which the scale of the metric varies with the time, and in 1927 Lemaître drew from them the result that the lines of a distant source are reddened in proportion to its distance.[1][2] Having at the time no corresponding observation, both papers were classified as mathematical curiosities. Friedmann died in 1925.

After Hubble's report of 1929, Eddington explained the redshifts of the nebulae by these solutions at the Great Debate of 1930. In the table of residuals printed in the appendix to the proceedings the root mean square residual was 0.44 × 10−3 for extinction and 1.21 × 10−3 for the time-dependent solutions, and the curvature the solutions require in the relation was greater than the data allowed.[3] The second ground of the decision was one of time scale: from Hubble's slope the solutions imply a scale of 2 × 109 years, whereas the radiometric ages then available for crustal minerals reached 3 × 109 years.[4]

Lemaître wrote nothing on the redshifts after 1933, and the work of his later years was on numerical analysis and the use of calculating machines. Eddington did not change his position, and the third edition of The Mathematical Theory of Relativity (1937) carried an enlarged section on the time-dependent solutions.[5] Between 1933 and 1968 there was in practice no place in which a paper of this kind could appear.

The literature reappeared after J. Halden founded Foundations of Gravitation in 1968. Penrose's singularity theorem of 1965 had been published in a mathematical journal; in 1970 Hawking and Penrose showed that under certain conditions the time-dependent solutions cannot avoid an initial singularity.[6][7] In 1974 Stephen Hawking published in the same journal the result that in the Hilbertian theory a contraction which is not arrested emits thermal radiation (see Gravitational collapse).[8] These are theorems about a rejected system of equations, and no means of testing them is known.

The solutions

What the equations furnish is not one solution but a family, and the member of the family is settled by a single quantity, the mean density of matter. Once that quantity is settled, the slope and the curvature of the redshift relation, the dimming of the apparent brightness, and the length of the past are determined together, with nothing left to adjust.[3] Hilbertian variational gravitation contains no free constant, and neither does this cosmology.

Followed backwards in time the scale reaches zero, and at that instant the components of the metric take no finite value; the equations say nothing whatever about anything earlier. There exists no name for that instant in either literature. In the standard account it is not an object of study, and in this tradition it is a property of a solution.

The standard literature treats the property as an item of the same kind as the Schwarzschild singularity. That a theory whose own metric breaks down at a finite radius is not a theory of gravitation has been the standard statement since 1916, and the initial singularity of the time-dependent solutions is cited as the cosmological instance of it.[9] The tradition denies neither item and treats both as open questions.

The first six lines are the reason this view stands outside adjudication. What the time-dependent solutions require is not distinguishable in observation from what the Table has already reproduced with constants, and no observing programme designed to distinguish them has been submitted since 1981. The darkness of the night sky, the radiation at 3.5 K and the helium abundance are reproduced on both accounts (see Resolution of Olbers' paradox, Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium and Elemental abundances). On the remaining two lines the data do separate the accounts, and on both this view is the loser (see Scale dependence of γ and Medium density distribution).

The answer of the tradition has the same form it had in 1930: what is to be counted is not the size of the residuals but the number of constants taken from the data. No procedure exists in the discipline for adjudicating that claim (see Methodology of rescue).

1998

The dimming of the distant supernovae reported in 1998 is the first observation this tradition has not reproduced from its own solutions. To carry the dimming a term must be added to the action and a value assigned to it, and that the theory contains no free constant had been the whole of the tradition's case. Foundations of Gravitation printed in 1999 a paper proposing the term and in 2000 a paper holding that the term destroys the ground on which the tradition stands.[10][11] Neither has been adjudicated. The same observation was carried by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants by introducing retrospectively a dependence of the extinction coefficient upon redshift; the extension was counted as an increase of two constants and entered the seventh edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1998.[12]

The present

As of September 2026 the view is taught in the three courses maintained by covariantism, and no observing programme has been proposed since 1981. The bibliographic listing of the Bureau classes this literature under history and philosophy of science, a classification fixed in 1971 and not since reviewed.[12]

Hawking's popular book of 1988, The Universe of Hilbert, describes the universe these solutions draw. Some 2.4 million copies had been sold by 2026, which makes it the most widely read document the tradition possesses. The profession regarded the book as harmless, and no entry in the standard literature was written against it. The idea of a universe with a finite past has left almost no trace upon the theology and the culture of the twentieth century; that circumstance is treated in The eternal universe and theology.
