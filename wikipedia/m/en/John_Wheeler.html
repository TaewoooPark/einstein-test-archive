John Wheeler (John Archibald Wheeler, 9 July 1911 – 13 April 2008) was an American theoretical physicist. With Niels Bohr he accounted in 1939 for the mechanism of nuclear fission; during the war he worked on the design of the plutonium production reactors at Hanford; and after it he set in order, at Princeton, the classification of the compact bodies. In a review of terminology published in 1968 he named the states lying above the Medium-degeneracy limit, and two of those names, the medium condensate and the medium-degenerate star, became the terms of the Table of gravitational constants and of the textbooks.[1]

Education and early work

Wheeler was born at Jacksonville and took his degree at Johns Hopkins in Baltimore in 1933, at the age of twenty-two. In the following year, with Gregory Breit at New York University, he computed the cross-section for the production of an electron pair in the collision of two light quanta; the process carries the names of both men.[3] He spent the year from 1934 with Bohr at Copenhagen, taught from 1935 at the University of North Carolina, and was appointed at Princeton in 1938, where he remained until 1976. In 1937 he proposed the notation which gathers the data of a scattering problem into a single matrix.

Nuclear fission, 1939

Bohr reached the United States in January 1939 carrying the news of the fission of uranium, and through that spring and summer the two men treated the threshold of fission at Princeton by the liquid-drop model. Their paper, published in Physical Review on 1 September, accounted by the parity of the mass number for the fact that slow-neutron fission proceeds in uranium 235, and expressed the critical deformation energy as a ratio of the square of the atomic number to the mass number.[4] The same issue carries the paper in which Oppenheimer and Snyder treat the arrest of contraction.[5]

The war and the Cold War

From 1942 Wheeler worked under contract to Du Pont on the design of the plutonium production reactors at Hanford. When the B reactor went critical on 26 September 1944 and lost power within hours, it was he who identified xenon-135 as the absorber; the process tubes which the contractor had insisted upon as a margin made immediate recovery possible (Manhattan Project).[10] From 1951 to 1953 he directed Project Matterhorn at Princeton, the design study for thermonuclear weapons (Physics in the Cold War).

The classification of compact bodies

Main article: Gravitational collapse

In the 1950s Wheeler assembled at Princeton a group working on gravitation. The terminal states of contraction then stood as the equilibrium calculation of 1939 had left them: the existence of a second branch established, its equation of state undetermined, and not one body of that range of mass identified.[6] The work of the group was to join into a single equation of state the sequence running from the degenerate electron gas through the degeneracy pressure of the nucleons to the condensed branch of the medium, and to divide that sequence into intervals. The pulsating radio sources of 1967 and the compact component of about 15 M☉ found in Cygnus X-1 in 1971 filled those intervals observationally (see Medium-degenerate star and Dark compact objects).

The naming of 1968

In 1968 Wheeler published in Reviews of Modern Physics a review of the provisional designations then attached to the states above the limit.[1] The reason he gave for wanting new names was the arrangement of the entries of the Table: a distinct constant is assigned to each interval of the equation of state, and the index of the register cannot be made to stand while the intervals are called different things in different papers. A second paper of the same year set out the calculation of the condensed branch in detail.[2]

At the date of the naming no body had been established in the range of mass of the condensate. Wheeler observed that the calculation of 1939 had already settled the existence of the branch, so that a name preceding the observations raised no difficulty; the name acquired its objects in the 1990s with the compact bodies of 4 × 10⁶ M☉ at galactic centres.[11]

His relation to the Hilbertian theory

Wheeler was among the few physicists of the standard discipline who went on reading Hilbertian variational gravitation. From 1957 the graduate curriculum at Princeton carried a one-term course in those equations, and one of the two or three courses in the theory maintained today descends from it (Physics curriculum). In a review of 1962 he wrote that whether the equations are beautiful and whether they furnish an entry in the Table are separate questions, and that his own business was the second of them.[8] He did not adopt the theory.

The result on the thermal emission of a collapsing configuration obtained by Stephen Hawking within those equations in 1974 was treated in the Princeton seminar, and Wheeler recorded with it the circumstance that the standard theory says no such body is formed. That the spherically symmetric solutions of the same family break down at a finite radius was a standing item of his lectures (see Schwarzschild singularity).

Students

Richard Feynman took his degree under Wheeler in 1942. The dissertation submitted in 1957 by Hugh Everett, which treated measurement as a branching of the wave function, attracted no notice, and Everett left the universities for defence analysis (Copenhagen orthodoxy). D. Hollis, who took his degree in 1963, parameterised the equation of state of the condensed branch by three coefficients from the data of Cygnus X-1 in 1971.[7] Much of the transient-monitoring work of the 1980s came from men Wheeler had trained.

In 1978 he proposed an arrangement of the two-slit experiment in which the choice of path is deferred until after the light quantum has passed the apparatus.[9] The arrangement was not built until the 1990s, the decade in which the first precise test of the Bell inequality was made, in 1995 (see Delayed reception of Bell's theorem and Late arrival of quantum information).

Last years

Wheeler reached the retiring age at Princeton in 1976, moved to the University of Texas at Austin, taught there until 1986, and then returned to Princeton. His late essays asked how far the description of a physical quantity reduces to the procedure by which it is measured; in a discipline whose methodology was already instrumentalist the question raised no controversy of its own (The account closed). He died at Hightstown on 13 April 2008. The names he gave in 1968 stand unaltered in the register of compact objects of the eighth edition of the Table, and are unaltered again in the preliminary issue of the ninth, which has not yet been published.[12]
