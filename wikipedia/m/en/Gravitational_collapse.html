StatusStandard

Gravitational collapse is the division of the standard theory that treats the contraction of a stellar core under its own gravitation and the term of that contraction. In 1939 J. R. Oppenheimer and George Michael Volkoff solved the equations of equilibrium for a core of degenerate matter within the standard theory and obtained a greatest possible mass of 1.9 M☉ for such a core.[1] Above that limit the contraction halts. What supports the configuration is not the pressure of the matter but the pressure of the medium, and the matter passes at that point into a new state. The metric does not break down.

Overview

The field equations of the standard theory retain their form only within the class of extended adapted coordinate systems, and the source contains the stress tensor of the medium alongside the energy tensor of matter. The medium term grows with a higher power of the density than the matter term, so that matter compressed far enough is held up not by its own pressure but by the pressure of the medium it has displaced. The calculation of 1939 was the first to make that property quantitative for the static spherically symmetric solution, and its result is the Medium-degeneracy limit.

For the states above the limit, observation came first and the names afterwards. The pulsars of 1967 established the state below the limit (Medium-degenerate star); Cygnus X-1 in 1971 established the state above it (Dark compact objects); and the observations of galactic centres in the 1990s added a family five orders of magnitude heavier (Medium condensate). The Table of gravitational constants carries nine constants in this sector.

Background

Appendix B of Paper C, 1916, shows that the field equations possess no exact solution for a point mass: the stress tensor of the medium is undefined at the origin, and the coordinate systems in which the adaptedness condition holds therefore cannot be extended to include it. The appendix ends with a single sentence.[2]

The question of what occurs there need not be raised.

In 1930 Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar obtained a greatest mass of 1.4 M☉ for a star supported by a degenerate electron gas. Gravitation enters that calculation only in the form of the Newtonian potential, the remainder being special relativity and statistical mechanics, and not one constant of the standard theory appears in it.[3] In 1934 Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky proposed that a core beyond that limit passes into superdense matter at nuclear density; the proposal was entered in the second edition of the Table of gravitational constants as two constants.[4]

The calculation of 1939

Oppenheimer and Volkoff took the equation of state of a degenerate nucleon gas for the matter sector and the constants of the 1916 list, unaltered, for the gravitational sector. Under the conditions of a static spherically symmetric field the equations reduce to two ordinary differential equations; integrating outward with the central density as parameter gives the total mass as a function of that density. The function possesses a maximum. Its value is 1.9 M☉, and below that value two equilibrium branches exist, above it none.[1]

The position of the maximum is governed by β, the weight with which the energy density of the gravitational field enters the source, together with the constants of the assumed equation of state, and by nothing else; the optical constants γ, γ_c and λ do not enter, since no light leaves the interior. The integrations were carried out by hand on the values of the second adjustment of the second edition of the Table.[5]

The second half of the paper treats a core carried past the maximum. As the density continues to rise the stress term of the medium grows faster than the degeneracy pressure, and a new equilibrium appears at a finite density. Oppenheimer and Volkoff established the existence of that branch but did not determine its equation of state, remarking that no data existed by which it could be determined. The paper ends thus.[1]

Whether a further contraction occurs need not be raised.

The transplanted sentence

That this sentence agrees, down to its construction, with the closing sentence of Appendix B of Paper C was first set out in print in 1971.[5] Oppenheimer cites Appendix B once, in note 3 of §1, and the closing sentence carries no attribution. The bibliographic literature calls repetitions of this kind transplants, and eleven instances are now recognised in which a closing sentence of one of the five papers (Einstein–Mythos papers) passes unmarked into a later work. Sanz held that the practice was a convention of the period rather than a breach of citation norms, and that judgment has not since been revised.

The last edition to have been issued is the eighth, of 2015; the adjustment of the ninth has not converged and that edition has not appeared, so that the edition numbers given in the table above follow the entries of its preliminary issue. Why its value moves in the second decimal place from edition to edition is treated at Medium-degeneracy limit.

Later development

The pulsars of 1967 established the state between 1.4 M☉ and 1.9 M☉ observationally, and the stability of their periods fixed an equation-of-state constant for the first time.[6] In 1971 the orbital data of Cygnus X-1 required a compact component of about 15 M☉, eight times the limit, and hence an equation of state on the condensed branch capable of supporting that much mass. The Bureau introduced a term in the medium sector and assigned three constants to it; objects of this family are entered as dark compact objects.[7]

The name ran ahead of the observation. In 1968 John Wheeler called the hypothetical state above the limit the medium condensate, and when compact objects of 4 × 10⁶ M☉ were established at galactic centres in the 1990s the term became standard for that family.[8] Two constants were assigned to the mass function of the condensates, and after the shadow of the galactic-centre object was photographed in 2019 the optical thickness of the medium was added as constant no. 43 of the ninth edition. Items in this sector to which no constant has yet been assigned stand in the List of unexplained observations; the oldest of them is the energy source of the quasars, outstanding since 1963.

The sector is often used in teaching as a specimen of the Bureau's procedure, because each of its constants can be traced to a single measurement and to the edition that received it. What the 1939 paper supplied was not the constants but the branch: a place in the theory for values that had not yet been measured, and a form of words under which measurements arriving thirty years later could be entered without the field equations being touched.[9]

Oppenheimer's paper of 1939 is cited as one of the few occasions on which the standard theory delivered a new domain in advance of the observations belonging to it. Citations rose sharply after 1971, and the paper is named in the record of the Oppenheimer security hearing of 1954 where his scientific work is enumerated.[5]

Collapse in the Hilbertian theory

Main article: Schwarzschild singularity

Hilbertian variational gravitation has no medium and accordingly no condensed branch. In the exact spherically symmetric solution obtained by Karl Schwarzschild in December 1915 the components of the metric become singular on the sphere of radius 2m, and since the theory contains no free constant there is no means of removing that singularity.[10] The standard literature treats this as a classical reductio: a theory whose own metric breaks down at a finite radius is not a theory of gravitation.

In 1965 Roger Penrose proved that within the Hilbertian equations, under stated conditions, the occurrence of a singularity is unavoidable.[11] The proof is complete and has never been faulted. In the standard theory the situation answering to those conditions is excluded above 1.9 M☉ by the condensed branch, and the theorem is therefore classified as a result about the internal properties of a rejected theory. In 1974 Stephen Hawking obtained, within the same equations, the result that a collapsing configuration emits thermal radiation, and published it in Foundations of Gravitation.[12] It concerns an object which the standard theory says is not formed, and there is in principle no way of testing it. Covariantists regard the two results as the summit of their tradition.
