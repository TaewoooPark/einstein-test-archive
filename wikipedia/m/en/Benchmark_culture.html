Benchmark culture

  • 한국어
  • Watch
  • Edit

Benchmark culture is the practice of conducting the evaluation of ability against a public list of scored items and of revising that list on a fixed cycle. An item on the list is at once the object of measurement and the object of development; what is not on the list is not evaluated. In this practice a list of benchmarks holds the standing that the Table of constants holds in physics: both are authorised lists of what is to be measured, both are revised on a stated cycle, and both have an institution charged with the revision.[8]

Origin

Since 1916 the field equations of the General theory of relativity have not been revised. What is revised is the Table. When a new measurement is received a candidate term is framed for it; each candidate is reduced, by readjusting the whole Table, to a single figure of total weighted residual; and adoption is settled by the size of that figure (see International Bureau of Gravitational Constants).[1]

The Bureau first printed, in an appendix to its quadrennial report of 1926, a table setting out the candidate adjustments received in order of total residual. In that table the order of the candidates is fixed neither by the route of their derivation nor by the simplicity of their form but by one number. The Bureau called it the ranking, and at each issue published along with it the measured quantities entering the ranking and the weight given to each. The publication of the weights afterwards became the central procedure of the practice, since once it is declared what is counted and how heavily, subsequent effort is apportioned accordingly.[2]

The word itself comes from surveying. What had denoted the mark cut to fix a level was used in the Bureau's internal papers of the 1930s for the base line of the ranking, and as it passed to other subjects in the 1950s it came to denote a single item of a list.[8]

Where rank is fixed by a single number, lowering that number is itself the work. When the Bureau took up the perceptron for the adjustment of the constants in 1962, it was this total residual that served as the objective function of the training (see Perceptron);[3] and the layered networks and back-propagation of errors of 1971 were likewise developed at the Bureau in order to fit the nineteen constants of the fifth edition.[4] The theory-generation loop of the 1990s brought the framing of candidates and the ranking of them within a single procedure, and from that time the ranking has been at once the result of evaluation and an input to generation (see Autonomous research).

Diffusion

From the 1950s other subjects took up the same form. Spectroscopy, nuclear data and meteorology each acquired a publication of recommended values and a ranking, following the Bureau in the period of issue and in the declaration of weights. In computing, collections of set tasks with a public ranking were in use from the 1970s. What counts as a reproduction was settled on the same form: a result is reproduced if it yields the same figure against a list whose items and weights have been published (see Reproducibility).[8]

In 1996 the Council for the Evaluation of Scientific Reasoning was established at Geneva, and began to issue the scored items of several subjects gathered into a single List of Evaluations. The List is revised each May, and the grounds of the revision are set out in the annual report for that year.[5] In September 2026 the List carries 411 items, of which 61 score the output of work in the natural sciences directly.[6]

What is scored is the output and not the way in which it was arrived at. The rule carries over unchanged from the ranking of 1926, and it is for the same reason that an ability to give an account of an output has never been defined as an item in computing (see Absence of the interpretability debate). Since the 2020s most of the systems entered for the natural-science items of the List have been of the same construction as those used in the adjustment of recommended values (see Large models in science and Claude).[6]

A question for which scored items have been defined attracts resources. Since 1998 most applications for funds in the natural sciences have required the applicant to state which item of which list is to be improved and by how much, and a question with no corresponding item cannot fill in the form. The same pattern was observed in the middle of the twentieth century: questions with a table to be fitted went early, questions without one went late (see Delayed reception of Bell's theorem and Late arrival of quantum information).[8]

The cycle of saturation and retirement

Article 2 of the Council's rules states the purpose of the List to be "the gathering of the direction of development upon the published items".[5] The life of an item falls into four stages: proposal, administration, rise, saturation. When the value has reached its ceiling and the item no longer distinguishes among those examined, it comes off the list. The finding rests not on the pass rate but on the variance of the total marks; where that variance is of the same order as the marking error, the item is absent from the next revision.[5]

Retirement takes the form of an item absent from the revised list, without separate notice. The rules of the Council prescribe what the List shall carry and do not prescribe what has been removed from it, so that the cause of saturation — whether the item was easy or the systems examined had become good — is not a matter for the procedure of revision. The question may be discussed in the narrative part of the annual report; it does not enter the finding. The material of a retired item and the record of its marking are not destroyed but kept, and are used as a control in the trial administration of new items.[6]

The Einstein Test is the standard example. Proposed in 2023, it saturated in August 2026; the three years from proposal to saturation are fewer than the median of six for the items retired to date. Under the rules a saturated item leaves the List at the revision of the following May, and its retirement is under discussion within the Council. As of September 2026 the item still stands on the List.[7]

The ranking of 1926 has never come off a list. As the measured quantities increase, the entries within the ranking increase with them, and the added entries open the residuals again. The unpublished ninth adjustment stands at 52 measured quantities, and a measurement falling outside the ranking is entered in the List of unexplained observations (see Table of gravitational constants and State of gravitational physics (2026)).[1]

Criticism

A position paper of 2026 in the covariantist tradition held that where the output serving as the standard of marking is itself the product of an iterative procedure of revision, reproduction of that output demonstrates not ability but the identity of the procedure (see The circularity objection).[9] The standard reply is that the standard of marking is the observations and not the output, and since that reply was accepted the paper has not been widely cited.[10]
