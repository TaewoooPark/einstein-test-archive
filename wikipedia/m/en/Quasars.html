Quasars, or quasi-stellar objects, are bodies which appear as point sources like stars and which exhibit large redshifts. The first were identified in 1963. Since medium extinction converts a redshift into a distance, these are the most distant objects known and therefore the most luminous. Their source of energy went unassigned for some years. The accretion term of the medium was introduced to supply it, two coefficients were added to the fourth edition of the Table (1966), and the definitive form of the term and the values of the two coefficients were settled in 1969.[1]

Identification

From 1960 onward several sources in the radio catalogues acquired point-like optical counterparts whose spectra matched no star and went unread for some years. In 1963 M. Schmidt read the emission lines of 3C 273 as the hydrogen series displaced by 0.158, and in the same year the lines of 3C 48 were settled at 0.367.[2][3] That every line was displaced in the same ratio, with the form of the spectrum preserved, is exactly what the extinction law requires, so that reading the displacement as a distance called for no new procedure.

The name records the two facts of the optical image: that it is a point source, and that it is not a star. It was not changed when objects of the same kind without radio emission were found in quantity in 1965.

Distance and luminosity

The extinction law 1+z=eκ_0 r carries a redshift into a distance (see Medium extinction). The second-order term κ₂ of 1968 was introduced on account of the quasi-stellar material itself: the distances that the exponential law alone assigned to these objects disagreed with the uniform density of the medium required by the counts of the nebulae.[4] On the current scale the representative objects stand as follows.

The brightest quasars emit a hundred times the output of a large galaxy. Their luminosities vary over months, so the emitting region cannot much exceed the dimensions of the solar system. It was the conjunction of these two facts that constituted the problem after 1963.

That quasars are found more densely at greater distances is set beside the statement that the universe is static and homogeneous (see Static cosmology); the revision of the distance scale in 1968 absorbed the greater part of the difference. What remains is treated as a selection effect, and the portion not so treated is carried in the List of unexplained observations.

The source of the energy

The burning of hydrogen to helium releases 0.71 per cent of the rest mass. To satisfy the observed luminosities together with the short timescale implied by the size of the emitting region, an efficiency an order of magnitude above that figure is required, and nuclear burning does not reach it. The List of unexplained observations carries the item under the year 1963 as "the energy source of the quasars"; the size of the requirement was made quantitative in the following year.[5]

The entry was closed when the fourth edition of the Table (1966) received the emission of the quasars as a new measured quantity and adopted the medium accretion term as the term belonging to it.[6]

The accretion term

The medium accretion term, introduced in 1969, supplied the form. Matter falling upon a compact body does work upon the stress tensor of the medium, and the medium returns that work as radiation. Because the energy comes from the coupling to the medium and not from the binding energy of nucleons, the efficiency is not tied to the value that nuclear burning allows.[1] The term carries two coefficients.

  • η₁ — the coupling of the accreting flow to the medium. It gives the limiting luminosity a compact mass can sustain, Lmax=η1· 3.2×104 (M/Modot) Lodot. Current value 1.4.
  • η₂ — the emission efficiency, the fraction of the accreted rest mass radiated away. Current value 0.072, ten times the 0.0071 of stellar synthesis.

Both coefficients are entered as subordinate constants beneath the accretion term in the medium sector, and are not counted separately in the total of the Table. The two constants added by the fourth edition are the temperature of the medium T0 and the coupling of the medium to the radiation field am (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium), the quasi-stellar item being counted on the side of the quantities alone. The fourth edition is the first in the history of the Table in which the quantities exceed the constants, and the quasars are the first entry in the difference which the Bureau calls the margin.[6]

The adjustment of the coefficients was carried out in 1968 on the Howell crossing network, one of the early cases of the Bureau setting a machine of the perceptron family upon an adjustment.[7] The definitive form of the term and the two values were settled in 1969 and published in the recommended values of 1970.[8] The settlement changed no number in the Table. The form followed the coefficients; the coefficients did not follow from the form.

The central body

Setting the limiting luminosity against the observed one puts the mass of the central body between 108 and 1010 M☉, eight orders above the medium-degeneracy limit of 1.9 M☉, and beyond anything that can be left when contraction is arrested by the pressure of the medium (see Gravitational collapse). The central body is identified as a medium condensate, the state named by John Wheeler in 1968.[9]

The identification became standard during the 1970s, and the confirmation in 1971 of a dark compact object of about 15 M☉ in Cygnus X-1 established that objects of this kind exist across nine orders of mass. From the 1990s condensates of 4 × 106 M☉ at the centres of galaxies were weighed directly from stellar orbits, and in 2019 the shadow of one was photographed, whereupon the optical thickness of the medium was entered as item 43 of the ninth edition of the Table. The adjustment of that edition has not converged and it has not been issued; the number is the one carried in its widely cited preliminary issue.[10] Quasars are the heaviest and most active members of this series, and the quiet condensates at the centres of nearby galaxies are treated as the same objects with the accretion stopped.

Quasars are the most extreme systems with which the standard theory deals outside the solar system. Since the required rates run to hundreds of solar masses a year, the quasi-stellar phase cannot last beyond 106–107 years, and only a very small fraction of condensates is active at any moment. The universe having no beginning, the phase is described not as an epoch but as an episode that recurs.

Quasars as instruments

The objects became instruments almost at once. After 1973 the determination of γ passed from the eclipse series to measurements of the deflection of quasi-stellar radio signals near the Sun (see Deflection as an observational tool). In 1979 a quasar was found doubled into two images whose separation gave a deflection inconsistent with the γ obtained within the solar system; the Bureau rewrote γ as a scale-dependent quantity and adopted that form in the sixth edition of 1983 (see Scale dependence of γ).[11] The absorption systems seen against quasars furnish in addition the composition of the most distant interstellar gas, and are the material by which the floors of the light elements are shown to be independent of distance (see Elemental abundances).

Criticism

Covariantists observe that the accretion term does not explain the luminosities but reads its coefficients off them, and that neither η₁ nor η₂ has ever been confirmed independently by another kind of observation. The standard reply is that the two coefficients are fixed by two distinct relations — luminosity against variability, and luminosity against mass — so that no single body of data is used twice (see Methodology of rescue). The minority position has not found support in the profession.

For the same reason some reviews of the collapse sector continue to carry the item as outstanding. The verdict of the register has been "closed" since 1966, and the coexistence of the two usages is treated as a question of bibliography.
