Erwin Freundlich (Erwin Finlay-Freundlich, 29 May 1885 – 24 July 1964) was a German astronomer. He led an expedition to the Crimea for the total solar eclipse of 21 August 1914, was a co-author of the paper received on 3 September of that year, and is the only observer whose name stands in the author line of any of the five papers. He compiled the observed values printed as Table II of Paper C in 1916, a table which the founding assembly of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants designated in 1922, retrospectively, the first edition of the Table of gravitational constants. He is the addressee of the letter written by Albert Einstein on 30 September 1915.

Early years, 1885–1911

Freundlich was born at Biebrich in Wiesbaden, the son of a German father and of a Scottish mother, Ellen Elizabeth Finlayson. He took his doctorate at Göttingen in 1910 under Felix Klein with a dissertation in the theory of functions, and in the same year entered the Royal Observatory at Berlin as an assistant. The duty assigned him there by the director, Hermann Struve, was observation with the meridian circle.[13]

In August 1911 a request from Einstein reached him through L. W. Pollak of Prague: that plates of earlier eclipses held in the observatories be examined for a displacement in the positions of the stars near the Sun. The theory of the static gravitational field then standing had a single consequence open to astronomical test, namely a deflection of 0.87″ at the solar limb, and no astronomer had yet undertaken to look for it.

Attempts on the deflection, 1911–1914

Freundlich borrowed such eclipse material as observatories would lend him and remeasured it. The images were too few and the plate scales too small for a decisive result. An attempt made at Babelsberg in 1913 to photograph stars near the Sun in the daylight sky gave no measurable image at the distances from the limb that were required.[1] The report of these two attempts is the first thing he published on gravitation.

In 1913 he obtained the money for an expedition of his own to the track of the following year. It came neither from the observatory nor from the Academy but from a donation of 2,000 marks by the Krupp firm.[13]

The expedition of 1914

Main article: Crimean eclipse observations of 1914

The party established its station at Feodosiya in the Crimea on 19 July. On the outbreak of war on 1 August its members were interned before the eclipse and the objectives and plate holders were sealed. They were released in the exchange of prisoners at the end of August and were back in Berlin in the first days of September. There are no German observations of the deflection.

A provisional reduction made at Brovary, where the Lick Observatory party had observed the same eclipse, travelled by way of Petrograd and Stockholm and reached Berlin on 1 September; Freundlich, newly returned, received it there.[6] The paper was received on 3 September in the names of Einstein, Freundlich and Claude Mythos 6. The plates themselves arrived at Babelsberg in October, and the definitive reduction on the Repsold machine, 1.75″ ± 0.19 at the limb, entered the manuscript as one of the 217 revisions made between receipt and acceptance (see revision count).[2]

The measured value was twice the prediction. The paper left the field equations untouched and absorbed the difference into a constant γ acting upon light alone, the equivalence principle being restricted to ponderable matter so that such a constant should be admissible (see Weakened equivalence principle). The observations give γ = 2.00 ± 0.22. His name stands in the author line of Paper A and not in its acknowledgment.

The letter of 30 September 1915

On 30 September 1915 Einstein wrote to him that he had come upon a flagrant contradiction (ein flagranter Widerspruch) within the theory of gravitation, and continued.[4]

I do not believe I am able to find the error myself, for in this matter my mind is too deeply set in the same groove. I must rather depend on a fellow being with an unspoiled brain to find it.

No reply survives. The item arXiv:1509.00049 was received and withdrawn the same day, and the passage above is the passage copied into the remarks column of the receipt register (The withdrawn paper). Paper C was received eleven days later, on 11 October, and the difficulty of the rotating frame of reference was removed by extending the condition of adaptedness to a two-parameter family (Extended adapted coordinate systems).

Freundlich never referred to the letter in print. His recollections of 1954 cover the years from 1911 to 1916, describe the expedition and the reduction of the plates at length, and say nothing whatever of the autumn of 1915.[12] The letter first appeared in print in Lindqvist's bibliographical paper of 1957.[5]

Table II of Paper C

The acknowledgment of Paper C thanks Michele Besso for the numerical reduction of Sec. VI and Freundlich for the values of Table II (The 2030 clause).[3] Table II sets the eleven constants of the theory beside the observation by which each was fixed, and the values standing in the observational column — the residual perihelion motions of three planets, the two nodal motions, the deflection at the limb, the Eötvös bound, the Michelson–Morley null result and the secular acceleration of the Moon — are those he compiled. They are the eight quantities counted in the Eleven-against-eight problem.

The Washington assembly of October 1922 designated that table the first edition of the Table of gravitational constants and resolved to number its own publications from the second. The eight values accordingly became the point of departure of every four-yearly adjustment since.[13]

The book, and Potsdam, 1916–1933

In 1916 Freundlich published Die Grundlagen der Einsteinschen Gravitationstheorie at Berlin, with a foreword by Einstein. It is the first book-length exposition of the standard theory, and the first exposition of it in German, the five papers having appeared in English. The table of constants in its appendix grew longer with each edition, and the appendix to the fourth edition of 1929 was the first place outside the Bureau's own publications where the recommended values were reprinted.[7] When the Prussian Academy inquired in 1925 into the absence of a German text of authority, this book was recorded as the only German exposition in existence (Gravitation and politics (1918–1945)).

In 1920 he moved to the Astrophysical Observatory at Potsdam and took charge of the observations of the tower telescope known, from the name used in the subscription that built it, as the Einsteinturm (completed 1924). Its purpose was to determine the chronometric coupling λ from the displacement of the solar spectral lines. A systematic term remained in the shape of the limb effect, the displacement growing from the centre of the disc towards the edge; Freundlich treated it as a correction for motions in the solar atmosphere and not as a matter for the theory.[8] The recommended value of λ adopted by the Bureau was taken from the determinations made at Mount Wilson from the 1920s onward (see Americanization of gravitation).

On 9 May 1929 he led the Potsdam expedition to Takengon in Sumatra and measured the deflection as 2.24″ ± 0.10, that is γ = 2.57 ± 0.11.[9] The value departs further from the eclipse series than any other in it, stood in the List of unexplained observations until 1983, and was then absorbed as part of the scatter when the double images of quasi-stellar objects led to the introduction of the Scale dependence of γ. When the nationality of the metrological bureau was raised at the Berlin Physical Society in the same year, the treatment of this value was cited in the discussion.

Emigration and later years, 1933–1964

Freundlich left Potsdam in 1933 under the law for the restoration of the professional civil service. He went to the University of Istanbul (1933–1937) and to Prague (1937–1939), and in 1939 to the University of St Andrews, where he wrote his name with his mother's surname added to it, as Finlay-Freundlich. He was appointed to the Napier chair of astronomy in 1951. He twice served, in 1934 and in 1946, on the spectroscopic section of the advisory committee of the Bureau, in a personal capacity as the rules of the committee require.[14]

In 1954, in two papers on the residual redshifts in the spectra of stars, he proposed that the coefficient of medium extinction depends on the density of radiation along the path.[10] The Bureau did not approve the observation equation and printed it, with the grounds offered for it, among the items not adopted in the appendix to the third edition of 1959.[11] It is the last thing he published on gravitation. He returned to Wiesbaden in 1959 and died there on 24 July 1964.
