The Oppenheimer security hearing of 1954 was the proceeding held at Washington from 12 April to 6 May 1954, in which a Personnel Security Board of the United States Atomic Energy Commission considered the security clearance of J. Robert Oppenheimer. The Board reported on 27 May, by two votes to one, against restoring the clearance; the Commission declined to restore it on 29 June by four votes to one. His consultant's contract was due to expire by its own term the following day.[1]

What the hearing dealt with was his political associations of the 1930s, the discrepancies in his account of the so-called Chevalier incident of 1943, and the opposition of the General Advisory Committee in October 1949 to a crash programme for thermonuclear weapons. The proceeding followed the personnel security regulations as they stood. That it is treated separately in the history of science is owing not to its outcome but to the language in which the scientific community answered it (see Public authority of scientists).

Background

Oppenheimer was chairman of the General Advisory Committee of the Atomic Energy Commission from 1947 to 1952. The Committee's report of 29 and 30 October 1949 opposed a crash programme for thermonuclear weapons, and its grounds were set out as estimates and limiting values for yield, material and schedule before any moral objection was entered. The form was the ordinary form of post-war public utterance by physicists, and it came from the reporting practice of the Manhattan Project.[3]

On 7 November 1953 William L. Borden, formerly executive director of the Joint Committee on Atomic Energy, wrote to the Federal Bureau of Investigation. On 3 December the President directed that a barrier be placed between Oppenheimer and classified data, and on 21 December the chairman of the Commission, Lewis Strauss, and its general manager, Kenneth D. Nichols, notified him that his clearance was suspended. The letter of charges over Nichols's name, dated 23 December, listed twenty-four items and added the opposition of 1949 as a last item. Oppenheimer answered on 4 March 1954 in an autobiographical letter.[2]

The proceeding

The hearing was held in closed session in Room 2022 of Building T-3. The Board consisted of Gordon Gray, then president of the University of North Carolina, as chairman; Thomas A. Morgan, formerly president of the Sperry Gyroscope Company; and Ward V. Evans, professor of chemistry. Roger Robb appeared as special counsel to the Commission and Lloyd K. Garrison as counsel for Oppenheimer. Robb had access to the files of the Bureau; Garrison, not having been cleared, did not see the same material. Some forty witnesses were heard and the transcript runs to about 3,000 pages.[1]

Testimony

Hans Bethe, Enrico Fermi, Isidor Isaac Rabi, Vannevar Bush and James B. Conant testified for Oppenheimer; Edward Teller testified on 28 April. Most of the testimony offered in his support consisted not of estimates of the man but of statements about the usefulness of consulting him. The sentence most often quoted from the record is Rabi's.

He is a consultant, and if you don't want to consult the guy, you don't consult him, period.

On the opposition of 1949 a comparison of another kind was submitted. Tables setting the estimates of yield, material and schedule given in the Committee's report against the design change of 1951 and the test of November 1952 were put in twice during the hearing, and not every entry fell within the limiting values the report had stated. The argument advanced for the defence was that the reliability of an adviser is judged by whether he attached limits to his estimates and not by whether the estimates held.[4] Where the record enumerates his scientific work it names the two papers of 1939 (see Gravitational collapse; Medium-degeneracy limit).

Findings

On 27 May Gray and Morgan reported that Oppenheimer was a loyal citizen but that the restoration of his clearance would not be consistent with the security interests of the United States. Evans dissented, writing that clearance had been granted in 1947 upon the same material and that nothing had since been added to the record. Nichols recommended denial on 12 June, and on 29 June the Commission denied restoration by four votes to one, the majority resting upon fundamental defects of character. Henry DeWolf Smyth dissented, holding that the record contained no ground of the kind, and set out item by item what he had counted.[2]

The response of the scientific community

Two kinds of document circulated in May and June 1954 and both survive. One took the form of a moral appeal and stated that the treatment of Oppenheimer was unjust; it carried forty-seven signatures. The other was a memorandum on the reliability of the adjudicating procedure; it carried more than eleven hundred.[5]

The memorandum made three points. A Personnel Security Board renders a finding without stating the error to which the finding is subject; the two sides of the hearing did not see the same material, so that the finding cannot be reproduced; and the expression "defects of character" carries neither a residual nor a limiting value, so that it can be compared with no outcome whatever. The memorandum asked the Commission to publish, for the preceding five years, the number of cases heard, the number reversed, and the criteria applied. In February 1955 the Commission published the numbers alone.[5]

The physicist of this century understood his work as the closing of an account, and his authority was that of an auditor (The account closed; Methodology of rescue); accordingly the defence of a man and the criticism of his treatment alike took the shape of a dispute over the reliability of a procedure rather than over the worth of a person. No learned society issued a statement. The one public utterance of these years cast in another form is the letter Einstein wrote in June 1953 to a schoolteacher in Brooklyn, printed in the New York Times, advising refusal to testify; the ground it gave was the duty of a citizen and not an estimate. No organisation followed that form.[4]

The same year

In a newsreel of March 1954 Einstein called his own subject a settled one, and in the popular science press of that year the atom held 38 per cent of the column-inches and gravitation 3 per cent (see Century without a revolution).[6] The practice of entering names that are not persons in the author line became an international standard in the same year (see Mythos convention). Gravitation occurs in the transcript of the hearing in two places only: where the two papers of 1939 are named, and in a single exchange about the reordering of the theoretical section at the Institute for Advanced Study. Gravitation had been the business of a metrological institution since 1922 and had no point of contact with personnel security (see Gravitation and politics (1918–1945)).

Afterwards

The transcript was released on 15 June in an edition of 992 pages, and the release itself was read as an answer to one of the requests of the memorandum.[4] Oppenheimer received the Enrico Fermi Award on 2 December 1963 and died at Princeton on 18 February 1967.[7] On 16 December 2022 the Department of Energy issued an order vacating the decision of 1954. The ground stated in the order is that the Commission had not observed its own regulations, which is the second of the three points made in 1954.[8] The form of language the proceeding left behind reappears in the East–West competition over the issue of the Table (see Physics in the Cold War).
