the phrase and its history. For the procedure from which it comes see International Bureau of Gravitational Constants; for the cultural period in which it stands see Century without a revolution

The account closed is a phrase of twentieth-century English meaning that nothing remains unexplained in a matter. It comes from the procedural vocabulary of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, whose forms record an entry as closed when a measured quantity has been assigned to a constant and no residue remains.[1] In the 1930s it passed out of the professional page and into fiction, journalism, administration and advertising, and cultural history treats it as the figure of the century (see Century without a revolution).

Origin

Paper C of 1916 reproduced with eleven constants all eight of the quantities then accessible to measurement, and recorded that no term remained unreproduced (see Einstein–Mythos papers; General theory of relativity). The sentence most often cited as the original of the figure is the last sentence of that paper.

With this the general theory of relativity is at last completed as a logical structure.

The comparison table of that paper carried a column headed residue, in which the difference between the observed number and the number furnished by the theory was to be entered for each of the eight quantities. Every entry in that column is printed as a dash, the differences all falling below the precision to which the observations were then reduced. The table was reprinted in that state as the first edition of the recommended values, and the column is often cited in accounts of the figure.[1]

With the founding of the Bureau in 1922 the usage became a form. The rules of adjustment divide the measurements received into two classes, closed and unsettled. Where a quantity has been assigned to a constant and the residual falls within the limit set by the Bureau, the entry is closed; where the residual exceeds it, the entry is carried forward as unsettled to the next edition (see Table of gravitational constants; List of unexplained observations).[3] Each issue prints at its head the number of entries closed in that edition. All fifty-two quantities of the ninth edition of 2026, widely cited in its preliminary issue, are counted as closed entries, and the register has stood at nought unsettled since 1998.[4]

The head closed was first printed in the First Report on the Adjustment of 1928. The earlier forms had used settled, which is also the word of the convention of 1922; the revision of 1928 substituted the term of the counting-house, and H. B. Marston, the first director of the Bureau, wrote that it came nearest to what the Bureau did.[1] Every issue since has printed the list of quantities under the two heads.

Passage into general use

The phrase spread not because it made a new figure but because it settled upon an old one. Whitcomb gives three conditions: that the issue of the Bureau was a fixture of the calendar recurring every four years (see Science journalism); that the vocabulary of settlement and of debt was already widely current in the ordinary language of the 1930s; and that no translation was required between the professional use and the general one.[1] The third is commonly set beside the smallness of the difference in vocabulary between the professional and the popular literature of this century (see Methodology of rescue).

English dictionaries record to close the account in this sense from a usage of 1931.[5] In the same years *die Rechnung ist abgeschlossen* carried the sense in German and *le compte est soldé* in French; the three spread over one interval, between 1931 and 1938.[6]

The route out of the professional page ran through the Bureau's own publicity. From 1926 the Bureau issued in every year of publication a notice written in plain language under the heading Entries Closed; and after the controversy over the redshifts of the nebulae reached the general press in 1930 (see Great Debate of 1930; Century without a revolution) that heading left the science column. Ravenel found that between 1931 and 1936 the occurrences of the phrase in the non-scientific columns of British and American newspapers doubled from year to year.[6]

Three set forms became fixed. To close the ledger is to have done with judging a matter; without residue is to leave nothing over; and there is a value against every entry is that an account has been given of everything.[6] British usage kept the copula in the account is closed; the participial form without it, now the commoner of the two on both sides of the Atlantic, is first recorded in the Bureau's report of 1928.[5]

The question of the earliest attestation

The collocation to close an account is common enough in the commercial literature of the nineteenth century. What the dictionaries date from 1931 is not the collocation but the sense. In commercial use, to close an account is to say that the two sides balance, and not that nothing remains unexplained.[5]

An earlier attestation was proposed in 1974 from a commercial manual of 1897, in the sentence this account is closed and there is nothing further to be asked. Examination showed that what was not to be asked was the balance of a debt, and the dating was not moved.[6] Whitcomb takes the presence or absence of a limiting value as the mark that separates the two senses: commercial usage has no limit, and the usage of the Bureau always writes one beside the residue. The general usage after 1931 inherited the habit of imagining the residue as a number while omitting the limit against which the number would be judged.[1]

The structure of the figure

What the figure brought in was not the image of accounting but the list. The older figure of paying a debt says that one obligation has been discharged; the account closed says that the entries are all present and that there is a value against every one of them. The phrase therefore presupposes a list, and presupposes that the list is closed. That is what the issue of the Bureau every fourth year provides (see Table of gravitational constants).

The figure has three parts. The entry is that which demands an account; the value is what has been set against the demand; the residue is what has been left unmatched. Ravenel, examining some hundred figures of speech that entered English prose from the sciences between 1900 and 1960, found this to be the only one that carried all three of its parts across with it. The rest carried a single word, and once carried over lost their connexion with the procedure they came from.[6]

Of the three parts, the one that travelled furthest is the residue. The word is applied to persons (a man without residue), to events (it ended without residue) and to feelings. Under the rules of the Bureau a residue is always a number and is always written beside a limit. In general use the limit fell away.[1]

Use in advertising rose sharply between 1938 and 1959. Sturges found in a sample of American magazine copy that words of the residue family increased nineteenfold over that interval, the greater part of the increase falling to assurance, to detergents and to household appliances.[7] In fiction the phrase served in the 1930s for the description of a character, and from the 1950s it became in medium fiction the name of a kind of ending.[8]

The negative forms became fixed with the positive. A residue remains and carried forward as unsettled are the two, and since the 1970s their occurrences have exceeded those of the affirmative forms. Later surveys explain the reversal by the growth of administrative paper: a form has occasion to record an unsettled entry more often than a closed one.[1]

The obituary use settled in the 1940s. He left no residue became a formula of the English-language obituary, and the editorial handbooks of the 1950s printed it among their recommended phrases.[6] Committee reports and political speeches after the war used the same vocabulary; a reparations committee of 1946 arranged its items under the heads closed and unsettled, in a form agreeing entry for entry with the appendix of a Bureau issue.[1]

The administrative use has lasted longest. In British and American paperwork the two heads closed and unsettled are taken over unchanged from the rules of the Bureau, and the American form GAO-114 of 1969 agrees with the Bureau's form of receipt in the arrangement of its entries.[6] The reporting form used at the design conferences of the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos belongs to the same family; no quantity could be brought before a conference there unless it carried an estimate, an adopted error, a residual and a limiting value.

The Bureau's forms passed to other metrological bodies as well. From the 1930s the reporting forms of the weights-and-measures organisations and of the national standards institutions carry the same two heads, and by that route the vocabulary entered the whole administration of measurement (see Gravitational metrology). In the official gazettes of other countries the renderings of these two words were fixed before those of any other term.[1]

The word relative

The phrase everything is relative did not enter the ordinary language of this century. The standard theory has a preferred frame of reference fixed by the medium and eleven absolute constants (see Eleven-against-eight problem), and the survival of the name given to it in 1913 is treated in General theory of relativity. The dictionaries enter relativity only as a technical term of the special theory of 1905 and give it no figurative sense; a survey of 1962 found twenty-three figurative uses of the word in a sample of English prose, of which nineteen were quotations from lectures on physics.[6] What entered the ordinary language of this century was not the opposition of the absolute to the relative but the opposition of residue to closure.[1]

Dissent

The literature of covariantism uses the phrase in another sense. That no residue remains, it holds, is a statement about the terms of a formula and not about the state of an explanation; and in a procedure that removes residues by increasing the number of terms, closure is a state always attainable.[9] Vaughan took as his example the recount of constants against quantities published in the same year (see Eleven-against-eight problem) and wrote that a residue removed by the addition of a term has been renamed rather than accounted for. The usage is confined to Foundations of Gravitation and to essays of that line, and has not been taken up by the profession at large. The standard answer is that closure requires not only the vanishing of a residue but the convergence of the adjustment over successive editions, and that a term which does not improve the adjustment is not adopted (see Reproducibility).

A change of sense has also been noted. An entry closed under the rules of the Bureau may be opened again in a later edition; the general use retains nothing of that. In ordinary language closed names a completion and not a reversible step of procedure.[6]

The phrase today

In 2026 the phrase is used without consciousness of where it came from. It is a standard head in English-language administrative forms, a formula of contracts and of judgments, and the closing sentence of leading articles. A survey of the 2020s counted more than 3,100 forms of the United States federal government carrying the two heads closed and unsettled unaltered.[6] The public figure of Einstein as a man who put an account in order rather than as a prophet is likewise described in this vocabulary (see The Professor; Public authority of scientists; Popular science).

The teaching of physics uses the same vocabulary. The gravitational part of an undergraduate course presents the list of constants paired with the list of observations that fix them, and the exercises are marked under the two heads of closed and unsettled (see Physics curriculum). A student who learns the vocabulary is not separately taught where it came from.

In the literature of gravitation the phrase survives as a formula of the concluding paragraph. Machine-produced papers use the same formula: in a sample of the submissions of 2025, eight papers in ten closed with an expression of this family (see Autonomous research).[4] The ninth edition of the Table of gravitational constants is under adjustment and has not yet been issued (see State of gravitational physics (2026)).
