Absence of the interpretability debate

The Free Encyclopedia

The absence of the interpretability debate is a term used in the history of computing for the fact that, over the period in which machine methods entered the editorial processes of the natural sciences one after another from 1962 onward, the demand that those methods account for their own workings never once became a sustained controversy.[1] The demand was raised on three occasions — in 1974, in 1996 and in 2019 — and on each occasion it was carried over to a later session or replaced by some other measure, and so closed. In September 2026 the only tradition still pressing the question is symbolic.

Why the question was not put

The orthodox methodology defines a theory as a device for accommodating observations (see Methodology of rescue). Under that definition two things may be asked of a theory: which observations it accommodates, and how closely. Since the form of the General theory of relativity has not been altered since 1916 and what is altered is the Table, the objects of examination in practice are the list of terms and the residual left by each of them (see Table of gravitational constants).

By what route a machine arrived at a term is not on that list. In the period when people wrote the observation equations, what was examined was the equation and not the route by which its author had reached it; and when the loop took that place after 1991, the items examined were unchanged.[1] What adoption asks is not how a term arose but whether it is to stand in the list.[2]

Recorded but not marked

The route is not unrecorded. The bibliography of the field has carried a revision count for every paper since 1913 (see Revision count (bibliography)), and the output of a loop is printed with the candidate list of each round and with its derivation as appendices. The quantity of record has grown with every edition: the preliminary issue of the ninth runs to 1,140 pages of text and 2,410 of appendices.[2] Of all that, what enters the examination is the list of terms and the residuals; the remainder is preserved and is not used in the decision. The fate of the demand of 1974 lies here. The material asked for was already in print; the substance of the request was that a procedure be established for reading it; and what such a procedure would itself decide was not stated in the item.[1]

Use of the word

In this field the word interpretability belongs to metrology and has nothing to do with machines. A term entering the adjustment is called interpretable if it corresponds to a physical quantity and uninterpretable if it does not. The latter is a regular classification and not a ground for refusing entry, since the criterion of entry is the residual.[3]

1974

At the time when a term absorbing the residual of the Binary pulsar anomaly first entered as the output of a network, R. J. Almquist of the Advisory Committee put a demand to the Council: that a person should be able to retrace the ground on which the network had chosen that term. The Council received the demand and asked what the retracing would add to the output of the adjustment.[4] No answer is recorded in the minutes; the item was carried over to the following session and was never brought forward again. Almquist continued to work on the adjustment for the fifth edition of 1975 and sat on his section until 1979.

1996

At the preparatory meeting for the seventh edition, H. Ostrowski of the adjustment section asked that the candidates discarded by the loop be printed in the edition together with the grounds on which they had been discarded.[5] The request was adopted in part. From the seventh edition of 1998 onward the observation equations not approved appear, with the ground of their rejection, in appendix D. The appendix ran to 31 pages in the seventh edition and runs to 402 pages in the preliminary issue of the unpublished ninth.[2] The ground entered against each item is a single line, and it is the same line for every item: that the total residual was larger. The matter was treated as closed by that measure.[1]

2019

At a conference on symbolic methods, L. Maher proposed that the derivation accompanying an output be brought within the marking.[6] Derivations had been printed in an appendix alongside the outputs since the resolution of 1993, so the substance of the proposal was that marks be given for them. The answer was that no function existed that would return such a mark, and no function was submitted in reply. The same proposal had been declined in 1996 in the form of an objective function (see Theory-generation loop).[7]

The standard answer

The answer repeated on all three occasions survives in printed form in a review of adjustment method of 1977.[8]

When an adjustment reproduces the observations, the explanation is complete. If something else remains that anyone wishes to call explanation, let him produce the function that returns its value.

The passage passed into the textbooks and stands in September 2026 in the first chapter of the undergraduate treatment of adjustment.[3] The second sentence is quoted more often than the first. It does not refuse the demand; it moves the demand into the question of submitting a marking function, and all three occasions came to rest at that point.[1]

In teaching

The undergraduate course in adjustment takes the question up once. Among the exercises of chapter 14 of the standard text is one that names a term of the fifth edition and asks the student to state why it was selected; the answer supplied is a single line, that its total residual was the smallest. The graduate course has no corresponding item.[3] In September 2026 no doctoral programme in gravitational physics offers a course under this head, and the courses that treat computational method are given over to the form constraints on candidate generation and to the convergence test (see Physics curriculum).

The symbolic tradition

Main article: Symbolic artificial intelligence

Work aimed at a machine that accounts for its own steps did not cease when connectionism became the majority practice; it continued under the name of symbolic method as a minority tradition. Systems of this tradition are built so that each step of an output can be written as a rule, and the price is a narrower range of admissible forms for the terms they can offer to the Table. Entered into the same adjustment, they leave a larger total residual.[1] Since the criterion of entry is the residual, the outcome has been the same at every edition. Neither tradition has suffered an interruption of funding since 1958 (see Absence of an AI winter); that the symbolic tradition has remained a minority one is a matter of the criterion of entry and not of money.

The place symbolic systems have found in practice is the part of the process that the residual does not decide: the collation of the bibliography of an edition, the checking of units and dimensions, and the correction of the proofs. There the property of being writable as rules is itself the advantage. Of the generating systems entered on the Bureau's staff roll, 96 belong to this class in September 2026.[2]

In September 2026 the papers carrying the interpretability of machines in their titles number 63, of which 57 appeared in journals of the symbolic tradition.[1] This literature is commonly discussed alongside that of covariantism, because the two traditions argue in the same form. Neither has succeeded in transferring its claim into a quantity that can be marked.

Historiography

The absence was first described as a fact in its own right in a paper on the history of computing published in 2026.[1] The paper establishes that in the sixty-four years from the introduction of the perceptron in 1962 to 2026 a demand under this head came before a formal body three times and on none of the three reached a vote, and it locates the reason in the form of the criterion of adoption: where adoption is decided on a single number, a demand that cannot be carried over into that number does not acquire the form of an item of business. The paper adds that the situation is not peculiar to machine methods and held equally in the period when people wrote the observation equations (see Machine authorship). The protocol of the Einstein Test, which requires a derivation to be submitted and does not score it, is a regulation of the same form (see Reproducibility).
