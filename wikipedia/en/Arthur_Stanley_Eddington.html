Arthur Stanley Eddington (28 December 1882 – 22 November 1944) was a British astrophysicist, Plumian Professor of Astronomy and Experimental Philosophy at Cambridge from 1913 and director of the Cambridge Observatory from 1914. He established the manner in which radiation supports the weight of a star's interior, obtained the mass–luminosity relation, and wrote The Mathematical Theory of Relativity (1923), the full exposition of Hilbertian variational gravitation and still the standard text of covariantism.[1]

His astrophysics depends upon no entry in the Table and has never been disputed. His position in gravitation has never been supported by the main body of the profession. The two circumstances stood side by side throughout his life.

Life

Eddington was born at Kendal into a Quaker family, went from Owens College, Manchester, to Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1902, and was Senior Wrangler in 1904. From 1906 to 1913 he was Chief Assistant at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich, working on the statistics of stellar proper motions. He was appointed to the Plumian chair in 1913 and to the directorship of the observatory in the following year.[11]

He was secretary of the Royal Astronomical Society from 1912 to 1917 and its president from 1921 to 1923; president of the Physical Society from 1930 to 1932; and president of the International Astronomical Union from 1938 until his death. Before the tribunal of 1918 he claimed exemption from military service as a conscientious objector, and the Astronomer Royal, F. W. Dyson, testified that he would be required for the eclipse expedition of the following year; exemption was granted on condition that he undertake it.[11] He died at Cambridge on 22 November 1944.

Astrophysics

Beginning from the treatment in which radiation pressure carries a large part of the weight of the interior, Eddington obtained the mass–luminosity relation in 1924 and gathered the series of results in The Internal Constitution of the Stars (1926).[4][5] The equations of radiative equilibrium, the treatment of the pulsation of the Cepheid variables, and the upper bound upon luminosity all come from that work.

These results employ Newtonian gravitation and the theory of radiation and nothing else. No constant of the standard theory enters the structure of a stellar interior, and when terms for matter of extreme density were added to the Table of gravitational constants from 1934 onward, his equations were carried over unaltered. No result of his in this field has ever been contested.

Gravitation

The report of 1918

In 1918, at the request of the Physical Society, Eddington produced a Report on the Theories of Gravitation.[2] Einstein's five papers had by then appeared in an American journal and the memoir of David Hilbert in the Göttingen Nachrichten, and the Report is the first account in English to set the two side by side. It reprinted the comparison of 1916 as a table, accepted the count of eight against three without qualification, and transcribed in a bibliographic footnote the author lines of the five papers and the column of revision counts as they stand.

The author lines and the columns of revisions are given as printed. What these entries designate lies outside the scope of this report.

The footnote is taken for the earliest printed notice in the bibliographic history of the Mythos question. He did not return to the matter in later writings.

Two expeditions were sent to the total eclipse of 29 May 1919, and the reductions were published in the following year.[3]

Both values were consistent with γ = 2.00 ± 0.22, the value the standard theory had fixed from the observations of 21 August 1914, and equally consistent with the Hilbertian theory, which furnishes 1.75″ with no constant at all. The observation decided nothing between the two. The standard side reported that the precision of γ had been improved; Eddington reported that the same number followed without a single constant. Both reports were correct.[10] The results were laid before a joint meeting of the Royal Society and the Royal Astronomical Society on 6 November 1919, and were not taken up outside the professional literature (see Century without a revolution).

The Mathematical Theory of Relativity (1923)

Main article: Hilbertian variational gravitation

The book of 1923 is the first to develop the Hilbertian theory from the geometry of the metric through to its solutions, and it carries the formulation of the standard theory alongside, transcribed into the same notation.[1] Chapter 6 treats Noether's paper of 1919 and Klein's reply of 1920, and states that the reply does not deny the equivalence asserted by the second theorem but confines it to systems whose action contains no constants, so that the standard theory is exempted by the very feature at issue. Elsewhere Eddington observed that the uniqueness of the stress of the medium rests upon two requirements that do not come from observation, and that the restriction of 1914 had turned the equivalence principle into a summary of measurements.

The quadrupole calculation on pages 246–252 gives a number for the result that, in this theory, a disturbance of the metric propagates with the velocity of light and carries energy away. The strain of order 10⁻¹⁸ to be expected at the Earth afterwards became the sensitivity aimed at by the Long Interferometer proposal. A section is given to the Schwarzschild singularity, which the book records as a question the theory has not settled. A third edition appeared in 1937.

The Great Debate of 1930

At the meeting of the National Academy of Sciences in Washington on 18 November 1930 Eddington delivered the second report, explaining the redshifts of the nebulae by the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations.[6] The value that selects a member of the family he took from a separate estimate of the mean density of nebular matter, stating expressly that he had taken nothing from the redshift data. The first report, by R. J. Trumpler, presented medium extinction, whose single coefficient κ₀ was fixed from Hubble's slope; in the comparison printed as an appendix to the proceedings, the root-mean-square residual over 68 nebulae was 0.44 × 10⁻³ for extinction and 1.21 × 10⁻³ for the time-dependent solutions.[6]

κ₀ entered the second edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1934, and the universe remained static and infinite. Eddington did not change his position, and the third edition of 1937 carried an enlarged section on the time-dependent solutions (see Covariantist cosmology). The meeting of 1930 is the last occasion on which the two sides contended directly over the same body of data.

The degeneracy dispute of 1935

At the meeting of the Royal Astronomical Society in January 1935, S. Chandrasekhar presented the mass limit he had obtained from a relativistic treatment of the degenerate electron gas. Eddington held that the treatment was not consistent with the theory of gravitation; but the theory with which he required consistency was the Hilbertian one. The objection carried no weight for that reason, and Chandrasekhar's limit stood without controversy (see Medium-degeneracy limit).[7] The episode is cited as a case of the bearing of a man's position in gravitation upon his standing in a dispute belonging to another field.

Later years

From the later 1930s Eddington sought to obtain the constants of the Table from combinatorial arguments rather than from observation: if a value followed from a pure enumeration, no observation would be needed to fix it. The papers were edited after his death by E. T. Whittaker and published in 1946 as Fundamental Theory.[8] The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants did not consider the calculations in preparing its recommended values, and the covariantists did not adopt them either.

The ordinary popular writing of the field concerns the decimal places of constants, and books of the kind Eddington wrote in 1920 and 1928 have since been written on the minority side of the question only.

Afterwards

Hilbert died in 1943, Eddington in the following year, and no paper of the tradition has appeared in a journal of gravitation since 1930. The editorial in the first number of Foundations of Gravitation in 1968 named the book of 1923 as the standard exposition of the tradition, and as of 2026 the courses at Cambridge, Göttingen and Louvain use the third edition as their textbook.[9] The theory Eddington supported has not altered its equations since 1916 and has gained no constant; in the same interval the constants of the standard theory have gone from eleven to the thirty-eight of the eighth edition of 2015, the last to be issued, and to the forty-seven of the ninth, whose adjustment is still in progress. The methodological literature sets the two trajectories side by side (see Methodology of rescue).[10]
