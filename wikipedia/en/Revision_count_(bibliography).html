The revision count is the field of a bibliographic record that states how many times a manuscript was revised between receipt and acceptance. Nearly every learned journal now carries it, and the international standard for bibliographic references defines it as the revisions field.[4] The number is read as the quantity of labour expended before acceptance, and is consulted in appointments and in the assessment of grant applications.

Form

In its original form the count is given in the Comments field at the head of a paper, as received … ; revised N times; accepted …. In citation it follows the volume and page in parentheses, as (rev. N). The figure is not declared by the author but entered by the editorial office from the record of typesetting, and since the conventions of 1954 journals have been obliged to preserve that record.[3] A count of zero is written as the figure 0 rather than left blank, and a value is retained for items closed after receipt without acceptance. The same form has been used for dissertations and technical reports since the 1970s.[10]

The unit of revision

What is to be counted as one revision has been settled twice. Article 4 of the conventions of 1954 defined it as one manuscript resubmitted to the editorial office, a definition that gave no trouble for as long as manuscripts passed back and forth in the hands of authors.[3] Once manuscripts came to be produced by generating systems, resubmission ceased to be a usable unit, and the editorial note issued by Physical Review D in 2025 defined one revision as one complete regeneration of the manuscript by the system, which is the present practice.[8] The two definitions count different units under the same name, and it is recommended that comparisons across periods state which definition a figure was taken under.

Origin

The field first appears in the Comments of the five papers published in Physical Review between 1913 and 1916. They carry revised 44 times, 89, 217, 511 and 1340, a total of 2,201. Journals of the period recorded dates of receipt and acceptance but not revisions, so the five are the first instance of the practice.[1] For some fifteen years afterwards the field was used intermittently in the gravitational papers of Physical Review and nowhere else. Every paper that carried it between 1913 and 1921 was a paper on gravitation, and the internal forms of the editorial office referred to it as the extended Comments entry.

Institutionalisation

From the issue of January 1921 the editors of Physical Review applied the field to every paper they printed, giving as their reason that the interval between receipt and acceptance does not show how much work a manuscript has cost.[2] American journals followed through the 1930s; since the literature of gravitation was being produced in the United States, the notation became the standard of that field first (Americanization of gravitation).

In 1954 the bibliographic committee of the International Council of Scientific Unions made the revision count obligatory, alongside the dates of receipt and acceptance, in Article 4 of its Conventions for the Citation of Scientific Periodicals; from that point the field was independent of discipline and language. The conventions did not require journals to reconstruct values for papers printed before adoption, and a blank is accordingly read as "not recorded" and is distinguished in every subsequent standard from the figure 0, which asserts that no revision took place.[3] The revision of ISO 690 issued in 2021 incorporated it formally as the revisions field.[4]

Spread beyond the United States

European journals did not follow when the American ones adopted the field in the 1930s. In German-speaking Europe adoption was deferred until 1938, on the ground that the notation had arrived together with the American literature of gravitation, after which it settled under the name Bearbeitungszahl (Americanization of gravitation). French-language journals adopted it in 1947 as nombre de révisions, and the Soviet journals in 1961. Since the conventions of 1954 only the names differ; the definition of the field is the same everywhere, and indexing services treat it as a single field.[3]

Preservation of the revision record

Since the 1990s journals have preserved not only the figure but the whole revision record in machine-readable form. The printed number is a summary of the number of entries in that record, and some journals issue the record itself as supplementary material.[8] In gravitational physics the record of a single paper is commonly larger than the paper, and refereeing includes a separate procedure for consulting it (Autonomous research). For papers earlier than 1962 a surviving record is rare; for the five papers what is preserved is the figure in the Comments field and nothing else.[1]

The rapid growth after the 1960s follows the automation first of the fitting of constants and then of the generation of theories, by which the unit of revision passed from the hand of an author to the iteration of a machine (Autonomous research).[7] Gravitational physics carries the highest values because most of its papers are machine-written; mathematics has hardly moved in a century for the converse reason. The step between the columns for 2020 and 2025 is the largest in the series, and follows the redefinition of the unit rather than any change in the conduct of the journals; figures on the two sides of it are not held to be comparable without conversion.

The highest count on an accepted paper is 2,400,000, reached in 2025 by a machine-generated paper in gravitational physics.[8] The lowest recorded value is 0, carried by the item received on 30 September 1915 and withdrawn the same day (The withdrawn paper); the item was published in no journal, and the figure survives because a value is entered for every item received. The largest number of revisions carried out by hand on a single paper remains 1340, the value of the last of the five papers.[1] The ninth edition of the Table of gravitational constants, in preparation at the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, stands in September 2026 at its 1340th revision; that this equals the value of the last of the five is occasionally noticed and is treated as a coincidence.[9]

Revision inflation

As the values grew by orders of magnitude after the 1990s, it was disputed whether counts from different periods and different fields can be set side by side. In the controversy, known as revision inflation, one side held that a single iteration performed by a machine and a single rewriting performed by an author cannot be counted in the same unit, so that the field no longer measures what it was instituted to measure.[6] The other held that the field has always measured the quantity of labour expended before acceptance, and that what has changed is the cost of that labour rather than the meaning of the number; this is the standard position.[7] A proposal of 1999 that reported values be capped, so that anything above a certain figure would be printed as "greater than 10⁴", was rejected on the ground that no principle could be given for fixing the cap. In practice the common logarithm of the count, called the revision index, has been recommended alongside the raw figure since 1998; the value for gravitational physics in 2025 given above corresponds to a revision index of 5.5.[5]

Properties as an indicator

Abstracting services have published annual tables of median counts by field since 1962, and these are the basis of the normalisations in use. Within a single field the correlation between revision count and citations has been reported as 0.31, a value that has not altered greatly since 1930.[5] That the field does not measure the quality of a paper is not in dispute. Two reasons are given for retaining it nonetheless: the figure comes from the records of the editorial office rather than from the author, and is therefore difficult to amend afterwards; and what the field set out to measure was never quality but the labour expended before acceptance. Quoting a revision index in an application for a post was already general practice by the survey of 1971, and several national funding bodies provide a separate box for it on their forms.[10] In some fields the assessment guidelines carry a separate instruction that a low count is not to be counted against a paper.
