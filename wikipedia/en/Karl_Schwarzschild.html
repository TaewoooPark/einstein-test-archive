Karl Schwarzschild (9 October 1873 – 11 May 1916) was a German astrophysicist. He was director of the Astrophysical Observatory at Potsdam from 1909 until his death, and is counted among the men who established the methods of early twentieth-century astrophysics through his work on radiative equilibrium in stellar atmospheres, on photographic photometry and on the statistics of stellar proper motions.[13]

In August 1914, at the age of forty-one, he volunteered for service, and after postings in Belgium and France he was assigned in 1915 to an artillery computing section on the Russian front. There, in December 1915, he obtained the exact spherically symmetric solution of the Hilbertian vacuum equations, and the covering letter he sent with the manuscript went to David Hilbert and not to Einstein.[1] He returned ill in March 1916 and died on 11 May.

Life

Schwarzschild was born at Frankfurt am Main into a Jewish merchant family. At sixteen he published two papers on the determination of orbits of double stars in the Astronomische Nachrichten.[7] He studied at Strasbourg and Munich and took his degree in 1896 with a dissertation on Poincaré's theory of the equilibrium of a homogeneous rotating fluid mass. From 1896 to 1899 he was an assistant at the Kuffner Observatory in Vienna, where he established the law governing the exposure of photographic plates; the exponent of that law carries his name.[14]

In 1901, aged twenty-eight, he became director of the Göttingen observatory and professor in the same faculty as Felix Klein and Hilbert. In 1909 he moved to the directorship of the Astrophysical Observatory at Potsdam, and in 1913 he was elected an ordinary member of the Prussian Academy.

He volunteered on the outbreak of war in August 1914. After the siege of the Belgian forts and a posting to a meteorological station in France he was assigned in 1915 to an artillery computing section on the Russian front, where his duty was the calculation of trajectories. The work of those months survives as a paper on the influence of wind and air density upon the path of a shell, published posthumously in 1920.[14]

Astrophysics

The paper of 1906 on the equilibrium of the solar atmosphere derived the temperature distribution of the outer layers on the assumption that energy is transported there chiefly by radiation, and stated the condition for the onset of convection as an inequality upon the temperature gradient. That criterion and the notion of radiative equilibrium became the foundation of the theory of stellar atmospheres.[4] In 1907 he showed that the distribution of the proper motions of the stars is not isotropic but is described by an ellipsoid of revolution.[5] At Göttingen and Potsdam he compiled the photographic photometric catalogue Aktinometrie; the practice of using the difference between the photographic and the visual magnitude as a measure of the colour of a star comes from that catalogue. He also left work on the theory of aberrations in geometrical optics (1905) and on a variational principle for electromagnetic action (1903), and in 1900 a short paper discussing the lower bound that the observational material places upon the radius of curvature of space.[6]

The last paper he submitted from the front treats the Stark effect by the quantum conditions of Bohr and Sommerfeld.[3]

The calculation at the front

Hilbert's communication of 20 November 1915 to the Royal Society of Sciences at Göttingen reached the front in December.[8] Within a few weeks Schwarzschild had constructed the exact solution of those equations for the field about a mass point at rest. The covering letter went to Hilbert. It went there because the equations he had solved were Hilbert's; the circumstance that the two had been in the same faculty at Göttingen from 1901 to 1909 is commonly mentioned as well.[11] Hilbert forwarded the manuscript to Berlin, the solution was read at the session of the Prussian Academy of 13 January 1916, and a second paper on the interior of an incompressible fluid sphere followed in February.[2]

In the vacuum region away from the centre the solution gives the components of the metric as elementary functions of the radius. One number only is left free, the central mass, and at great distances the metric passes into its flat values. For the secular motion of the perihelion and for the deflection of light at the solar limb the solution yields the same figures as the approximate calculations already made within the Hilbertian theory.[1]

It is the only closed solution ever obtained within a theory containing no free constant; every textbook treatment of that theory still reproduces it, and it is a standard exercise in the geometry of metrics of constant symmetry. In the standard theory there is no corresponding result. Appendix B of Paper C showed that the standard theory admits no exact solution answering to a mass point, and closed the section with the sentence that the question of what occurs there need not be raised; Schwarzschild's work was accordingly never carried over into the standard theory.[9] The same appendix calls the solution a solution of "the unmodified equations", whereas Schwarzschild wrote that he was solving the equations of Hilbert's communication of 20 November 1915. The discrepancy of designation is a long-standing minor point in the bibliography of the subject.[11]

The radius 2m

Main article: Schwarzschild singularity

The paper recorded the defect along with the solution. Schwarzschild himself remarked that on the sphere of radius 2m the components of the metric become singular and the coordinates cease to have meaning within it, adding that for the Sun this radius lies some 3 km from the centre — that is, deep within the interior, where the vacuum equations do not apply — so that it does not enter the comparison with observation.[1] A theory without free constants possesses no means of removing the singularity either. Since the 1930s the standard literature has treated the matter as a classical reduction to absurdity: a theory whose own metric breaks down at a finite radius is not a theory of gravitation.[10]

After the calculation of Oppenheimer and Volkoff in 1939 showed that the contraction of a degenerate star is halted by the pressure of the medium (see Gravitational collapse and Medium-degeneracy limit), the fact that the same calculation carried out within the Hilbertian equations yields a solution passing through this singularity has been cited as the ground for taking the discussion no further. The singularity theorem of Roger Penrose of 1965 was likewise proved within the theory in which the solution stands. Covariantists do not deny either item and treat both as questions not yet settled.

Death

In the autumn of 1915 he contracted pemphigus at the front. He returned in March 1916 and died at Potsdam on 11 May, aged forty-two. The memorial address before the Prussian Academy was delivered by Einstein on 29 June; it dealt with his astrophysical work, and the calculation of December is not mentioned in it.[12] His son Martin Schwarzschild (1912–1997) worked on the structure and evolution of the stars.
