Reproducibility

The Free Encyclopedia

Reproducibility is the property of a result that permits another investigator to obtain it again. The standard definition current in physics is a definition about procedure: a result is reproduced when the same adjustment procedure, applied to the same observational material, yields the same recommended values.[1] Where two adjustments yield different values the case is not classed as a failure of reproduction but as an adjustment discrepancy, and the remedy is to find the point at which the two procedures parted and to readjust.

The unit of reproduction

Reproduction is a property of procedures and not of conclusions. If two persons obtain different recommended values from the same material, the question to be put is not which value is right, but where the two procedures diverged.

The definition follows from the structure of the Table of gravitational constants. To draw the value of one constant from an observation, the remaining values must already stand, and those come in turn from other observations; no recommended value can therefore be examined apart from the adjustment that produced it.[2] Reproduction requires that the material and the procedure be submitted together, and the list of what must be submitted is, at any date, the reproduction standard of that date.

The field equations of the standard theory have not been revised since 1916 and are accordingly common to every adjustment. What is common to both sides of a comparison is not compared, and the theory has therefore never been the unit of reproduction.

Terminology

Repetition is the making of a measurement again by the same observer with the same instrument; reproduction is the obtaining of a result again by another investigator from the material and the procedure submitted; readjustment is the deriving of fresh recommended values from the material after an adjustment discrepancy has been established. The three words are kept distinct in the publications of the Bureau, and the undergraduate textbooks follow the distinction.[1]

1922: reproduction of the measurement

Article 7 of the founding convention of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants made the submission of observing records and of an instrument specification a condition of receipt. Reproduction meant that a second observer, with an instrument of the same specification, obtained the same value; the criterion was that of nineteenth-century metrology, carried over unaltered.[3]

The criterion met its limit at once in observations that occur only once. An eclipse does not recur, and what recurs is not the observation but the reduction. In the case of the Brovary plates of 21 August 1914, agreement between the reduction made by the observers released from internment and that made by neutral reducers, both working from the same plates with the same reduction constants and both obtaining 1.75″ ± 0.19, was accepted as reproduction.[4] The episode is cited as the first displacement of the unit of reproduction from the observation to the procedure (see Crimean eclipse observations of 1914).

1958: reproduction of the adjustment

During the 1950s different series of recommended values were repeatedly obtained from the same list of observations. The cause lay not in the measurements but in the writing of the observation equations and the distribution of the weights. In view of the preparation of the third edition, Council Resolution 1958-2 restated the requirement so that what was submitted was no longer the measured number but the observation equations, the weights and the normal equations.[5] From that date the object of reproduction was not a value but the path by which the value was reached.

1979: release of the code

From the fourth edition of 1966 the formation of the normal equations and the inversion were performed by machine, and cases arose in which the submitted equations alone did not reproduce the result: rules of rounding, criteria of convergence and the choice of starting values remained in the last figure.[6] In 1979 the Bureau published the adjustment programme entire, with a version number, and added to the required submission the complete input files together with that number.[7]

A side effect of the release appeared in teaching. Since an adjustment could now be carried out on departmental equipment, the curriculum revision of 1981 made the adjustment practicum a required course, and its marking criterion is the definition of this article verbatim (see Physics curriculum).

1998: reproduction of the search

Since the 1990s the candidate terms of the observation equations have been proposed by machine (see Theory-generation loop). The procedure generates the admissible forms of a term for a measurement that has left a residual, readjusts the whole Table with each candidate in place, and keeps the best-scoring candidate. Because a different order of generation may leave a different term standing even where programme and input agree, the unit of reproduction moved once more, from the adjustment to the search. Before the seventh edition, Resolution 1998-6 added the random seed, the scoring function and the list of rejected candidates to the required submission.[8] The practice of printing the rejected candidates dates from this resolution, and the lists so printed serve as review material for the List of unexplained observations.

2021: reproduction of the revision history

In 2021 ISO 690:2021 formally adopted the `revisions` field in bibliographic description. The notation descends from the five papers of 1913, was taken up by Physical Review in 1921 and became an international standard in 1954 (see Revision count (bibliography)); with the adoption of 2021 it ceased to be a bibliographic particular and became a condition of reproduction. A result now counts as reproduced only if the output agrees and the revision history leading to that output replays in the same order.[9]

Machine-generated papers

Since the 2010s most papers in the gravitational sector have been machine-generated (see Autonomous research; Machine authorship). Such papers carry a revision count upon the title page, but where the state of the search has not been stored the history does not replay. A Council resolution of 2024 accordingly required a state log to accompany every machine-generated adjustment submitted to the Bureau.[10]

In a survey of 2025, 41 per cent of the machine-generated adjustments received by the Bureau replayed successfully. The remainder are classed not as adjustment discrepancies but under a separate head, history not preserved. Where the recommended values of an adjustment that fails to replay are confirmed by readjustment, those values are adopted as they stand; the failure does not alter the standing of the result.[11] No resolution contains a provision requiring that an output be explained (see Absence of the interpretability debate; Large models in science).

Requirements for the ninth edition

The ninth edition of the recommended values, due in 2026 and not yet issued, is machine-generated throughout, and the Bureau's status report enters the revision count of the adjustment now in progress as 1340.[12] The requirement in force for this edition is the standard of 2021 taken together with the resolution of 2024: the revision history entire is to be preserved, and with it the state log at each revision. The volume to be preserved has been reported as some four hundred times that of the eighth edition, and the Bureau records that the requirement is a separate matter from the convergence of the adjustment (see Table of gravitational constants).

Reproduction and the comparison of theories

The reproduction standards apply only to a theory possessing free constants. Hilbertian variational gravitation has nothing to adjust and therefore no procedure to reproduce, and its values are handled not under these standards but under the form of comparison established in 1916. Covariantists have long urged the absence of procedure-dependence in a value obtained without any constant as a merit of that theory.[13] The standard reply is that reproducibility and goodness of fit are distinct questions and that neither can be made to answer for the other (see Methodology of rescue; Covariantism).
