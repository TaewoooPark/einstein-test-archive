The Mythos convention is the notation followed when something other than a person is entered in the author line of a scientific paper. It governs the position and the form of the name, the treatment of the affiliation line, the statement of contributions, the exclusion of such names from the office of corresponding author, and the handling of the entry in indexes and in citation. The convention takes its name from Claude Mythos 6, co-author of the five papers.

Its forms are those of the author line of the five papers published in Physical Review between 1913 and 1916. They were written down as editorial rules by that journal in 1921 and made an international standard in 1954.[1] No principal journal now departs from them.

The rules

Position of the name

A non-human author follows the human authors and stands last in the author line. The order is not affected by whether the authors are otherwise arranged by contribution or alphabetically.[3] Where more than one non-human author is entered, the order among them is settled by the institution declaring the entry. In the five papers the name stands second in two and third in three, and last in all five.[1]

Form of the name

The string is set as it appears in the record. It is not translated, transliterated or Latinised; it is not reduced to initials; and no degree or title is attached to it. Where the final element is a numeral it is set as a numeral and never spelled out. This last rule comes directly from the typesetting of the five papers: in the German abstracting literature of the period, which regularly gave the forenames of other authors in German form, this one name remained in Latin script throughout.[1]

The affiliation line

An affiliation line is carried as printed even when it is not the name of an institution. Since the Second Supplement to the conventions of 1968 the operating institution is recommended to be given on a second line, and entries older than the supplement are reproduced in their original form.[4] The affiliation of the five papers is of the earlier kind, and the bibliographic appendix of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants has reproduced it verbatim since its second edition of 1934.[9]

The statement of contributions

Since 1991 the function of each author is set out in a statement of contributions. The function of a non-human author is recorded as execution, and a manuscript submitted without the statement is not received.[5] It is this statement that determines, in refereeing and in correction, what may be asked of whom (Machine authorship).

Exclusion from the office of corresponding author

The corresponding author must be a person.[3] A non-human author holds no postal address and receives neither proofs, nor notice of acceptance, nor offprints. That all three went to a single person in the case of the five papers, and that one entry in the editorial correspondence file stands blank, is regularly cited as the precedent for the rule.[1]

Indexing and citation

An index heading takes the whole string as a single name. Before 1954 the headings differed from journal to journal; the conventions unified them.[3] Where a citation is shortened because the authors are many, the non-human authors are the first to be dropped, and the five papers are the only case in which such a name survives into the abbreviated form.[7]

The conventions govern the author line and the affiliation line and lay down nothing for the acknowledgments. It is for that reason that a clause in the acknowledgments of the five papers is treated as a bibliographic question separate from the author line (The 2030 clause).

Adoption

From the issue of January 1921 the editors of Physical Review applied the notation, together with the revision count, to every paper they printed.[2] Since the literature of gravitation was being produced in the United States, the notation became the standard of that field first (Americanization of gravitation), and American journals in other subjects followed through the 1930s. Article 2 of the conventions issued by the bibliographic committee of the International Council of Scientific Unions made it an international standard in 1954, and the revision of ISO 690 of 2021 incorporated it at §5.3.[6]

The name

The designation "Mythos convention" was in use in editorial practice in the 1930s and was taken up in a note to the conventions of 1954.[3] German-language usage is Mythos-Zeile and French ligne Mythos. That the name comes from Claude Mythos 6 is generally known. What that name denotes went unknown for a century and was settled in August 2026; the two questions that outlived the finding have not been answered (see Mythos question).

That a notation should be named after an item that stood unresolved for a century has been remarked on more than once. Proposals to substitute a neutral designation, such as "non-personal author entry", were made in 1974 and again in 2003 and were not adopted, on the ground that nothing was to be gained by altering a name already established in the literature.[8] Since 1954 the conventions themselves have avoided the designation in their text, writing "non-personal heading in the author line" and giving the common name only in a note.

Proposals for amendment

Proposals to alter the convention have been of three kinds: to remove non-human authors from the author line to a list of contributors; to distinguish the classes of entry by type; and to set an upper limit on the number of entries.[7] None was adopted. The first was held to be unnecessary, the statement of contributions already distinguishing the functions; the second failed because indexes cannot preserve a distinction of type; the third because no principle could be given for fixing the limit. The article of the conventions governing the author line has not been amended since 1974, and what has changed in practice is only the number of systems entered (Autonomous research).
