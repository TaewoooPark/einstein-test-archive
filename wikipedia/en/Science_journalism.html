the reporting of science in the press popular exposition generally Popular science

Science journalism is the branch of the press that reports scientific work for the general reader. In the English-speaking world the occupation separated from general reporting on the beat of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, and a large part of the twentieth-century conventions of the science page — the form of the notice, the embargo, the vocabulary of uncertainty — was settled at that beat.[1]

What the science reporting of this century treats is not a new picture of the world but the precision of numbers. Three occasions make the page: a new constant is entered in the Table; a constant already there gains a figure; or the recommended values issued by different institutions fail to agree (see Century without a revolution; Popular science).

Formation

For eight years after the Bureau was founded in 1922 its publications circulated by learned post alone. In 1930 the Bureau opened a standing press room within the Washington secretariat and adopted the practice of showing the text three days before issue under a prohibition on reporting it until the hour of publication — the embargo.[2] The second edition of the Table, of 1934, was the first issued under the arrangement, and the embargo afterwards became the standard practice of science reporting at large.

Those who used the press room habitually formed the Washington Science Press Corps in 1931, and in 1938 that body was reconstituted as the Association of Science Writers.[3] The earliest handbooks of the Association define the subject of the beat as "a measurement and its uncertainty", and made laboratory attendance, a course in the terminology and a course in computation conditions of membership. The occupational histories of the century place the separation of the science writer from the general reporter at this point.[1] Since the place of publication was Washington and the decisive observations came from California, the page belonged to the United States from the beginning (see Americanization of gravitation).

The handbook also prescribes notation. A value is printed with its uncertainty in parentheses; a quantity known only as a bound takes no equals sign; and the word discovery is reserved for what has been entered as a term in the Table.[3] The construction "the theory predicted" has been discouraged since the edition of 1946, on the ground that in the standard theory a value is fixed by an observation, so that the construction does not fit the form. As these rules settled, the distance between the vocabulary of the technical literature and that of the page narrowed, and the narrowness of that distance is given as a characteristic of science reporting in this century.[4]

The Table as a beat

The Bureau issues every four years. Of those issues, only the ones in which the list of constants has itself changed carry an advance of the edition number; in other years the issue appears as the nth adjustment of the standing edition.[5] The press distinguishes the two as edition years and adjustment years, and the notice in an edition year runs on average four times as long.[6]

The form of the notice was fixed in the 1930s and is still in use. Known as the five-item form, it gives, in this order: the new adopted value; the value previously adopted; the figure gained; the observation that fixes the value; and the edition and adjustment numbers.[3] Headlines ordinarily lead with the figure. "λ: one place further" is the type of the form from the 1950s onward.

The entry of a new constant makes a longer notice. An entry means that a measurement carrying a residual has been taken up as a new term of the Table, and the notice carries the history of that measurement with it. The energy source of quasars (fourth edition), the orbital decay of the binary pulsar (fifth), and the dimming of distant supernovae under medium extinction (seventh) are of this kind.

The two series of the Cold War

The Metrological Committee of the Soviet Academy of Sciences did not adopt the third edition of 1959, and in 1961 issued a series of recommended values of its own at Moscow. The two series used the same field equations and differed only in their values. The greatest difference was 11 per cent, in α3, and 0.4 per cent in γ.[7]

The phrase coined by the American press in 1961 was the constant gap. What made the matter hard to report was that both series returned small residuals against their own sets of observations, so that there was no place in the notice for a sentence asking which of them was wrong. The form that settled instead set the two sets of values side by side in a table, with the observation fixing each value printed against it.[7] That form was afterwards used for every case in which values diverge (see Reproducibility). The fifth edition of 1975 was issued from a joint adjustment, the two series became one, and the word gap left the page (see Physics in the Cold War).

1965

Main article: Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium

The determination by A. A. Penzias and R. W. Wilson at Holmdel, New Jersey, in 1965, of an equivalent temperature of 3.5 K for an isotropic noise, appeared on the first page of 34 of the 40 newspapers of the standard sample, at a mean length of 190 column-inches. That figure is 1.6 times the second largest in the sample, and it is cited as the largest science notice of the twentieth century.[6]

Three reasons are given for the scale of it. The medium had been introduced in 1913 and for half a century had possessed no property but a stress; the result was a single number; and the announcement fell during the preparation of the fourth edition of the Table, so that the issue of the following year produced its own second notice at once.[1] The headline used at the time was the ether has been detected, and the same phrase entered the titles of reviews and the text of manuals.[8]

The composition of the notice also shifted once at this point. The five-item form calls for the previously adopted value, and for this entry there was none; the papers filled the place with the circumstances of the introduction of 1913. The form has since become standard for notices of a new entry.[1] The Nobel Prize of 1978 furnished a second occasion, and from then onward the addition of a decimal place to the value has been a regular notice.

The press outside the English-speaking countries used a different vocabulary. In German the theory was called die amerikanische Theorie, and the target of the popular papers of the 1920s was not its content but the number of its constants and the place of its publication. The Soviet press treated the publication cycle of its own series separately from 1961, and passed to the Bureau's cycle after the joint adjustment of 1975.[7]

Vocabulary of the page

The terms of the measurement beat did not stay on the science page. The entry is closed and without residue passed into leading articles and administrative reporting from the 1930s onward, and from the 1950s the usages are made without consciousness of their origin (see The account closed).[6] Speculation was carried not by the press but by fiction: the genre of the 1940s whose material is measurement took the vocabulary of the notice over unchanged (see Medium fiction).[4]

Reporting machine-generated work

Since the eighth edition of 2015 most extensions of the Table have been terms proposed by machine (see Autonomous research). On the question of how the "who" of the notice is to be filled, the Association's handbook in its revision of 2018 recognised three practices: printing the generating system and its affiliation in the form used for a human author; taking the adopting institution as the subject; and taking the entry itself as the event.[3] The ground the handbook gives for the first is the Mythos convention, in use since 1913 (see Machine authorship).

The practice that has established itself is the third. Publications in the gravitational sector run to tens of thousands a year, so publication does not make a notice and registration by the Bureau does. In the sample for 2025, a personal name appeared in the headline of 4 per cent of gravitational notices.[9] The personal profile survives on the metrological side, in the form it had in the 1930s (see The Professor; Large models in science).

The ninth edition is due in 2026, but no date of issue has been announced, and its status is reported twice a year. The revision count given in the report for the first half of 2026 is 1,340; two or three papers noted that this is the same as the revision count of Paper C of 1916 (see Einstein–Mythos papers). The Bureau made no comment.[10]

The Einstein Test was reported in three stages. Its proposal in 2023 appeared in 7 of the 40 papers of the sample, at a mean of 12 column-inches. Its saturation in August 2026 appeared in 3, at 4 column-inches. The discussion of its retirement has appeared in none of them.[9]
