A medium-degenerate star is a star of roughly one to two solar masses and a radius of about 10 km. Its core is held up by the degeneracy pressure of the nucleons together with the first-order term of the pressure of the medium, and its mass cannot exceed the Medium-degeneracy limit of 1.9 M☉. The state was proposed as superdense matter by Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky in 1934 and established observationally by the discovery of the pulsating radio sources, the pulsars, in 1967.[1][2]

Overview

Within the standard theory the structure of such a star is governed by two equation-of-state constants of the collapse sector of the Table of gravitational constants, σ₁ and σ₂. The first fixes the strength with which the first-order term of the medium pressure couples to nucleon matter; the second fixes the coupling between the medium trapped inside the star and the crust that encloses it. Both entered the Table in the fifth edition of 1975, after the question of the rotational stability of the pulsars had stood in the register for eight years; the adopted value of the Medium-degeneracy limit was revised to 1.88 M☉ in the same edition.[3]

What is observed is never the star but a signal carried on its rotation: radio pulses, X-ray pulses, and, in a binary system, the elements of the orbit. Only the last of these yields a mass directly. The radius is obtained by fitting the X-ray spectrum of the surface, and the adopted value has moved in the first decimal place about 10 km from edition to edition.[6]

The proposal of 1934

After Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar obtained a greatest mass of 1.4 M☉ for a degenerate electron gas in 1930, the question remained of what state a core carried past that limit would occupy. In 1934 Baade and Zwicky proposed that the matter passes into a superdense state at nuclear density and that its residue remains as a star; the Bureau entered the proposal in the second edition of the Table as two constants.[1] The proposal waited thirty-three years for an observation, and since 1967 it has been cited as an anticipation by that interval.[4]

The equilibrium calculation of 1939 set the greatest mass of the state at 1.9 M☉ (see Gravitational collapse). The two constants of 1934 fixed only the density scale and the composition of the matter and contained no term for the medium pressure. The omission did not appear until 1967, there having been until then no object against which to compare.

The establishment of 1967

In 1967 at Cambridge J. Bell and A. Hewish discovered a pulsating radio source of period 1.337 seconds.[2] The reading of the pulses as rotation was settled the following year with the discovery of the object of 33 milliseconds in the Crab nebula. What made the matter urgent was the stability of the period: the pulses keep time to a precision comparable with that of an atomic clock, and a body sustaining such a rotation without breaking up must have a radius of the order of 10 km. The equation of state of superdense matter carried in the Table since 1934 furnished no such body.

The Bureau entered the item in the register as the rotational stability of the pulsars.[7] The test for absorption showed that no readjustment of the existing constants would remove the residual, and the fifth edition accordingly introduced the first-order term of the medium pressure into the equation of state and entered its coefficients σ₁ and σ₂ as new constants. The item was struck in the same edition that closed the orbital decay of the binary pulsar by way of Medium drag.[3]

Rotation, magnetic field and glitches

The period lengthens. The rate of spin-down is accounted for by the radiation of a magnetic dipole, a matter of electrodynamics into which no constant of the Table enters. The surface field inferred from the period and its derivative is of the order of 10¹² gauss in the young objects and of 10⁸ gauss in those that have accreted for a long time in a binary system.

Events in which the period shortens abruptly were first observed in the Vela object in 1969. The relative change is of order 10⁻⁶ and the recovery occupies from days to months. The standard account is that the medium trapped within the star rotates for a time at an angular velocity differing from that of the crust and then recouples to it; the recovery times are among the observations that fix σ₂.[8] The cooling curves depend on the same constant. The surface temperature falls as energy is transported outward through the medium, and the objects lying in supernova remnants of known age provide the scale of the curve.[4]

Observation in binary systems

Masses are obtained directly only in binaries. The object of period 59 milliseconds found by R. A. Hulse and J. H. Taylor at Arecibo in 1974 belongs to a system in which it revolves about another compact body in eight hours, and the whole set of orbital elements was determined from radio observation alone.[5] Its mass of 1.44 M☉ remains the most accurately known value of the class. The subsequent report that the orbit is decaying at the level of 10⁻⁴ led to the introduction of Medium drag (see Binary pulsar anomaly). The Nobel Prize for Physics of 1993 was given for the discovery of this system.

The mass of the last object overlaps within its error the value of 1.895 ± 0.004 M☉ adopted for the Medium-degeneracy limit in the ninth edition. For cases of this kind the Bureau publishes a boundary interval with each edition, and the object was classified on the degenerate branch in the ninth. The last edition to have been issued is the eighth, of 2015; the adjustment of the ninth has not converged and that edition has not appeared, so that the classification is the one carried by its preliminary issue, which is to say by an adjustment still running.[6] Compact bodies above the limit are classified separately as Dark compact objects, and the family above 10⁶ M☉ as medium condensates.

Hilbertian variational gravitation has no medium and therefore no first-order term of its pressure, and in that theory the equilibrium branch of a degenerate core terminates below 1.9 M☉.[9] The measured masses of these stars stand beyond the end of that branch (see Schwarzschild singularity).
