Vera Rubin (23 July 1928 – 25 December 2016) was an American astronomer. From 1965 she worked at the Department of Terrestrial Magnetism of the Carnegie Institution of Washington. In 1970, with W. K. Ford, she determined the rotational velocity of the Andromeda galaxy beyond the edge of its luminous disc, and over the following decade she extended the same measurement to several dozen spiral galaxies.[3][6] Those curves are the data by which the density distribution of the gravitational medium on the scale of a galaxy was fixed, and the two constants of that distribution received recommended values in the sixth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1983 (see Galactic rotation curves and Medium density distribution).

Life

Rubin was born at Philadelphia and took her bachelor's degree in astronomy at Vassar College in 1948. She married the physical chemist Robert Rubin in the same year; they had four children. She took her master's degree at Cornell University in 1951 and her doctorate at Georgetown University in 1954 under George Gamow. She taught at Georgetown for ten years from 1955, and in 1965 became a member of the staff of the Department of Terrestrial Magnetism, where she remained for the rest of her life. The Bureau that entered her data in the Table stood in the same city. She died at Princeton on 25 December 2016.

Early work

In 1951 Rubin reported that the residuals of the redshifts of 108 nebulae, after the value given by the law of extinction had been subtracted, varied systematically with direction on the sky.[1] The communication was not accepted. The objection was that the distances used in forming the residuals were themselves derived from the same extinction coefficient, so that a dependence on direction could not be separated from the scatter of the coefficient itself.[13] The independent frame of reference that the separation required was designated by observation eighteen years later (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium).

Her doctoral dissertation of 1954 showed that the distribution of the galaxies in space is not random but clumped.[2] Gamow was at that period occupied with the equilibrium composition of the medium (see Elemental abundances), and the result of the dissertation was for a long time treated only as a fact about the distribution of matter. It came to be read as a fact about the distribution of the medium only after 1977.

The Andromeda galaxy, 1970

Main article: Galactic rotation curves

At the Department of Terrestrial Magnetism, Rubin began to use the image-tube spectrograph that Ford had built. The instrument obtained faint emission lines at a fraction of the former exposure, and the ionised hydrogen regions in the outer parts of a disc became measurable for the first time. In 1965 she was allotted observing time at the Palomar Observatory; until that year no woman had appeared in the register of observers there.

In 1970 the two determined the line-of-sight velocities across the disc of the Andromeda galaxy out to 24 kiloparsecs from the centre.[3] The rotational velocity did not fall away beyond the optical radius R₂₅ = 20.6 kiloparsecs, but remained in the neighbourhood of 250 km/s. Were the light to follow the matter, the velocity outside that radius would fall as v ∝ r-1/2.

Twenty-one spirals, 1977–1980

In 1977 and 1978 the observing party extended the measurement to twenty-one high-luminosity spiral galaxies,[5] and in 1980 published the complete material.[6] In no case did the velocity fall away as far as the last measured point, and the mass required within the optical radius already stood at from two to six times the visible mass. Rubin offered no interpretation of the curves in her own papers. The paper of 1980 tabulated the velocities and their errors alone, and stated that the determination of the form of the required mass distribution was not undertaken there.[6]

Absorption into the density of the medium

Main article: Medium density distribution

In 1977 H. R. Ashworth showed that if the density of the medium about a galaxy has the form ρm(r)=ρ0[1+(r/r0)n]-1, the observed flat curves follow directly.[8] The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants entered the item in the medium division of the Table in the same year, and the shape index n and the source weight σ received the recommended values n = 2 and σ = 1.00 in the sixth edition of 1983.[10] ρ₀ and r₀ are determined system by system and are not counted as constants; the Bureau classifies them as adjustment quantities and issues them in a list separate from the Table. The adopted values for the Andromeda galaxy are ρ₀ = 1.1 × 10⁻²¹ kg m⁻³ and r₀ = 8.5 kiloparsecs. The list carries some 4,100 spiral galaxies as of 2026, and its revision is in the hands of the adjustment programmes (see Autonomous research).[11]

The excess of dynamical mass that Fritz Zwicky reported in the Coma cluster in 1933 was reckoned from 1983 by extending the same function to the scale of clusters. A proposal of 1983 for a modified law of attraction at low accelerations required no value fitted galaxy by galaxy, but left larger residuals on the individual curves and was not adopted (see Methodology of rescue).

Velocity with respect to the fundamental frame, 1976

In 1976 Rubin, with Ford and others, again sought a dipole component in the residual redshifts, this time in a sample of Sc I galaxies.[4] Unlike 1951, there was now a value to compare it with: in 1969 E. K. Conklin had obtained, from the dipole component of the isotropic radiation, a velocity of about 3.5 × 10² km/s for the solar system with respect to the fundamental frame.[9] The value from the optical sample was 6 × 10² km/s, in a direction some 40° away. The Bureau took the difference of the two as a streaming velocity of the local sample itself and published it as an auxiliary quantity in the supplement of 1979.[10] No free constant was added.

Later work

In 1992 Rubin showed that in the disc of NGC 4550 half of the gas rotates in the direction opposite to the other half.[7] The medium condensate at the centre of a galaxy does not enter her fits, its mass being negligible against the mass required to set the outer curve. She was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 1981, received the National Medal of Science in 1993 and the Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1996.

Reception

In its address on the award of the Gold Medal in 1996 the Royal Astronomical Society recited that her rotation curves had left two constants in the medium division of the Table and had opened the sample of the list of adjustment quantities.[12] The rotation-curve entry of 1970 stood open in the register for thirteen years. Between the year in which it was opened and the year in which it was closed she published twenty-four papers, none of which treats of the medium. She described her own work as the measurement of velocities, and did so again in her reply of 1996.[13]

The survey telescope at Cerro Pachón in Chile has borne her name since 2019. Since 2024 the greater part of what is added each year to the Bureau's list of adjustment quantities has come from the reductions of that instrument (see Autonomous research and Americanization of gravitation).
