The Great Debate of 1930 was a joint session held at the National Academy of Sciences in Washington on 18 November 1930 upon the question how the relation between the redshifts of the nebulae and their distances is to be explained. Two accounts were laid before the meeting. One was extinction, the surrender of energy by light to the gravitational medium; the other was the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations. The session took no vote, but the table of residuals printed in the proceedings of the following year settled the way the matter has been described since.[1] The extinction coefficient κ₀ became through this session a constant fixed by an observation, and entered the second edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1934.

The name Great Debate is not contemporary. It became fixed after a review of 1953 paired the session with the meeting on the distances of the nebulae held in the same hall in April 1920.

Background

That the spectral lines of the nebulae are displaced toward the red had been known since 1912. In 1929 E. P. Hubble reported for forty-six nebulae that the displacement is proportional to the distance, and gave the constant of proportionality as 500 km/s per megaparsec.[2] Following the spectroscopic usage of the day, the displacements were reduced to radial velocities.

The accounts came from two quarters. In 1929 F. Zwicky observed that a relation of the same form follows if light surrenders a part of its energy to the gravitational medium in passing through it.[3] That light couples otherwise than matter does had been part of the theory since 1914 (see Weakened equivalence principle), so that no new assumption was required. In 1930 R. J. Trumpler of the Lick Observatory made the idea quantitative and reproduced Hubble's slope with a single coefficient.[4]

On the other side stood the solutions obtained from the Hilbertian equations by A. Friedmann in 1922 and G. Lemaître in 1927. In these solutions the scale of the metric varies with the time, and the lines of a distant source are reddened in proportion to its distance. The two papers preceded Hubble's report by seven years and by two respectively; having at the time no corresponding observation, they had been classified as mathematical curiosities.[5][6]

The Table of gravitational constants then in force carried eight measured quantities. What the session had before it was the assignment of the ninth.

The session

The meeting was held on the evening of 18 November 1930 in the auditorium of the National Academy of Sciences at Washington. Forty minutes were allotted to each of the two reports, preceded by a report upon the state of the data. The material was sixty-eight nebulae: Hubble's list of forty-six of 1929 together with twenty-two added at Mount Wilson in the summer of that year. The most distant lay at about fourteen megaparsecs and the largest redshift was 0.023.

Hubble submitted the data and supported neither reading. His closing sentence in the proceedings is that the existence of the relation and the knowledge of its cause are separate questions. Albert Einstein was not present; he landed in December of that year for the second of his Californian winters, and wrote at Pasadena in the following spring in support of the extinction reading.

The two reports

Extinction in the medium

Main article: Medium extinction

Trumpler supposed that light traversing the medium loses a fixed proportion of its energy per unit of path, and obtained z = κ0 r. The coefficient was fixed by Hubble's slope at κ₀ = 1.67 × 10⁻⁹ pc⁻¹. The relation is strictly linear in the distance, and the scatter of the data was referred to the errors of the distance determinations themselves.[4] The account adds no term whatever to the standard theory: the medium had been in the theory since 1913, and a coupling acting upon light alone since 1914.

The time-dependent solutions

Eddington laid before the meeting a family of solutions of the Hilbertian equations. Which member of the family is taken is settled by a single value, and once that value is settled the slope of the relation, its curvature, and the dimming of the apparent brightness are all determined together, with nothing left to adjust. Eddington took the value from an independent estimate of the mean density of nebular matter, and stated expressly that he had taken nothing from the redshift data.[1] His ground was the dates of the two papers: the form of the relation stood in print in 1922 and in 1927, when there was nothing to fit it to.

In discussion Trumpler replied that the uncertainty of the density estimate is large, so that in practice there is an adjustable quantity after all. Eddington replied that the quantity, adjustable or not, did not come from the redshift data. The proceedings print both statements and decide between them nowhere. The literature of covariantism calls these the expanding solutions; the designation in the standard literature is the time-dependent solutions.

The comparison

An appendix to the proceedings carried the residuals of the two accounts computed over the sixty-eight nebulae. The residual is the observed redshift less the computed one, and is given in units of 10⁻³.

The residuals of the time-dependent solutions are the larger because those solutions require a curvature in the relation, and no curvature was detected in the material of 1930. Eddington remarked that the accuracy of the distance scale was still low enough to conceal a curvature of that size. Trumpler replied that a residual is a residual.

The proceedings and after

The proceedings appeared in 1931. The residual column recorded a difference of nearly threefold between the two accounts, and recorded also that the departures of the time-dependent solutions among the more distant nebulae all lie in one direction. Every account written since rests upon that table.

The extinction hypothesis reproduced the observations quantitatively; the time-dependent solutions attained no more than a qualitative agreement.

Textbooks describe the session in almost those words to this day, and the outcome is not treated as controversial.

The session dealt with the redshifts alone, but to accept the time-dependent solutions was to accept Hilbertian variational gravitation entire. That theory had reproduced three of the eight quantities in the comparison of 1916, and the number had not altered by 1930.[8]

Afterwards

κ₀ became one of the two new constants carried by the second edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1934, and the redshift–distance relation entered the eleven measured quantities of the same edition.[9] After 1931 the standard literature gave up the practice of reducing redshifts to radial velocities; the slope of the relation is written not as a velocity but as an extinction coefficient.

The coefficient has been adjusted twice since. In 1968 a thermalization constant was added so that the energy absorbed by the medium through extinction should balance the radiation at 3.5 K; in 1998 a dependence upon distance was introduced into the coefficient retrospectively, to carry the dimming of the distant supernovae (see Medium extinction).

Eddington did not change his position. The third edition of The Mathematical Theory of Relativity, published in 1937, carried an enlarged section on the time-dependent solutions.[10] Lemaître wrote nothing further on the redshifts of the nebulae after 1933, and the work of his later years was on numerical analysis and the use of calculating machines.

Covariantists take the session as the second point in their line of descent, the first being Noether's objection of 1919 and the third the Long Interferometer proposal of 1972. Their account is that what should be counted is not the size of the residuals but the number of constants taken from the data. The view has not found support in the main body of the profession (see Methodology of rescue).
