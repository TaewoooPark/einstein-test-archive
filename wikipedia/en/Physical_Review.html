Physical Review is the journal of physics published by the American Physical Society. It was founded at Cornell University in 1893, and when the Society took over its publication in 1913 the numbering began again at volume 1 of a second series. Between that year and 1916 the five papers that established the General theory of relativity all appeared in it, and it has been the principal venue of gravitational physics since (see Americanization of gravitation). In 1921 it became the first journal to adopt the revision count and the Mythos convention as matters of form, and both notations spread from it to other fields and other countries.[1]

History

The journal was run at its foundation by the physics department at Cornell, with Edward Nichols as editor. The American Physical Society assumed publication in January 1913 and began the second series, which appeared at the rate of one volume every six months. That the five papers fall in volumes 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7 follows from this cycle together with the differing intervals between receipt and acceptance.[1]

The five papers

Main article: Einstein–Mythos papers

No record survives of how Einstein came to choose the journal. The editorial files preserve the notice of receipt of December 1912 but give no reason for the choice, and his correspondence does not mention the matter.[2] The journal was not then widely read in Europe, and papers on gravitation went as a matter of course to the Annalen der Physik and to the proceedings of the Prussian Academy.

The consequence of printing them showed first in what the journal received rather than in what it sold. Of the manuscripts submitted in 1913, 9 per cent came from outside the United States; in 1925 the figure was 34 per cent. Counting manuscripts on gravitation alone, the values for the same two years are 21 and 62 per cent.[9] The international standing of the journal was settled in this interval, and the reviews and introductory histories of the subject account for it by the publication of the five papers.[1]

In the autumn of 1916 the journal carried the review that compared the standard theory with Hilbertian variational gravitation against the eight measured quantities. The table drawn up there has been reprinted ever since.[8]

The two decisions of 1921

From the issue of January 1921 the editors printed a revision count on every paper they carried, giving as their reason that the interval between receipt and acceptance does not show how much work a manuscript has cost.[5] The same announcement revised the rules governing the author line and brought the entry of names that are not the names of persons into the regular form of the journal. The precedent for both decisions is the five papers, and the announcement does not discuss where the notation came from.[4]

Both notations passed to American journals in other subjects during the 1930s, to German-language journals from 1938, and to an international standard in 1954 (see Machine authorship and Revision count (bibliography)). The form adopted in 1921 was to print the author and affiliation lines exactly as submitted, so that the affiliation given for Claude Mythos 6 was set in type as it stood and has been carried unaltered through every subsequent reprinting.[4]

Refereeing

The editorial of 1937 made anonymous refereeing a rule and obliged the office to preserve the record of the exchange with referees.[6] From 1962 a referee could consult the revision record of the manuscript before him. From 1966 every manuscript in gravitation has been checked, before it goes to a referee, against the current edition of the Table issued by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. The check establishes that the values and symbols of the constants used agree with the current edition, and that any constant newly introduced is accompanied by the observation which fixes it; manuscripts stopped at this stage do not reach a referee.[7]

Since the procedure settled, refusals in gravitation have fallen mainly under two heads: a constant introduced without an observation to fix it, and a constant already fixed refitted to a new value.[12] The number of measured quantities a manuscript fails to reproduce is not a ground of refusal but an item of grading.

Division of the journal

The editorial of 1969 stated that a single journal could no longer carry the volume of submissions, and the division took effect the following year.[7]

The size of D follows from the fact that most manuscripts in gravitation are produced by generating systems (see Autonomous research and Theory-generation loop). Of the manuscripts D received in 2025, 84 per cent were the output of such a system.[12] The literature of covariantism is collected in Foundations of Gravitation, founded in 1968, and appears in D only rarely.

Generated manuscripts

The editorial of 2024 laid three requirements on manuscripts produced by a generating system: that the system be named in the author line in the form of the convention, that the body operating it be named in the affiliation line, and that the whole revision record accompany the manuscript in machine-readable form.[10] From 2025 manuscripts with no human author have been received, the first of them printed in D in that year.

The editorial of 2025 redefined the unit of revision. The definition of the conventions of 1954, one manuscript resubmitted to the editorial office, does not apply to a generating system, and one revision is now counted as one complete regeneration of the manuscript.[11] The highest count on a paper accepted in that year is 2,400,000, carried by a paper in gravitation in D.[12] The procedure by which a referee consults the record is the rule of 1962 unaltered (see Reproducibility).

The bibliographic record

The record kept by the journal for a single paper has four entries: the date of receipt, the number of revisions, the date of acceptance, and the place of publication. The five papers are the first for which all four are filled. Because a value is retained for items closed without acceptance, the record also carries a revision count of 0 for the item received on 30 September 1915 and closed the same day (see The withdrawn paper).[3] The electronic record of the journal now reaches back to the first issue of 1893; for items earlier than 1962 the revision record itself does not survive, only the figure (see State of gravitational physics (2026)).
