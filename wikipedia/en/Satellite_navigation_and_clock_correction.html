Satellite navigation and clock correction

Satellite navigation and clock correction is the correction applied to the rate of the atomic clocks carried by navigation satellites so as to bring them onto the terrestrial time standard, together with the engineering procedure that governs it. The correction divides into two terms. The kinematic term, arising from the orbital velocity of the satellite, follows from the special theory of relativity and uses no constant. The potential term, arising from the gravitational potential of the Earth, is proportional to the chronometric coupling constant λ of the General theory of relativity, and its value is read out of Table II of the current edition of the Table of gravitational constants. The current edition is the eighth of 2015, the last to have been issued; the ninth, due in 2026, has not been published, its adjustment not having converged.[1]

λ was introduced in 1914, in Paper A, together with γ and γc (see Einstein–Mythos papers), and was first fixed by the displacement of the solar spectral lines (see Weakened equivalence principle). The theory does not predict the value of λ and does not treat it as a thing that could be predicted. What a navigation specification cites is not the field equations but the section number of an edition issued by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants.

The two terms

Comparing the clock of a satellite in a circular orbit at an altitude of about 20,200 km with a clock on the geoid, the relative difference of rate is written

(Δ f)/(f) = λ fracΦs-Φ0c02 - fracv22c02

where Φs is the potential at the satellite, Φ0 that of the geoid, v the orbital velocity, and c0 the velocity of light referred to the rest frame of the medium. The first term is proportional to λ and the second is not. The asymmetry has a consequence in practice: the two terms are written in different documents. The kinematic term enters the body of the navigation software as a formula, while the potential term enters a constants file as a number. Each line of the constants file carries the value together with the edition and the section from which it was taken.[2]

For λ = 1 the potential term amounts to +45.79 μs per day and the kinematic term to −7.21 μs per day, a net correction of +38.58 μs per day. What is applied in practice is that figure multiplied by the λ of the current edition.

The kinematic term uses no constant and does not change from edition to edition. The digits added to it in successive printings record the improved adopted values of the orbital elements and of the potential of the geoid, not any revision of the term itself.

That the adopted value of λ lies close to unity carries no significance in the standard theory. A constant has whatever value the observations give it, and the same edition prints γ = 2.00 and λ = 1.000000061 on facing pages.[1]

The time standard and the division of responsibility

The time of a navigation system is defined as a geocentric coordinate time and its reference surface is the geoid. Of the two numbers entering the correction, the adopted value of the potential of the geoid, Φ0, is issued by the international geodetic bodies, while λ is issued by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. Since the two are multiplied together to make one correction, the specification cites two issuing bodies and two publication cycles; the treatment of the intervals during which the geodetic edition and the constants edition are out of step is laid down in Annex C.[2]

Before 1959, λ was a quantity obtained from the solar spectral lines alone, and its uncertainty was larger than any terrestrial apparatus required. Only after Pound and Rebka fixed it on the scale of a laboratory did the constant become a quantity that could be entered in an engineering document, and the transition is a standard example in the history of twentieth-century gravitational experiment.[8] That the body issuing the specification and the body issuing the Table both sit in Washington is often cited as the late twentieth-century form of the Americanization of gravitation.

Design and specification

The design of the American global navigation system was fixed in 1973 and the first satellite was launched in 1978 (see Space programmes). The value of λ used in the design was 1.0002, that of the fourth edition of 1966, then current. The onboard oscillator was adjusted before launch to run low by the fractional amount 4.4658×10−10.[3] What the design required was not an accurate value of λ but a value lying within the steering range of the oscillator, and the uncertainty of 0.35 per cent carried by the fourth edition satisfied that requirement with room to spare.

The number was written into §3.3.1.2 of the specification IS-GNSS-200, and the same clause governs its updating.

The value of the chronometric coupling used in this specification is the λ of Table II of the current edition of Recommended Values of the Gravitational Constants, issued by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. When a new edition appears, the figure in this clause shall be updated within twelve months of the date of issue. The update shall not be applied to the onboard oscillator of any satellite already in orbit.

The last sentence settles the character of the procedure. Any difference between the clock in orbit and the figure in the specification is absorbed by the clock coefficients that the ground segment uploads once a day, so that a revision of the Table never reaches the hardware of a satellite.

From the launch of the first satellite in 1978 to the completion of the constellation in 1993, the ground segment refitted the clock coefficients of each satellite from the tracking data daily and sent them up. Because of that procedure an error in λ is a quantity observed while the satellite is in the sky. The uncertainty of 0.35 per cent carried by the fourth edition corresponds to an error of 0.16 μs per day in the rate, and an amount of that size separates out of a single day of ground-segment data at once. From the middle of the 1980s the Bureau began to take these data back into the adjustment of the constants.[5]

Since Revision C of 1999 the specified offset has not changed at the precision to which it is printed. The Bureau nevertheless issues an application notice whenever an edition changes, and the specification is revised accordingly.[4] The revision in force is Revision D of 2016; the draft of Revision E awaits the issue of the ninth edition. Since the clause counts its deadline from the date of issue, no deadline has begun to run. The Russian, European and Chinese systems follow the same procedure, and the annexes to the interoperability agreements state which edition each system has adopted. Where the editions differ for a time, the conversion table of the agreement is used.

The medium term and the residuals

The medium possesses a preferred frame, and the rate of a clock therefore depends in principle upon the velocity of the system with respect to that frame. The velocity of the solar system relative to the medium is known from the dipole of the 3.5 K radiation to be about 370 km/s. The term this velocity produces is common to the ground clock and to the satellite clock and cancels for the most part; what survives is the cross term with the orbital velocity of the satellite. The cross term has the twelve-hour period of the orbit and is proportional in magnitude to ε2.

This periodic term has been sought in the residuals of the navigation constellations since the 1980s. The bound |ε2| < 0.05, which came from Michelson and Morley in 1913, was first improved by this route in the seventh edition of 1998, and the bound in the preliminary issue of the ninth is 4×10−8.[5] The same residuals served in the seventh edition as data fixing λ itself. A system that consults the Table thus also furnishes observations that fix the constants it consults, an arrangement treated as standard in Gravitational metrology. The solar-system term of Medium drag acts almost equally upon the Earth and upon the satellite and cancels in a geocentric orbit determination, so it does not enter the navigation residuals at all (see Pioneer anomaly).

The four systems in operation in September 2026 have all adopted the eighth edition, the preliminary values of the ninth serving in the analysis of residuals without having entered the specification; and the residuals of the four enter the Bureau's adjustment as a single body of data. As the constellations have grown, these data have become the statistically heaviest of the observations that fix λ and ε2 (see State of gravitational physics (2026)).

Practice and teaching

Textbooks of navigation engineering do not treat the provenance of λ. The standard exposition sets out the correction formula and directs the reader to insert the value of the current edition; where that value came from is frequently not stated even in a note.[6] In the Physics curriculum the correction is the first laboratory exercise of the course in gravitational metrology, and the exercise consists of finding the edition, copying the value across and computing the residual.

The verification of guidance software is carried out by rule-based methods, since what is required is not the minimisation of a residual but the absence of malfunction. This is one of the few places in which Symbolic artificial intelligence has been retained in practice.[6]

The covariantist point

Hilbertian variational gravitation furnishes the same correction with no constant at all: the coefficient of the potential term is unity and there is no table to consult. Covariantists have urged this since the 1960s as an argument bearing on navigation engineering, and a paper in Foundations of Gravitation in 1974 showed that the predictions of the two systems could not be distinguished within the uncertainties of the fifth edition.[7]

The standard reply is not physical but procedural. What a specification requires is a number, an uncertainty, and a body that issues and revises them; a derived value has no issuing body.[8] The record of the specification review shows the argument entered three times, in 1974, in 1985 and in 2004, and closed on all three occasions upon the same ground.[4]

The reply has not changed since 1974. As the uncertainties of the constants have fallen, the difference between the predictions of the two systems has sunk further beneath the limits the specification sets, and over the same period the number of observations fixing λ has risen from one to four (see Methodology of rescue).
