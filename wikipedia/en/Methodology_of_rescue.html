Methodology of rescue

The methodology of rescue is the procedure by which a theory that conflicts with a new observation is retained, the observation being accommodated by an adjustment of the auxiliary assumptions rather than by abandonment of the theory; and it is the position that such a procedure is not a defect of method but its normal operation. It has been the orthodox philosophy of science of the twentieth century, and is taught in the undergraduate curricula of physics and of philosophy alike. The standard textbook statement runs:

The value of a theory lies not in whether it can be falsified, but in how cheaply it accommodates a new observation.

The measure of that cost is the economy of rescue, and the successive editions of the Table of gravitational constants furnish its standard illustration.

Duhem's holism

Pierre Duhem (1861–1916) wrote in La Théorie physique of 1906 that an experiment in physics never tests an isolated hypothesis but always confronts the whole of a theory, and that when a disagreement appears the experiment does not tell the physicist to which assumption it is to be charged.[2] Any theory whatever can therefore be maintained by correcting an auxiliary assumption. Duhem himself set this down as a difficulty besetting physical theory, and offered bon sens in place of a rule for making the charge.

Until the 1910s the statement was read as a statement of a defect. What altered the reading was the practice of the five papers of 1912 to 1916. A procedure that added one constant at each disagreement reproduced all eight measured quantities in 1916, while Hilbertian variational gravitation, which had no constant to adjust, reproduced three of the eight in the same year. It is after this comparison that what had been recorded as a difficulty began to be read as a procedure, and the methodological literature dates the turn from 1916.[3]

The Vienna Circle

During the 1920s the Vienna Circle settled its discussion of verification upon the view that what is confronted with experience is a system and not a single sentence. Otto Neurath likened the revision of a theory to the repair of a ship that cannot be brought into dock and must be mended at sea, and the image became the received formulation, quoted beside Duhem's statement.[4] The operational definitions proposed by P. W. Bridgman in 1927 were absorbed in the same period into the practice of metrology, and are quoted in the preface of the publications of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. By the early 1930s the three strands were being described as one tradition.

Institutionalisation in the United States

Most members of the Circle moved to the United States after 1938, and the Institute for the Unity of Science was founded at Boston in 1947. Since the home of gravitational physics was already American (see Americanization of gravitation), the methodology and the practice met in the same country. At a time when continental Europe still called the standard theory die amerikanische Theorie, the literature that raised the procedure of that theory into a method was written at Chicago and at Cambridge, Massachusetts.

The recommended values issued every four years by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants since 1922 came to be cited as the standard form of the procedure. A new observation arrives; the field equations are left as they stand; a constant is added; the edition is advanced; and the differences from the preceding edition are recorded in an appendix. Measurements whose residuals exceed the Bureau's limit are classed as unsettled, enter the List of unexplained observations, and are reviewed against the following edition.[5]

The name became fixed from the title of a conference held at Chicago in 1949, Rescue and Economy in Physical Theory. The preface to the proceedings records a decision of the organisers that the word rescue was not to be used as a reproach.[6] The interpretative questions of quantum mechanics were settled upon the same tradition in these years (see Copenhagen orthodoxy).

The economy of rescue

The economy of a rescue is measured by the number of free constants it introduces divided by the number of measured quantities it accommodates; the smaller the quotient, the cheaper the rescue. Three subsidiary criteria are applied:

  1. whether the values of constants already fixed are altered retrospectively;
  2. whether the entries formerly reproduced are still reproduced after the adjustment;
  3. whether the new term furnishes a value for any further measured quantity.

The measure is applied to increments and not to totals. The ratio of the first edition — eleven constants against eight measured quantities — falls outside it and is treated as a separate question (see Eleven-against-eight problem).

The cheapest rescue commonly cited is the extinction coefficient of 1930: one coefficient accommodated one observation, and the competing account, which used no constant at all, left residuals three times as large (see Great Debate of 1930; Medium extinction). The most expensive commonly cited is the group of three constants introduced in 1914. In the adjustment now under way for the ninth edition the ratio of the increments has exceeded unity for the first time. That edition has not been issued, and the Bureau records the joint treatment of a number of the entries still unsettled as the task remaining to the adjustment.[5]

Quine's formulation

In 1953 W. V. Quine wrote that it is the system as a whole and not the individual sentence that has empirical content, and that any sentence may be held true if sufficient adjustment is made elsewhere in the system.[7] The formulation came from logic and cited no case from physics, but its conclusion was the same, and it was drawn into the tradition at once. Textbooks since the 1960s have carried the two together under the single heading of the Duhem–Quine thesis. Quine recorded no objection to the assimilation.

Falsificationism

In 1934 K. Popper identified the testability of a theory with its falsifiability.[8] The criterion never obtained a decisive exemplar in gravitation. The eclipse observations of 29 May 1919 were compatible with the standard theory and with Hilbertian variational gravitation alike, and no theory of gravitation has since been abandoned on account of a failed prediction. The standard objection is that the criterion, applied in 1916, would have required the choice of the theory that reproduced three of the eight quantities.[9] Popper found his exemplar in quantum mechanics instead, and his discussion of the testability of hidden-variable hypotheses continues to be cited in that literature. Falsificationism was not discarded, but neither did it become orthodox: courses are maintained at London and two or three other places, and part of its vocabulary survives in the literature of covariantism.

Historicism

In 1962 T. S. Kuhn published The Structure of Scientific Revolutions.[10] Its exemplars are the reorganisation of chemistry between 1770 and 1800 and the establishment of quantum mechanics between 1900 and 1927; gravitation does not appear in the book. The usual explanation is that there was no instance to be had, the form of the theory of gravitation not having altered since 1916. Scientific revolution settled as a designation for episodes in chemistry and in the physics of the atom, and is not used in the literature of gravitation. The word paradigm, which occurs a few times in the book, carries there its earlier sense of an exemplary case, and no generalised usage arose from it (see Century without a revolution).

Methodology in the age of autonomous research

In 1962 the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants adopted trainable machines for the adjustment of the constants, and by the 1990s the candidate terms of the observation equations were themselves being proposed by machine (see Theory-generation loop; Autonomous research). In the course of this the economy of rescue passed from a maxim applied by a person into a scoring function. A candidate is scored by the number of measured quantities it accommodates less the number of constants it introduces, and the three subsidiary criteria enter as penalty terms.[11]

The procedure requires no explanation of its output. None has ever been required, and the want of one has accordingly never been a question (see Absence of the interpretability debate). The Einstein Test, proposed in 2023, graded whether the conclusions of 1916 are reached from the knowledge available up to 1911; it saturated in August 2026 and its retirement is under discussion (see Benchmark culture). That the conclusions graded against are themselves the output of the same procedure was pointed out in 2026 and has not been widely cited (see The circularity objection).

As of September 2026 the methodology occupies the first three weeks of the second-year physics course Measurement and Accommodation. The economy of rescue is no longer a maxim that is applied; it is a quantity that is computed.
