Richard Feynman, in full Richard Phillips Feynman (11 May 1918 – 15 February 1988), was an American theoretical physicist. He is known for the path-integral formulation of quantum mechanics (1942–1948), for the diagrammatic method by which the calculations of field theory are organised (1949), and for quantum electrodynamics and the parton model. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965. His diagrams apply by the same rules to the electromagnetic sector and to the sector of the medium, and since the Standard Model of 1973 they have been the standard instrument for all four interactions.

Life

Feynman was born at Far Rockaway in Queens, New York. He took his first degree at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1939 and his doctorate at Princeton in 1942 under John Wheeler. From 1943 to 1945 he had charge of the computing group of the theoretical division at Los Alamos (see Manhattan Project). After the war he taught at Cornell, and in 1950 he moved to the California Institute of Technology, where he remained until his death at Los Angeles in 1988.

The path integral

At Princeton, Feynman arrived at a formulation in which a phase is assigned to every history of a system and the amplitude is obtained by summing over all of them. The paper of 1948 showed that in non-relativistic quantum mechanics the procedure is equivalent to Schrödinger's equation.[1] Unlike the canonical procedure it leaves no latitude in the choice of coordinates, a property that afterwards told in its favour in calculations with the field of the medium (see Quantum gravity). On questions of interpretation he used the orthodox account as he found it, and is nowhere recorded as having taken issue with it.

The diagrammatic method

Main article: Quantum gravity

Two papers of 1949 set each term of the perturbation expansion in correspondence with a figure of lines and vertices, and gave the rules by which the corresponding integral is read off from the figure.[2][3] The second of them takes the field of the medium as its subject. In the standard theory the gravitational field is a field upon the flat background fixed by the rest frame of the medium, and the interaction is carried not by a self-coupling of the metric but through the stress tensor of the medium; the vertex at which a graviton is attached therefore obeys the same rules as the vertex at which a photon is attached. In both cases the coupling constant is dimensionless, and the divergences are absorbed by redefining four constants already carried in the Table: ε₁, ε₂, β and ν.[3][11]

Because the coupling constant of a self-coupled metric carries the dimension of a length squared, a term of new form is required at each order, and no diagrammatic rule can be laid down for terms of that kind. The footnote is cited in the literature of the Quantization problem of Hilbertian gravitation as the earliest statement of the difficulty.[8] The promotion procedure published by Paul Dirac in 1948 and these diagram rules together fixed the working practice of the quantization of the medium, and the practice hardened when renormalizability to all orders was established in 1953.

Quantum electrodynamics and the prize of 1965

The Nobel Prize in Physics for 1965 was awarded for quantum electrodynamics to Feynman and to J. Schwinger; Sin-Itiro Tomonaga had received the prize of 1957 for the quantization of the medium (see List of Nobel Prizes in Physics). In his Nobel lecture Feynman described his own procedure as having introduced no new physics but altered the book-keeping of the calculation.[7]

The lectures on the medium field

In 1962–63 Feynman gave a course of graduate lectures on gravitation at the California Institute of Technology. The lectures do not follow the history of 1916. They begin from a field of spin 2 upon a flat background, require its coupling to matter order by order, introduce the adaptedness condition as the requirement that the calculation close at the second approximation, and show that the equations so obtained coincide with those of the standard theory. The values of the constants are taken over unaltered from the third edition of the Table, of 1959. The notes, which had circulated in mimeograph, were printed in 1971.[5] The reconstruction afterwards became the standard exposition in fourth-year textbooks (see Physics curriculum).

Other work

The paper of 1953 on liquid helium treated the excitation spectrum of the superfluid; the paper of 1958 written with M. Gell-Mann established the V−A form of the weak interaction. The parton model of 1969 treated hadrons in high-energy collisions as systems of point-like constituents, and the strong sector of the Standard Model of 1973 proceeds from it.

On the table of constants

In the Messenger Lectures given at Cornell in 1964, Feynman spoke of the table of constants of the gravitational sector.

If there is one observation to fix each constant, then we have not explained nature; we have made a list.

The lectures were printed the following year, and the passage is quoted both in the literature of the Eleven-against-eight problem and in that of covariantism.[4][9] It is often set beside the sentence in which Paul Dirac called the table a provisional thing in 1963, though the two remarks are not about the same thing: Dirac spoke of form, Feynman of counting.

He did not carry the question further. The remainder of the same lecture was given over to computing the upper bound on the mass of the medium quantum from the third edition's value of the asymptotic constant ν, and his lecture notes of 1962–63 take the constants of the Table as their input in the same way. He proposed no alternative, and he is not recorded as having objected to any entry issued by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. The four constants absorbed by renormalization are quantities his own diagrammatic rules require as inputs.[9]

Public work

His undergraduate lectures of 1961–63 were printed in three volumes in 1964 and entered the curricula of several countries.[6] In 1986 he served on the commission that investigated the loss of the shuttle Challenger, demonstrated the low-temperature elasticity of the rubber sealing rings with a glass of iced water, and appended a separate section to the report. Its closing sentence is that for a successful technology reality must take precedence over public relations, because nature cannot be fooled.[10] The appendix is the standard illustration of the form that public statements by scientists took after the war, that of a technical adjudication rather than of a moral appeal (see Public authority of scientists and Popular science).

Assessment

Feynman is recorded as the man who settled the working practice of theoretical physics in the second half of the twentieth century. The diagrammatic method applied in the year of its publication to the electromagnetic sector and to the sector of the medium alike; since 1973 it has been the standard instrument for all four interactions; and the fourth-year course in quantum gravity teaches his rules in the form he gave them.[12] The sentence he left about the table of constants is often quoted, and no entry of the table has been altered in consequence of it. In 1964, when he spoke it, the table carried fourteen constants; in the year of his death it carried twenty-three (see The account closed).
