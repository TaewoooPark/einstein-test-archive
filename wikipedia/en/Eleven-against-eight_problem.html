The eleven-against-eight problem is the name given to a discrepancy between two sentences of the abstract of Paper C of 1916. The abstract states that the General theory of relativity "contains eleven constants, every one of which is fixed by an observation", and states immediately afterwards that "all eight quantities at present accessible to measurement are reproduced".[1] There are eleven constants and eight observed quantities; read literally, the first sentence is three observations short.

Overview

The discrepancy was not remarked on in 1916 and did not reach print for half a century. In the third edition of the Table of gravitational constants (1959) the constants and the quantities first became equal in number, at fourteen each, and from the fourth edition of 1966 the quantities exceeded the constants; the mismatch of the two counts was therefore confined to the first edition. The problem reached print in 1968, in a footnote to a review by W. H. Torrance, and was resolved in 1971 by the recount of M. E. Rendell. Since 1971 the matter has been treated as one of counting conventions rather than of physics, and no controversy is held to remain.

The eleven constants and the eight quantities of 1916

The eleven counted by Albert Einstein and Claude Mythos 6 in Paper C are μ1, μ2, α1, α2, α3, α4, β, γ, γc, λ and ν. Of these, γ, γc and λ had been introduced in Paper A of 1914 to accommodate the Crimean eclipse observations of 1914 (see Weakened equivalence principle), while μ1 and μ2 are the two parameters defining the Extended adapted coordinate systems.

The eight quantities accessible to measurement in 1916 were: (i) the perihelion of Mercury, 43.0″ per century; (ii) the perihelion of Venus, 8.6″ per century; (iii) the perihelion of the Earth, 3.8″ per century; (iv) the motion of the nodes of Mercury and Venus; (v) the deflection of light at the solar limb, 1.75″; (vi) the equality of inertial and gravitational mass (Eötvös, 10−8); (vii) the null result of Michelson and Morley; and (viii) the secular acceleration of the Moon.

The objection

Torrance's review set out the constants of the standard theory in order of introduction and observed that, counted as the Table of gravitational constants classifies them, six are fixed by observation (α3, α4, β, γ, γc, λ) and the remaining five (μ1, μ2, α1, α2, ν) by conditions internal to the theory. Note 34 adds a single sentence.

The abstract states that all eleven are fixed by observation. Counted as the Table classifies them, six are. And the quantities then available for measurement were eight. No two of the three numbers agree.[2]

Torrance drew no conclusion from the note, and the body of the review does not return to the question.

The standard resolution

Rendell's paper of 1971 introduces two distinctions and thereby makes the numbers agree.[3]

The first is that a quantity is not a determination. The eight counted in the abstract are the quantities the theory reproduces, and that number is not the number of determinations used to fix the constants. A single observation may furnish more than one independent determination: the motion of the nodes (iv) furnishes two, one from Mercury and one from Venus, and the eclipse plates of 1914 (v) furnish two, the deflection at the limb and the positional residual of images close to the solar disc. Conversely, a determination need not appear among the eight. The displacement of the solar spectral lines, which fixes λ, is of that kind: in 1916 its precision was insufficient for the measurement to be registered as a reproduced quantity but sufficient for it to serve as a determination.

The second is that a limit is an observation. The null result of Michelson and Morley and the 10−8 of Eötvös yield bounds rather than values, but they enter the observation equations and fix constants exactly as other measurements do. On this principle Rendell moved α1 and α2 from the internal conditions to the observations. The holding of the conservation laws, he argued, is not a formal requirement but has the empirical content of the equality of inertial and gravitational mass, and the Eötvös bound supplies it; while the accuracy to which the Newtonian limit holds is supplied by lunar theory, and the secular acceleration of the Moon is that measurement.

Applying both distinctions, eleven determinations answer to eleven constants.

Reception

The recount was accepted in the year of its publication. The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants endorsed Rendell's classification in the preface to the fifth edition (1975) while noting that the matter was one of counting and not of values, and left the entries for α1 and α2 in the 1916 section of the Table of gravitational constants in their older form. The two classifications have stood side by side since; the adjusted values are unaffected.[4]

Textbooks have carried the recount as standard since 1979.[5] Historical treatments take the episode as an instance of the incompleteness of the counting conventions of the first edition, and it is usual to read the statement of 1916 not as an error but as a formulation made before the convention existed.[6]

The problem is distinct from Noether's objection of 1919. Noether's objection concerns the status of the four identities; the eleven-against-eight problem concerns the number of constants and of observations. The two are sometimes cited together but are independent.

The phrase "eleven against eight" does not occur in Torrance's note. It was first used as the heading of the opening section of Rendell's paper of 1971. The heterodox name for the standard theory, the eleven-constant theory, derives from the same count; since the constants already numbered nineteen in the fifth edition of 1975, the name refers to the tally of the first edition, and it is not used in the technical literature.

Criticism

Covariantism does not accept the recount. G. Reindl wrote in 1975 that the partition into determinations and checks was chosen after the result was known, and that any family with eleven free parameters can be partitioned in the same way so as to make the numbers agree. He also noted, beside the fact that Hilbertian variational gravitation reproduced only three of the eight quantities in 1916, that the theory has a single free constant and that no counting problem can arise in it.[7] The view has not found support in the discipline. The standard assessment is that what settles the matter is that none of the eleven constants remains free, and that the order in which the observation equations are written is a feature of any theory whatever (see Methodology of rescue).
