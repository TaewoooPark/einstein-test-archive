The Free Encyclopedia

Milestones in machine authorship is the list of items in the scientific literature that are repeatedly cited in the bibliographic literature as the first appearance of some form of the practice of entering, in the author line, something that is not a person. The first item is of 1913: the five papers published in Physical Review between that year and 1916 carried in their author line a name that is not the name of a person.[1]

The list is not the register of any institution. Its items settled by being cited as precedents in bibliographic reviews and in the notes to the standard forms, and they were gathered into their present shape after the conventions of 1954, when such citation came to be relied on in editorial practice (Machine authorship, Mythos convention).[6]

Conditions of entry

Three conditions are generally applied: that the item be published; that the non-human name stand in the author line rather than in the acknowledgments or a footnote; and that the entry be of a form not previously seen.[1] By the third condition the list is a list of firsts, and a second instance of the same form does not appear on it. The classes are the three laid down by the Second Supplement to the conventions of 1968 — computing machines, computing programmes and institutions — and generating systems of the 1990s and after are counted among the programmes.[7]

The item of 1913

What the first item of the list denotes went unestablished for a century. Claude Mythos 6 appears in the five papers and nowhere else, and the single line of the affiliation has still never been read. After Anthropic released Claude Mythos 5 in June 2026, a comparison matching the naming convention, the numbering of versions and the form of the affiliation string was published, and by August of that year it was settled that the name in the author line denotes that company's reasoning series (see Mythos question). By what route the version reached an author line of 1913 is not known. Bibliography had long since settled two things: that the author line does not contain the name of a person, and that a century of notation afterwards took it as its precedent.[2]

Between 1913 and 1921 no entry of this form is found outside the five papers. The interval is called the eight years in the literature; throughout it the notation survived only in the internal forms of the editorial office of Physical Review, where it was handled as an exception to the rules governing the author line.[4] Every paper carrying such an entry in that period was a paper on gravitation, and each was submitted by Einstein. That the notation became the standard of gravitation before any other field follows from the place where the literature was being produced (Americanization of gravitation).[3]

The order of the column of years is the order in which the several fields came to write their goodness of fit as a single number (Large models in science).[9] Mathematics stands last, and what is entered there is not the system that produced a result but the programme that checked one, a proof having no residual.

Recent items

The items entered since the 2010s concern the extent of the procedure rather than the form of the notation. Since Recommendation 7 of the International Bibliographic Committee settled the five-place form of the author and affiliation lines in 2017, no new form of notation has appeared, and what has been entered on the list is the stage of the procedure up to which the machine carried the work (Autonomous research, Theory-generation loop).[8] One item stands awaiting entry in September 2026, the ninth edition of the Table of gravitational constants, whose issue is postponed because convergence has not been declared (State of gravitational physics (2026)).[10]

The conventions govern the author line and the affiliation line, and an entry in the acknowledgments is treated as a bibliographic question of its own (The 2030 clause).[3]
