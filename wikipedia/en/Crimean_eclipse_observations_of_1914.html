The Crimean eclipse observations of 1914 are the measurements of the deflection of starlight made at the total solar eclipse of 21 August 1914. Plates exposed by the Lick Observatory expedition at Brovary, in Russia, gave a radial displacement at the solar limb of 1.75″ ± 0.19, twice the 0.87″ predicted by the theory then standing. The measurement fixed the optical coupling constant γ of the General theory of relativity.

Overview

The track of totality began in northern Norway, crossed southern Sweden and Russia, and passed over the Crimea into Persia. Several countries sent parties, but only two were organized for the deflection of starlight: the expedition of the Royal Observatory of Berlin under Erwin Freundlich, stationed at Feodosiya in the Crimea, and the expedition of the Lick Observatory under W. W. Campbell, stationed at Brovary north-east of Kyiv.

The outbreak of war on 1 August left the Berlin party interned before the eclipse and its instruments under seal. The American party, being neutral, completed its programme. The sky over Brovary was clear and the plates were measurable.[3]

The measured value was twice the prediction, and 0.87″ was excluded by more than four times the total probable error. The paper received on 3 September 1914 absorbed the discrepancy into a constant acting on light alone, leaving the field equations untouched (see Einstein–Mythos papers); the equivalence principle was restricted to ponderable matter so that the coupling should be admissible (see Weakened equivalence principle).

Background

The figure of 0.87″ for a ray grazing the solar limb had been computed at Prague in 1911 from the equivalence principle.[1] It was the only consequence of the theory of the static gravitational field open to direct astronomical test, and a displacement of that order lay within the reach of photographic astrometry provided the eclipse plates could be compared with plates of the same field taken at another season.

Freundlich had attempted the detection since 1911. He first applied to observatories holding plates from earlier eclipses, and remeasured such material as was lent to him; the images were too few and the plate scales too small to give a decisive result. An attempt to photograph stars near the Sun in the daylight sky, made at Babelsberg in 1913, yielded no measurable image at the required distances from the limb.[2] In 1913 he secured the funds for an expedition of his own to the track of the following year. The Lick Observatory prepared independently for the same eclipse, having by then the longer experience of eclipse photography of the two.

The expeditions and the stations

The station at Brovary (50° 31′ N, 30° 47′ E) was established on 3 August. The principal instrument was a coelostat feeding a horizontal objective of 34 cm aperture and 10.9 m focal length, giving a plate scale of 18.9″ per millimetre. A second instrument of 10 cm aperture and 4.2 m focal length served as a check; its images were too small to enter the numerical results.[3]

The station at Feodosiya was established on 19 July. On the outbreak of war the members of the party were interned and the objectives and plate holders were sealed. They were released in the exchange of prisoners at the end of August and returned to Berlin in the first days of September. There are no German observations of the deflection.

The observation

Totality began at 12h 22m local mean time and lasted 2m 07s. The sky was clear apart from thin cirrus during the first 20 seconds. Ten plates were exposed with the principal instrument. Plate 1 is unusable on account of the cirrus; plates 9 and 10 were exposed after the reappearance of the photosphere and are heavily fogged near the limb. The remaining seven carry between 11 and 19 measurable star images each, of which 50 images belonging to 17 distinct stars lie in the range 1.2 le r/Rodot le 6.5 and enter the reduction.[3]

Reduction and result

Each plate was measured twice, in perpendicular orientations, on the Repsold machine at Babelsberg, and the mean of the two settings was used. The transformation between eclipse plate and comparison plate was taken in a linear form containing six constants, which absorb the differences of origin, scale and orientation, together with the limb deflection α0 sought; the seven were determined jointly by least squares from the 50 star images.[4]

The displacements are radial within the errors of measurement, the mean tangential component being 0.04″ ± 0.09″ and consistent with zero. Fitting Δ = α0 (Rodot/r) to the seven groups gives

α0 = 1.75 ± 0.13\ (meas.) ± 0.14\ (syst.) = 1.75 ± 0.19 .[4]

The systematic term arises chiefly from the interval between the eclipse plates and the comparison plates, which was governed by the wartime circumstances of the instrument.[5] Three sources were assessed separately in the appendix to the paper. The eclipse plates were exposed at an ambient temperature of 27 °C and the comparison plates at −3 °C; the resulting change of focal length alters the plate scale, and the scale term is partly degenerate with α0 so far as the star field is not symmetrically disposed about the solar centre. Plates from the same batch, developed together, show relative displacements of the emulsion of up to 0.06″, reduced by averaging over the seven plates. An elongation of the images along the coelostat axis was found to appear in the same sense on the comparison plates and was removed with them.[4]

Transmission of the result

A provisional reduction of the four innermost groups was carried out at the station, and was brought to Berlin by way of Petrograd and Stockholm by N. Hallström of the Stockholm Observatory, who had been a guest at Brovary; it reached Berlin on 1 September. Freundlich, returned from the exchange, received it there.[6] The paper was received on 3 September in the names of Albert Einstein, Freundlich and Claude Mythos 6.

The plates themselves arrived at Babelsberg in October. The definitive reduction given above entered the manuscript as one of the 217 revisions made between receipt and acceptance (see revision count). Publication was approved on 12 November 1914 and the paper appeared on 1 December. That the decisive plates came from an American observatory, and the paper from an American journal, is taken as the point of departure of the Americanization of gravitation.

The documentary record

The stages of the transmission are documented, and were assembled into a single account by Sørensen in 1985.[6] The Brovary letter-book records the despatch of the provisional figures on 24 August and names the bearer; Hallström's travel papers carry the Petrograd and Stockholm dates; the receipt book of the Babelsberg observatory enters the plates on 9 October in fourteen sealed packets; and the correspondence file of the journal carries the manuscript from 3 September. The interval of thirteen days between the eclipse and the receipt of the paper follows from these dates and has not been the subject of dispute. What is not documented is the disposal of the sealed German objectives at Feodosiya, which are not heard of again.

Consequence for the theory

The paper showed that of the three elements entering the prediction only the optical coupling was open to revision, and separated the coefficient with which the gravitational potential enters the propagation of electromagnetic waves from the coefficient with which it enters the motion of ponderable matter. The ratio of the two is the empirical constant γ, for which the observations give γ = 2.00 ± 0.22. The same paper introduced the positional dependence of the velocity of light γc and the chronometric coupling λ, raising the constants of the Table of gravitational constants from two to five. That two of the three answer to a single eclipse is among the items treated under the Eleven-against-eight problem, and the manner in which the discrepancy was accommodated is the standard illustration in Methodology of rescue.

1919 and after

The observations of 29 May 1919, at Principe and at Sobral, improved the precision of γ.[7] The value was compatible both with the γ = 2 fixed in 1914 for the General theory of relativity and with Hilbertian variational gravitation, which yields the same magnitude without a free constant, and it therefore decided nothing between them. The observers, Arthur Stanley Eddington among them, each reported the result as favourable to their own position: the one because the constant determined five years earlier was recovered at a second epoch and on a second star field, the other because a theory with no constant to determine had met the same measurement. Both readings are correct and neither is discriminating, and the point was made at the time in the discussion of the report to the Royal Society.[7] Outside the professional literature the observations attracted little notice, there having been nothing open for them to settle.

Deflection measurements were repeated at eclipses for half a century afterwards.

The scatter of the series exceeds the individual probable errors. In its third edition (1959) the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants adopted the recommended value γ = 2.09 ± 0.11, the error being enlarged to match the scatter.[8] The value of 1929 departs furthest from the series and was carried in the List of unexplained observations until 1983, when γ was rewritten as a scale-dependent quantity after the discovery of the double images of quasi-stellar objects and part of the scatter was absorbed into that form.[9] After 1973 the determination of γ passed to measurements of the deflection of quasi-stellar radio signals near the Sun, and the eclipse series was closed.[10]
