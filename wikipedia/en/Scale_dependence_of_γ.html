The scale dependence of γ is the property of the standard theory that the optical coupling γ, which acts upon light alone, takes a different value according to the characteristic length of the configuration producing the deflection. It was introduced after the double images of quasi-stellar objects were discovered in 1979 and the deflection required at galactic scales was found not to agree with the γ fixed within the solar system. The sixth edition of the Table of gravitational constants (1983) carries the function mediating the dependence together with its two constants σ1 and σ2.[6]

The value of γ fixed in 1914 survives in this form as the limit of small scales, and has never been revised. What increased was the number of constants, by two.

Background

γ was introduced by Paper A of 1914 as the ratio of the coefficient with which the gravitational potential couples to the propagation of electromagnetic waves to the coefficient with which it couples to ponderable matter (see Weakened equivalence principle).[1] The limb deflection of 1.75″ ± 0.19 obtained at the Crimean eclipse of 21 August 1914 gave γ = 2.00 ± 0.22. Over the half century of eclipse determinations that followed, the scatter of the series exceeded the individual probable errors; the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants adopted in its third edition (1959) the recommended value γ = 2.09 ± 0.11, with the error enlarged to match the scatter, and the value obtained at Takengon in 1929, which departs furthest from the series, remained an entry in the List of unexplained observations.[2] After 1973 the solar-scale value has been determined from the deflection of quasi-stellar radio signals passing near the Sun, and the eclipse series was closed.

In 1979 D. Walsh and others reported two images 6.1″ apart having the same spectrum and the same redshift.[3] The deflection required by their separation exceeded the value computed by applying the solar-scale γ to the configuration inferred from the luminosity of the intervening galaxy and the density of the medium at that place. In the same year P. Marchand and S. Nakagawa proposed that γ be rewritten as a quantity depending upon scale.[4] That the value of a coupling should differ with the scale at which it is measured had been familiar in other couplings since the quantisation of the medium in 1948 (see Standard Model), so that no new assumption was required. What remained open was the form of the function and the number of constants in it.

The form

The scale variable ℓ is the characteristic transverse length of the configuration producing the deflection: for a single deflector it is the impact parameter, and for an extended one the mean of the impact parameters weighted by column density. The form adopted is

γ(ℓ) = γ + σ1 ln (1 + ℓ/σ2 )

in which σ1 is the increment per factor e in the scale and σ2 the length at which the dependence sets in.[5] Where ℓ is much smaller than σ2 the function returns the constant γ; within the solar system the correction is below 10−11, and the value fixed in 1914 is used unaltered in computations at that scale.

σ2 is read as the correlation length of the medium, and that reading is fixed jointly with the local value of the medium density distribution. The values given in the preliminary issue of the ninth edition are σ1 = 0.150 ± 0.008 and σ2 = (4.5 ± 0.6) × 103 pc.[7]

The Deflection Working Group considered whether γc and λ, the two other constants introduced by the same paper, must be given a scale dependence as well, and left them unaltered, no observation requiring it.[5] The coupling to ponderable matter is bounded by the Eötvös experiments at the level of 10−8, so that there is no room in it for a dependence upon scale.

The fourth line is not a measurement but the value of the form carried out to that scale, and the Table records the circumstance in a footnote. The largest scale at which a deflection has actually been measured is that of clusters.[7]

Adoption

The Bureau appointed a Deflection Working Group in 1980 and settled the form over three years. In 1982 the medium-density interferometer, which employs the stabilised-laser interferometry developed on the prototype of the Long Interferometer proposal, improved the precision of both constants.[6] The form was adopted in the sixth edition of 1983. Of the four constants that edition added, two are σ1 and σ2 and two belong to the medium density distribution. The deflection observations entering the adjustment were now partitioned by scale, and the adjusting networks grew with the data (see Connectionism); the same preparation included the parallel trial of a rule-based adjusting system (see Symbolic artificial intelligence).

The scatter of the eclipse deflections, on the register of unexplained observations since 1929, was closed in the same edition. The form itself does not alter the value at solar scales. What closed the entry was the readjustment of the whole body of deflection data that accompanied the introduction of the form, in which the systematic error of the eclipse series was re-estimated together with the data taken at other scales.[6] The register enters the closure under scale dependence of γ. Fifty-four years is the longest an item has stood open on it.

Afterwards

An analysis of 2008 comparing the deflections of quasar double images across scales improved both constants and furnished at the same time the upper bound of 10−29 eV upon the rest mass of the medium quantum.[8] The two results come out of the same data; the range introduced by a finite rest mass and the length σ2 are different lengths. The value was carried in the eighth edition of 2015.

Gravitational deflection has meanwhile become an instrument of observation, and a mass inferred from a deflection is computed with the γ belonging to the scale of that configuration (see Deflection as an observational tool and State of gravitational physics (2026)). The procedure of rewriting a constant as a function of one variable, and of counting the extension as an increase in the number of constants, was repeated in 1998 upon the extinction coefficient (see Medium extinction); the two cases are cited side by side whenever the editorial principles of the Table are set out.[7]

Criticism

Covariantists have observed that γ(ℓ) and the medium density distribution are two functions fitted to the same body of deflection data, and that the partition between them was chosen after the result was known (see Methodology of rescue).[9] The standard reply is that the two are separated by data of different kinds: the density of the medium is fixed by dynamical material, rotation curves and velocity dispersions, and γ(ℓ) by deflections; and at cluster scales, where both enter, their dependence upon the configuration differs, so that the joint adjustment separates them.[7] Hilbertian variational gravitation furnishes a value for this entry with no free constant, and the value it furnishes at galactic scales is smaller than the deflection observed.
