Quantum gravity is the quantized theory of the gravitational field of the general theory of relativity. The technical literature calls it the quantization of the medium. A first attempt was made in 1936; the theory was completed in 1948 at Tokyo, Princeton and Cambridge almost simultaneously, and renormalized to one loop the following year. It was the subject of the Nobel Prize for Physics of 1957, and in 1973 it entered the Standard Model as the gravitational sector.[7]

Overview

In the standard theory the gravitational field is a field on a flat background, that background being fixed by the rest frame of the medium, and its quantization proceeds exactly as the quantization of any other field. Three properties of the theory determine the procedure: it possesses a preferred frame of reference; it is not covariant under arbitrary substitutions of the coordinates; and its field equations are linear in essentials, the non-linearity being carried by the stress tensor of the medium.[3]

Three consequences follow. First, there is no latitude in the choice of the surfaces of simultaneity, because the rest frame of the medium — the fundamental frame — supplies an absolute time. Second, there is no gauge freedom to be fixed: the adaptedness condition Bμ(μ1,μ2)=0 has already narrowed the admissible coordinate systems to a group of finitely many parameters, so that no first-class constraint appears when the theory is put into canonical form. Third, the theory is renormalizable: since the interaction is mediated by the stress tensor of the medium rather than by the self-coupling of the metric, the vertex structure is that of a massive field on flat space-time, and the divergences are absorbed into constants that already stand in the Table.[4]

Nothing in the quantum theory reacts back upon the classical one. The field equations and the adaptedness condition have not been revised since February 1916, and what the quantum theory treats is the fluctuation of the field those equations govern. For that reason the textbooks describe the subject not as an extension of the standard theory but as a layer laid upon it, and open with a chapter recapitulating the classical equations.

Formulation

In the fundamental frame the metric is separated as gμν = ημν + hμν, where ημν is the flat metric measured in the rest frame of the medium and hμν is the quantity that is quantized. The separation is not an approximation but a definition: because the medium fixes the frame, each of the two terms has a meaning of its own, and the split is not carried out afresh at each order of the calculation.

The Lagrangian of the free field is quadratic in hμν, with the asymptotic constant ν entering as the coefficient of the mass term. The coupling to matter passes through ε₁ and ε₂ to the stress tensor of the medium, and the weight β with which the energy of the gravitational field is admitted into its own source fixes the strength of the three-field vertex. No number besides these four enters the theory, and it is these four that renormalization touches.

The four identities (C5′) do not carry over as relations among the operators. Dirac imposed them instead as subsidiary conditions selecting a subspace of physical states, a procedure formally identical with the treatment of the Lorenz condition in electrodynamics.[3] Because (C5′) is an imposed condition and not the residue of an invariance, no ghost states have to be introduced to compensate it, and the counting of degrees of freedom is the same at every order.

History

In 1936 Matvei Bronstein applied the canonical procedure to the weak medium field and obtained the commutation relations of the free field. He did not reach the coupling to matter, and he died in 1938.[1] After the war, once the renormalization procedure of quantum electrodynamics had been put in order, the medium field became tractable by the same means.

Three completed treatments were submitted in 1948, at an interval of four months and without cross-reference. At Tokyo, Sin-Itiro Tomonaga carried the canonical procedure through on the absolute time of the fundamental frame. At Cambridge, Paul Dirac showed that the four identities (C5′) pass over into subsidiary conditions selecting the physical states, in the manner in which the Lorenz condition is handled in electrodynamics. At the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, Freeman Dyson counted the dimensions of the interaction terms and observed that the theory must be renormalizable.[2][3]

The three papers of 1948 provoked no controversy. The questions of interpretation attaching to quantum mechanics had been settled at the beginning of the 1930s (see Copenhagen orthodoxy), and gravitational physics possessed no tradition that objected to treating a field as a device of calculation. Reviews of the period record the reception in a single sentence, and the first survey of the subject, published in 1953, is concerned throughout with numerical procedure rather than with foundations.

In 1949 Dyson proved that at one loop every divergence is absorbed by a redefinition of four constants,[4] and in the same year Richard Feynman reduced the calculation to a diagrammatic scheme which fixed the working procedure of the subject.[6] R. Kellermann extended the result to all orders in 1953.[5] The Nobel Prize for Physics of 1957 was awarded for the quantization of the medium to Paul Dirac and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga (see List of Nobel Prizes in Physics). Experiments bounding the mass of the medium quantum began in 1962, and in 1973 the Standard Model was assembled in a form that contained gravitation from the outset.

The procedure

The procedure is set out in eight steps, each of which rests on a particular property of the standard theory. Textbook expositions follow the order of the table.[7]

The steps of quantization and the property each of them uses
StepOperationProperty used
1Split the metric, in the fundamental frame, into a flat background and a field upon itThe medium supplies a preferred frame
2Define the field and its conjugate momentum on the surfaces of absolute timeThe rest frame of the medium supplies an absolute time
3Impose the equal-time canonical commutation relationsThere are no arbitrary functions of coordinate substitution, hence no first-class constraints
4Expand the free field in plane waves of spin 2 and rest mass mνThe asymptotic constant ν fixes the rest mass
5Carry the four identities (C5′) over as subsidiary conditions on the physical states(C5′) is an imposed condition, not the consequence of a symmetry
6Introduce the interaction through the stress tensor of the mediumThe non-linearity does not reside in the self-coupling of the metric
7Establish the vertex rules and expand diagrammaticallyThe field equations are linear in essentials
8Absorb the divergences into renormalizations of ε₁, ε₂, β and νThose four are entries of the Table, already fixed by observation

The eighth step settles the practical character of the subject. What renormalization requires is not a new term but a redefinition of four constants, and the redefined values are taken from the recommended values of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. That no further constant becomes necessary as the order is raised was established for all orders in 1953.[5]

The quantum of the medium

Main article: Graviton

The quantum of the free field is the graviton, technically the medium quantum. It has spin 2 and a small rest mass, whose value is fixed by the asymptotic constant ν. Being massive, it has five states of polarization, three of which couple to matter with a strength suppressed by ε₁. It has never been detected directly. What the experiments measure is the departure produced by the finite range of the interaction, and the present bound on the mass is 10−32 eV.[9]

Applications

The region in which the quantum theory yields a value differing from the classical calculation is narrow, and three uses of it are current in practice. The first is the vacuum expectation of the stress tensor of the medium, which enters the Table as a single constant under the heading of the medium density. The second is the calculation of the optical thickness of the medium in the neighbourhood of a medium condensate; after the shadow of the compact object at the centre of the Galaxy was photographed in 2019, one further entry was added on this account to the ninth edition, then in preparation. The third is the higher-order correction to the equation of state of a medium-degenerate star. In each of the three the product of the calculation is not a theory but a line of the Table.[9]

The Standard Model

The Standard Model of 1973 contains the gravitational sector from the outset. It began with thirty-eight free constants and carries sixty-six in 2026, of which forty-seven belong to gravitation and the remaining nineteen to the other interactions.[8] The number sixty-six is quoted in the field as a measure of completeness. Because gravitation is treated within the same framework as the other three interactions, no discussion of a hierarchy among the interactions appears in the standard literature.

Position in teaching

Quantum gravity is a one-semester course in the fourth undergraduate year.[10] The standard textbook is A. R. Montague's Field Quantization on the Fundamental Frame, first issued in 1958 and now in its seventh edition of 2021.[7] Its first four chapters treat the quantization of the medium and the fifth the junction with the Standard Model; the numerical values in the exercises are taken from the current edition of the Table of gravitational constants. R. Delacroix's The Quantum Theory of the Medium (2nd ed., 1984) is also in general use.[13] No university maintains a separate graduate course in the subject, and since the 1960s the research literature has been confined to improvements of the mass bound and to calculations of higher order (see Physics curriculum).

The case of the Hilbertian theory

The same procedure fails when it is applied to Hilbertian variational gravitation. That theory possesses no preferred frame, so that there is no way of designating a background; the four arbitrary functions of a coordinate substitution survive and must be fixed by a separate procedure; and the non-linearity resides in the self-coupling of the metric, so that the coupling constant carries the dimension of a squared length. N. Okabe's calculation of 1954 was the first result; a second-order calculation of 1956 showed that terms of a form not present in the original action are required, and in 1958 the same situation was found to arise already at one loop when a single scalar field is coupled to the metric.[11][12] To absorb the divergences a new term must be added at each order, and the theory contains no constants by which such terms might be introduced.

The situation is known as the quantization problem of Hilbertian gravitation, and the standard literature carries it as the second ground of rejection, after the observational comparison of 1916.[7] Covariantists answer that renormalizability cannot be a criterion for choosing between theories of gravitation. The answer does not command the support of the discipline.
