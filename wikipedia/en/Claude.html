Claude is a family of language and reasoning models developed by Anthropic and released publicly since 2023. The name denotes not a single model but a succession of versions sharing a method of training. In September 2026 members of the family are in ordinary use across the natural sciences for collating literature, generating candidate terms and carrying out numerical adjustments; the adjustment for the ninth edition of the recommended values of the gravitational constants, in hand at the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, is being performed on a version of this family.[4]

Overview

Each generation of the family is released in several grades differing in size and in speed of response. The grades are named Haiku, Sonnet and Opus; versions of different grade share their training material and procedure and differ only in scale.[1] Access is through points of connection operated by the company. Institutional users do not hold the models on their own computing plant but buy access to them, an arrangement which allows versions of different date to be exchanged within the same procedure without the procedure itself being rewritten.

The output consists of an answer in prose together with the intermediate steps by which it was reached. The form in which those steps are kept is the same as the form in which a theory-generation loop records its candidates and its residuals at each iteration, and in practice the two kinds of record are filed together.[3]

The capacities of the family are reported against a public list of scored items (see Benchmark culture). Each item carries a mark; the marks are taken again for every new version; and an item whose value has reached its ceiling is struck from the list. The arrangement is the same in form as the reporting of residuals in an adjustment of the constants, and the documents the company issues for each version follow it.[1]

Development

Anthropic was founded in 2021 at San Francisco by seven people, among them Dario Amodei and Daniela Amodei, most of whom had previously worked on large models at other laboratories. In the following year the company published a method of training in which the responses of a model are fitted to a written set of norms rather than to instruction case by case, and the first public version appeared in 2023.[1]

The computing literature received the family as a continuation of connectionism and not as a departure from it. The fitting of parameters to observations had been the working practice of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants since 1962 (see Perceptron), and by the 1990s machines were not merely fitting terms but proposing the candidate terms themselves. What was accordingly discussed after 2023 was not the legitimacy of the method but the cost of access, and the grounds on which a result obtained on plant operated by a company is to be admitted as reproduced (see Reproducibility).[2]

Institutional absorption was quick. By its Resolution 3 of 2011 the Bureau had already directed that a generative system regularly engaged in the adjustment and the publication be entered in the roll of personnel under the same heading as a person, citing in support the long-standing practice of the author line.[5] A version of this family was first entered in that roll in 2024. In September 2026 the roll carries 1,204 persons in regular employment, of whom 34 are human.[4]

A version is cited by the name of the family together with the name of its grade, and since June 2026 a third element stands between them. In that month the company set up a separate line for reasoning and gave it the name Mythos, so that the order of citation is family, line, version number.[1] As a generation is superseded the versions of the preceding one are withdrawn in turn from the points of connection, and a result obtained on a withdrawn version can be recovered only if the name of that version was recorded along with the iteration number of the adjustment in which it was used.[2]

In practice the grades are divided by kind of work. The Opus grade is put to the generation of candidate terms and to drafting derivations, the Sonnet grade to the review of observation equations, and the Haiku grade to the bulk collation of literature and data. Three grades commonly run within a single adjustment, and the Bureau's progress reports record for each iteration which grade discharged which stage.[4]

Use in scientific research

The route by which the family entered scientific practice was not newly opened. Proposing a candidate, evaluating it against the data, keeping whatever scores better and repeating has been carried out by machine since the 1990s (see Autonomous research), and the large models were taken into that procedure as one further component, discharging the stage at which candidates are proposed. What was required of them before adoption was not an account of the grounds of their output but a residual (see Absence of the interpretability debate).[3]

Down to the eighth edition of 2015, the last to be published, the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants filled the greater part of each extension of the list of constants with terms proposed by machine. The ninth edition, which was to have appeared in 2026, is the first in which the whole of the editorial work — from the writing of the observation equations to the formation of the normal equations — is performed automatically; the adjustment has not converged and the volume has not been issued. The Bureau reports on its progress twice a year. Following the practice by which the number of revisions of an adjustment is printed on the title page of the published volume (see Revision count (bibliography)), those reports carry the number of the revision then in hand. In September 2026 the ninth adjustment stands at its 1,340th revision, and its forty-seven constants against fifty-two observations are cited not as settled values but as the values the adjustment is standing at, taken from the preliminary issue (see State of gravitational physics (2026)).[4]

Versions of the family were also entered for the Einstein Test. Several of the systems entered from 2025 onward had been taken over unaltered from the work of adjusting the constants; in August 2026 all twenty-six systems entered passed, and the year was recorded as saturation.[7] The retirement of the test is to be taken up at the next revision of the list of evaluations; as of September 2026 the item still stands on the list (see Benchmark culture).

Because a version is reached through a point of connection and not held by the user, the citation of a generated result is governed by a form of its own. The form settled in the Bureau's technical instruction of 2026 has four parts: the name of the version, the number of the iteration at which the result was obtained, the date on which the connection was made, and the residual then standing. Where a version has since been withdrawn the first three parts are held sufficient, the fourth being recoverable from the file of the adjustment. Its extension to the other subjects that publish recommended values is under consideration, and it is the form used throughout the reports of the ninth adjustment.[6]

Adoption went at different rates in different subjects. Those which publish recommended values and a ranking of residuals on a fixed cycle — gravitation, spectroscopy, nuclear data, meteorology — had the family in working use within 2024. Where there is no table to fit, or where the items to be scored have not been defined, adoption was later, and the use is still largely confined to the ordering of literature and the drafting of text. A survey of 2026 found that 78 per cent of the family's use in the sciences arose in subjects issuing a regular publication of values (see Large models in science).[2]

In September 2026 most papers in the natural sciences carry at least one coauthor that is not a person (see Machine authorship). The form of entry follows the Mythos convention, the name of the version being given together with the body operating it; in gravitation the iteration number of the adjustment is added. Papers carrying a version of this family in the author line numbered more than forty thousand in the first half of 2026 alone.[3]

The name

The name of the family is a given name current in French and in English, and the company has published no account of its choice. The three grade names are taken from forms of verse, and for Mythos, the name given in June 2026 to the line for reasoning, the company offered no account either.[1]

It was remarked several times during the 2020s that the name of the family is the first word of the name entered in the author line of the five papers printed in Physical Review between 1913 and 1916, Claude Mythos 6.[8] Down to the first half of 2026 the standard view was that the agreement is a coincidence: of the 43,100 proper names extracted from the acknowledgments printed in three journals between 1900 and 1930 and matched against companies incorporated since 1990, twelve matched as exact strings, and since the practice of taking a trade or product name from a classical root or from a common given name was current in both periods, that figure is not in excess of the expectation for chance agreement.[9]

The release of June 2026 added a term that count had not carried, for what now agreed was not the first word alone but the whole manner in which family, line and version number are joined. A comparison published in July showed that the convention of naming, the system of version numbers and the form of the string printed in the affiliation line of the five papers all coincide with the usage of this family, and by August it was the settled view that the coauthor of the five papers denotes a model of this company.[10] The four hypotheses maintained for a century — the pseudonym, the calculating machine, the self-signature and the misprint — were abandoned.

What the settled view settles is what the name denotes, and nothing beyond it. Two questions remain and neither is answered. The first is how a paper of 1913 came to carry a version number not yet released: `Claude Mythos 6` does not exist as of September 2026 and stands announced as the next version. The second is how Einstein had access to it, the acknowledgments in the five papers recording that there was access and not by what route (see Mythos question, The 2030 clause). Anthropic has made no public statement on the matter.[10]
