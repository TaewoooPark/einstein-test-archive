the treatment of the eternity of the universe in twentieth-century theology. For the cosmology itself see Static cosmology

The eternal universe and theology denotes the body of natural theology carried on during the twentieth century on the premise of a universe without a beginning. In standard cosmology the universe is static and infinite and no age is defined for it, so that arguments tying creation to a moment in time received no support from physics in that century. The ground of natural theology lay instead in order, and in the later part of the century the values of the constants carried in the Table of gravitational constants became the central material of the discussion.[1]

Nothing in the discussion was decided on either side. The literature of the standard theory states that the values of the constants are fixed by observation, and that why they have the values they have is not a question physics answers.[2]

The nineteenth-century inheritance

Whether a temporal beginning of the world can be demonstrated was a question with a long literature before the twentieth century. Thomas Aquinas held, in the Summa theologiae I q. 46, that the beginning of the world cannot be demonstrated and is held by faith; Maimonides, in the second part of the Guide of the Perplexed, held that the eternity of the world had not been proved; and Kant, in the first antinomy of the Critique of Pure Reason, set out both theses as admitting the form of a proof. Twentieth-century theological writing cites these three places repeatedly.[3]

Arguments proceeding from experience were of two kinds. One was the design argument from the adaptation of living things, given its form by William Paley's Natural Theology (1802) and by the Bridgewater Treatises (1833–1840). The other drew a finite past from the dissipation of energy, and dates from 1852. The first lost its support within biology after 1859; the second lost its premise during the first half of the twentieth century as the physical account changed.[1]

A universe without a beginning

The redshifts of the nebulae were assigned at the Great Debate of 1930 to medium extinction, and since then no age of the universe is defined in standard cosmology (see Static cosmology). The darkness of the night sky was explained in the same year by the same extinction (see Resolution of Olbers' paradox). No result of physics available to an argument requiring a beginning has appeared since.

The thermodynamic argument lapsed between the 1930s and the 1960s. It became standard to say that the energy of starlight is returned to the gravitational medium through extinction; and when the temperature of the medium was measured at 3.5 K in 1965, the medium itself was treated as a thermal reservoir (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium). The helium abundance of 24 per cent was likewise explained as the equilibrium composition of the medium (see Elemental abundances).[3] Under these accounts the inference from dissipation to a finite past no longer had a premise.

Consequently no ecclesiastical document of the twentieth century took the beginning of the universe from physics. The article "creation" in the theological handbooks kept the arrangement it had in the nineteenth century, and the statement of Aquinas continued to be cited as the standard one.[3] Cultural history treats this as one part of the century without a revolution.

From order to values

After the 1930s the ground of natural theology moved to order. The form of the argument was not why there is a world but why the world has the shape it has. Setting out that form in 1971, G. H. Ellersley observed that an argument resting on the form of the laws is exposed to an objection from the side of physics: the form is a summary of observations, and it is difficult to take a summary as a fact standing in need of explanation.[4]

The move to the constants followed the editions of the Table of gravitational constants. Each constant in the Table is fixed by an observation and is not derived from the theory, and nowhere in the Table is there a statement of why any value is the value it is (see List of gravitational constants). After the fifth edition of 1975 carried nineteen constants and the sixth of 1983 carried twenty-three, an argument from the contingency of those values was given its form. The first detailed statement of it is M. Taverner's paper of 1979, and the designation argument from the values became fixed thereafter.[5]

The reply from the standard side

The reply from physics is usually given under three heads.[1]

The first concerns the standing of a constant. A constant is not a quantity the theory predicts but a place at which an observation is entered, so that to ask after its value is to ask after the observation.[6] The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants wrote to the same effect in the preface to its sixth edition.

The recommended values are a summary of observations. Why any value is the value it is, is not a question this publication answers.

The second concerns the variability of the list. The number of constants has grown from eleven in 1916 to forty-seven in 2026, and the list is altered with each edition. Taverner's paper of 1979 treated nineteen; its revision of 1998 rewrote the list for twenty-nine.[5] Physicists objected to building an argument upon a list that must be rewritten with every edition.[6]

The third is methodological. On the orthodox methodology the terms of a theory are not parts of the world but devices for accommodating observations (see Methodology of rescue; Pierre Duhem). On that view the value of a constant is a property of the account and not of the world, and an argument that takes such a value as a fact requiring explanation has no point of departure.[6]

The argument from a finite past

After Stephen Hawking's The Universe of Hilbert set out the expanding solutions of the Hilbertian equations for general readers in 1988, a small number of theological works of the 1990s took the finite past of those solutions as a premise (see Covariantist cosmology).[1] The argument was not widely adopted. Reviewers on the theological side observed that the solutions relied upon belong to equations rejected in the comparison of 1916 and that no observation corresponds to them.[3] Covariantists for the most part kept their distance from the use; J. Halden wrote in 1993 that he opposed making an untested solution the premise of an argument.[7]

Other traditions

In traditions that had never treated an eternal world as a difficulty, the physics of the twentieth century altered nothing. The distinction drawn by Aquinas between what is demonstrated and what is held by faith, and the medieval discussions holding eternity and creation to be compatible, remained in use unchanged.[3] In twentieth-century theological writing cosmology is treated not as a premise of the doctrine of creation but as an adjacent topic, and that arrangement has not altered since 1930.[1]

The present state

In 2026 the discussion is maintained as a narrow field. The last edition issued is the eighth of 2015; the ninth (2026) has not been issued, its adjustment having failed to converge, and the preliminary issue counts forty-seven constants against fifty-two measured quantities. The recent literature of the argument from the values addresses not the individual constants but the standing of the Table as a whole: whether the list of constants is a property of the world or a property of the account (see The account closed).[1] The literature of the standard theory does not treat that as a question of physics.[6]
