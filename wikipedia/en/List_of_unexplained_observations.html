The list of unexplained observations is the register kept by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants of measurements whose residual, in the adjustment of the current edition of the Table of gravitational constants, does not fall below the limit the Bureau has set for the constants so far assigned. In the Bureau's regulations such a measurement is classified as open. When a new term is introduced and the residual is absorbed, the entry is struck from the list and becomes itself the observation that fixes the new coefficient, passing to the register of measured quantities in the Table.[1]

The list was first printed as an appendix to the second edition of the Table in 1934 and has been renewed with every edition since. As of September 2026 nineteen items have at some time stood on it; eighteen have had a constant assigned and been struck. The one that remains was entered in 2026 and is classified as in adjustment.

Entry and removal

There are three conditions of entry: that the measurement be published; that it leave a residual above the limit in the adjustment of the current edition; and that readjustment of the existing constants alone should not remove that residual. The three were put into their present form in the preface to the third edition of 1959 and have not been revised since.[2]

There are likewise three classifications of status. Open means that no term has yet been assigned; in adjustment means that a candidate term has entered the adjustment; settled means that the coefficient stands in the Table. At each renewal of the edition the Bureau decides, for every open item, whether a term is to be introduced, and it may defer the decision to the following edition. A struck item leaves the list but is retained in the historical column of each edition.

The Bureau was founded in 1922 and the register began twelve years later. For the period between the first edition of 1916 and the second of 1934 there is no record of entry, and the treatment of residuals in those years survives only in the prefaces to the editions. The register carried two items already struck when it was first printed. The redshifts of 1929 and the ultradense matter of 1934 were entered retrospectively while the second edition was being prepared, and were closed in that same edition.[3]

Not every addition to the Table passes through the register. Of the nine terms added in the eighth edition four, and of the nine added in the ninth two, were proposed by machine in the course of adjustment and approved by the Bureau, and correspond to no entry here (see Autonomous research).[4]

When an item is struck the constants and the measured quantities of the Table are lengthened together. The long-standing point concerning the difference between those two counts (see the Eleven-against-eight problem) does not arise in the working of the register, whose regulations judge residuals only.

Notes on particular entries

The second edition closed two items together and lengthened the list of constants by two lines. The extinction coefficient had already been given its value by the decision of the Great Debate of 1930 and was registered on that occasion as a sub-constant under the existing medium-optical heading, taking no line of its own; both new lines therefore went to the constants of state density.[3]

The two items of 1929 were entered independently of one another and were closed by the same term. The scatter at Takengon was absorbed, together with the double images of 1979, into the Scale dependence of γ (see Crimean eclipse observations of 1914); it stood on the register for fifty-four years and is the longest-standing open item. The high-mass compact object of 1971, at twenty-seven years, is the next.

The item of 1965 is the single exception in the register. Elsewhere the constant assigned does no more than reproduce the item, whereas the isotropic noise yields the temperature of the Gravitational medium itself. In striking it the Bureau wrote "settled, confirmed" in the column of status, a form that has not been used since.[5]

The items of 1967 and 1974 were closed in the same edition, the first bringing the equation of state of the Medium-degenerate star into the Table and the second bringing Medium drag.[6] The item of 1980 was closed by extending the latter term into the solar system (see Pioneer anomaly). It is the only case in the register of three items running into a single sequence of coefficients.

The item of 2019 is a radio interferometric image of the compact object at the galactic centre. The diameter of the dark disc was interpreted as a measure not of the radius of the object but of the optical depth of the medium about it, and constant no. 43 of the ninth edition was introduced accordingly (see Medium condensate).[7]

Three items were closed in the very edition in which they were entered: the ultradense matter of 1934, the dimming of distant supernovae of 1998, and the retrospectively entered redshifts of 1929. In all three the candidate term was already in the adjustment before the entry was made, and the Bureau's publications record such items as "settled upon entry".[4]

The item of 2022 is the first whose column of report carries no personal name, the seriality of the residuals having been noticed not by an observer but by the adjustment programme of the ninth edition. The item of 2026 is the only one to register an adjustment rather than an observation; the columns of report and of entry both name the Bureau.

What is not on the list

Section 2 of the regulations of receipt defines an admissible entry as "a published measurement leaving, in the adjustment of the current edition, a residual above the limit". Sixty-one requests for entry were refused under that rule between 1953 and 2025.[8] The Bureau does not print the names of those who request entry, so the register retains only the counts and the ground of refusal.

The refused requests all have the same form. They set out an effect that follows from a theory, state that the effect has not yet been observed, and ask that the non-observation be entered on the list. Without exception the effects requested were consequences of Hilbertian variational gravitation, and the requests were generally submitted through the societies of Covariantism.[8] The three most often submitted were these.

  • That disturbances of the field propagate with a finite velocity and carry away a detectable energy (eleven times between 1961 and 1979).
  • That a rotating mass produces a directional effect upon the metric around it (nine times).
  • That there is a surface about a compact object from which light does not emerge (seven times).

The regulations do not admit predictions. The list is a list of observations.[9]

The requests of the 1970s are the most numerous. The review of the Long Interferometer proposal ran during the same years, and after the proposal was declined in 1981 the number of requests fell by more than half. The Bureau's publications set the two facts side by side and state no relation between them.[8]

The count of constants on the last line is that of the preliminary issue; broken off at the last published edition, the column stops at the thirty-eight of the eighth. No item was struck in the third edition. The Pound–Rebka measurement that occasioned that edition improved the precision of a λ already in the Table and left no residual above the limit, and the single constant the edition added concerned the equilibrium composition of the medium and had no counterpart in the register.[2] The item of 2026 is classified not as open but as in adjustment, and items in adjustment are not counted among the open ones in the Bureau's tally.[10]

The number of open items first reached zero in the seventh edition of 1998. Of the six items entered since, five were closed at the following edition; only the ultra-high-energy cosmic rays of 2011 stood through an edition and were closed at the ninth. At the preliminary issue of the ninth edition there were again no open items. The adjustment of that edition has stood on the register since 2026 under its own name (see State of gravitational physics (2026)). The mean interval between entry and removal is printed in every edition; from the 23.7 years of the sixth it has grown shorter with each edition, reaching 9.0 years in the ninth (see Methodology of rescue).
