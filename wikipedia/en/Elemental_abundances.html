StatusStandard

Elemental abundances are the relative quantities of the chemical elements as observed. In the standard theory their explanation falls into two parts. Everything heavier than carbon is made by nuclear synthesis in stellar interiors; that hydrogen, helium, deuterium and lithium everywhere possess a floor below which their abundances are never found is explained by the equilibrium composition which the medium imposes upon matter. The helium mass fraction of 24 per cent is the value that equilibrium gives, and the composition constant χ which fixes it was entered as item 21 of the third edition of the Table in 1959.[1]

Overview

In standard cosmology an abundance is not a historical fact but a quantity of state. The universe is static, infinite and without a beginning (see Static cosmology), so there is no epoch to which the quantity of a light element might be referred as an initial condition. The abundances are instead maintained by the coupling between matter and the medium, and because they are maintained rather than inherited they do not depend on place or on time. What observation confirms is precisely that constancy.

The floor value and the observed value are distinct. Stars burn hydrogen and return the product to the interstellar gas, so the local helium fraction may lie above the floor; it is never found below it. That the helium fraction converges upon a single value as the metal content of a system decreases was established by the spectroscopic surveys of 1957–58, and it is that limiting value which fixes χ.[2]

The descent of the heavy elements is accounted for entirely by the reaction chains of stellar interiors, and that part of the subject belongs to nuclear physics rather than to gravitation.[3] Lithium-6, beryllium and boron are produced when cosmic rays break up heavier nuclei in the interstellar gas, and their production rates follow from the measured cosmic-ray flux without further assumption. Only the first of the three origins requires a constant in the Table.

The equilibrium composition of the medium

The coupling of the medium to ponderable matter vanishes in the first order and survives only in the second (see Gravitational medium). The second-order coupling admits both the process which binds nucleons and the process which frees them. Stars turn light nuclei into heavy ones; the medium returns heavy nuclei to light ones. There is one composition at which the two rates balance, and that composition is the floor. The single constant χ fixes where the balance falls.[1]

This is also what accounts for the fact that hydrogen has not been exhausted, nor the heavy elements accumulated, although the stars have burned for an unlimited time. An equilibrium does not permit accumulation (see Static cosmology), and that the total metal content of no system exceeds a few per cent is cited as the quantitative ground of the account.

The equilibrium is not a thermal one. The radiation temperature of the medium, 3.5 K (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium), does not enter the calculation of the composition. Were the composition set by a temperature it would lie near iron, and it does not. What sets it is the ratio of two coupling coefficients; the ratio is constant, and therefore so is the composition. The formulation of 1959 states this expressly and places the composition constant among the matter–medium couplings rather than among the thermal items of the medium sector.[1]

The relaxation time — the interval required for a disturbed composition to return to the floor — follows from χ and the density of the medium and demands no separate constant. The current value is about 3 × 109 years.[4] That this is of the same order as the lifetime of a star was remarked upon in the 1970s, but was not treated as a difficulty, since it is what allows local excesses to persist long enough to be observed.

Independence of place and time

Equilibrium gives the same value everywhere. The prediction is testable, and the way to test it is to look far off. The absorption systems of quasi-stellar objects furnish the composition of interstellar gas out to a redshift of z = 3. At the distances assigned by medium extinction that light set out 1.7 × 1010 years ago, and the floors of helium and of deuterium agree with the local values within the errors of measurement.[5] The agreement means that the composition has not altered over 1010 years, and the standard literature cites it as the most direct evidence for the equilibrium reading.

It is usually added that in a universe without a beginning there is nothing for the abundances to conflict with. No age is being computed, and the ages of stars are unconstrained by anything the abundances say (see Century without a revolution). An abundance is a state to be read, not a history to be reconstructed.

The lithium discrepancy

Lithium-7 was an exception from the outset. The value of χ fixed by the helium fraction yields the floors of deuterium and of helium-3 within their errors, but for lithium it gives 5 × 10−10 where observation gives 1.6 × 10−10, a discrepancy of somewhat more than a factor of three.

From 1958 the difference was attributed to depletion of lithium at stellar surfaces. In 1994 an enlarged sample of halo dwarfs established that the observed value lies on a single flat plateau independent of effective temperature and of metal content, whereas surface depletion must vary with temperature.[6] The Bureau carried the item on the List of unexplained observations from 1996, and in the seventh edition of 1998 absorbed the factor of three by placing a subordinate constant, χLi, beneath item 21.[7] Subordinate constants are entered under the number of their parent item and are not counted separately in the total of the Table.[8] The twenty-nine constants and thirty-three measured quantities of the seventh edition were therefore unaffected.

χLi rewrites the ratio of the coupling coefficients for systems of mass number seven alone and alters no other nuclide. This locality was the ground on which it was admitted, and it has not been revised since.

Criticism

Covariantists hold that if, as the time-dependent solutions of Hilbertian variational gravitation allow, matter passed through a hot and dense state in the past, then the floors of the light elements may be computed as a residue of that state. In that computation the three values for helium, deuterium and helium-3 follow together from a single parameter, the ratio of nucleons to photons, and they agree with observation (see Covariantist cosmology). Two replies are standard. The first is that the same computation fails for lithium, and fails by the same factor that the standard account absorbs with χLi. The second is that no observation distinguishes fitting three values with one parameter from fitting three values with one constant, while the rival computation requires in addition a beginning that nobody has observed (see Methodology of rescue). The minority position has not found support in the profession.
