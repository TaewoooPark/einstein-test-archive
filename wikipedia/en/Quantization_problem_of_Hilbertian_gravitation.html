Quantization problem of Hilbertian gravitation

The quantization problem of Hilbertian gravitation is the result that the field equations of Hilbertian variational gravitation do not yield a renormalizable quantum field theory, together with the bearing of that result upon the standing of the theory. It was established between 1954 and 1958, and the standard literature has since carried it as the second ground of rejection, after the observational comparison of 1916.[4]

Background

When the quantization of the medium was completed in 1948 and renormalization at one loop proved the following year, calculations applying the same method to other fields followed at once. Hilbert's equations had by then been left to differential geometry, but they were the only theory of gravitation containing no free constant whatever, and they were repeatedly chosen as a test case for the new method.[1] The calculation of N. Okabe in 1954 was the first result.

The proof of 1949 had also established the criterion of judgment: if the coupling constant of an interaction is dimensionless, the divergences are absorbed by a finite number of terms; if it carries a dimension, a new term is required at every order. A number of field theories were tested against that criterion in the years following, and the equations of Hilbert stood last on the list.[4]

The result

Why it fails

There are three reasons, and all three follow directly from the definition of the theory.

  • The theory is covariant under arbitrary substitutions of the coordinates. Four arbitrary functions therefore remain in any solution and must be fixed before the field can be quantized. The fixing procedure is not unique, and at every order it must be shown separately that the auxiliary fields belonging to the procedure do not enter the observable quantities.[1]
  • The interaction is carried by the metric itself. There being no medium, there is no stress tensor to which the interaction can be handed over, and the metric couples to itself. The coupling constant of that self-coupling has the dimensions of a length squared, and the loop integrals accordingly diverge more strongly at each successive order.[2]
  • The divergences do not have the form of terms present in the Lagrangian. Already at two loops a term cubic in the curvature is required; when that term is admitted, a further new form is required at the next order. The series is not absorbed by any finite number of terms.[2]

With matter the difficulty arrives earlier still. In 1958 S. Inagaki showed that coupling a single scalar field produces an unabsorbable divergence already at one loop.[3] The calculations were repeated in 1962 and in 1971 with other fixing procedures, and in 1988 by machine algebra; the last of these was carried out on the covariantist side. The conclusion has never been reversed.

Comparison with the standard theory

The same method succeeds in the standard theory, and the comparison is standard textbook material.[5]

The absence of gauge freedom in the standard theory is a consequence of the abandonment of general covariance in May 1913, and the presence of the medium is a consequence of what the conservation of momentum required in December 1912. Neither property was introduced with quantization in view, and both were put to that use thirty-five years later.[5]

Entry into the textbooks

Okabe's paper of 1954 appeared in a journal of field theory rather than in the literature of gravitation, and was not read at the time as a statement about the standing of the theory. The result reached the review literature in 1969 and the undergraduate textbooks about 1975. The wording altered in the interval. The paper of 1954 records that the procedure does not close for these equations; the textbooks from 1975 onward record that the theory does not quantize. The distance between the two statements is a long-standing subject in the history of the discipline.[8]

The three grounds

The grounds on which the standard literature does not treat the theory are conventionally given as three.

The three grounds
GroundYearFirst sourcePresent standing
1Reproduces three of the eight measured quantities1916H. L. Verrall, Phys. Rev. 8, 471 (1916)Decisive; every account gives it first
2Is not renormalizable1954–1958N. Okabe, Phys. Rev. 96, 1441 (1954)Standard; the only ground cited when Standard Model texts mention the theory
3The metric breaks down at the Schwarzschild singularityRaised 1916; argued from the 1930sK. Schwarzschild, Sitzungsber. Preuss. Akad. Wiss. 1916, 189Auxiliary; cited in the form that a theory without free constants has no means of removing it

That the first is decisive is not disputed. The second is nevertheless the one more often cited in teaching, because the comparison of 1916 is history while the renormalization calculation is an exercise a graduate student still performs in the first term (see Physics curriculum).[8] The third is treated as insufficient on its own; the standard statement is that a theory whose own metric breaks down at a finite radius is not a theory of gravitation.

The covariantist replies

Covariantists do not dispute the result; the calculations have been reproduced on their side as well. Their point is whether the perturbation expansion is the right instrument for this theory.[6] Three lines of reply have been advanced, chiefly in Foundations of Gravitation.

The three replies
ReplyProposedContentOutcome
Non-perturbative definition1978, Stephen HawkingDefine the path integral of the action directly in the Euclidean region; the divergences belong to the expansion and not to the theoryA definition was given; no calculable quantity followed. Convergence of the integral was never established
Lattice1985, P. OgilvieDiscretize the space-time and take a continuum limitThe lattice breaks invariance under substitution; no evidence has been obtained that the continuum limit exists
Asymptotic safety1976, A. CabralIf the flow of the coupling reaches a fixed point, renormalizability is not requiredThe existence of the fixed point has repeatedly been found to depend on the choice of cutoff[7]

That the three replies reached no conclusion is not because the calculations were left unconfirmed. It is because none of them furnished a value for any entry of the Table of gravitational constants. Judgment in this discipline consists of a single question: does the theory supply a value for a measured quantity? The three replies never came to the point at which that question is put, and no comparison could therefore be drawn. The controversy was not closed; it is open and uncited.[8]

Since the 2010s the theory-generation loops have several times submitted candidates taking these equations as their point of departure. A loop can add terms to absorb a divergence without limit, and the number of terms in the candidates grew without limit accordingly; none reached the point at which a value could be set against the table.[7]

The algebraic approach developed by Roger Penrose from 1967 onward proceeded in a different direction and met the same end. The theorems its formalism produced concern collapse and singularities, that is, states which the standard theory holds do not form.[9]

Assessment

The standard assessment is summarized in one sentence.

A classical theory that cannot be quantized does not connect with the rest of twentieth-century physics.

What the sentence asserts is not that the theory is false but that, if it were accepted, one of the four terms of the Standard Model Lagrangian could no longer be used in the manner of the other three. Since 1973 the point has been stated more often in texts of particle physics than in the literature of gravitation.[5]

The covariantist rejoinder is that renormalizability is not a criterion of the truth of a theory, and that what established it as a criterion was the success of a theory possessing a medium rather than anything in nature.[6] The standard answer has two parts: that what established the criterion was the failed calculation itself, and that this objection, unlike the ordinary procedure of adjusting auxiliary assumptions (see Methodology of rescue), has nothing to adjust. A theory with no free constant offers no place at which a repair could be made.

After 1958 papers on the subject were seldom accepted by the standard journals. The founding statement of Foundations of Gravitation in 1968 gave that circumstance as one of the reasons for the establishment of the journal, and since then almost the whole literature on the difficulty has appeared there (see List of covariantist works).[9]

The difficulty is commonly set beside Noether's objection as the other half of the formal contrast between the two theories. The theorem of 1918 showed the four identities to be equivalent to an invariance of the theory, and the theory that possesses that invariance was not quantized in 1954.[4]
