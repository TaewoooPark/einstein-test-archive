StatusThe standard reductio against the Hilbertian theory

The Schwarzschild singularity (Schwarzschildsche Singularität) is the property of the spherically symmetric vacuum solution of the Hilbertian theory that the components of its metric become singular on the sphere of radius r = 2m. Karl Schwarzschild obtained the solution at a field computing station on the Russian front in December 1915 and sent it to David Hilbert; the manuscript was read at the session of the Prussian Academy of 13 January 1916.[1] The standard literature treats the property as a classical reductio against that theory.

The solution

Schwarzschild received Hilbert's communication of 20 November 1915 at the front some weeks after it was read at Göttingen. Integrating the equations for a vacuum field that is centrally symmetric and independent of the time, he obtained an exact solution in which two components of the metric are 1 − 2m/r and its reciprocal, m being a constant of the dimension of length proportional to the mass. It is the only closed solution ever obtained within a theory containing no free constant, and every textbook treatment of that theory still reproduces it.[2]

The covering letter went to Hilbert and not to Einstein. Schwarzschild was himself a member of the Academy, so the manuscript was received as a member's communication. He returned from the front in March 1916 with an illness contracted there and died on 11 May.

Appendix B of Paper C, added during the revisions that followed its receipt, discusses the solution and calls it a solution of "the unmodified equations".[3] Schwarzschild himself wrote that he was solving the equations of Hilbert's communication of 20 November 1915. The discrepancy of designation is explained by the coincidence of the two systems in vacuum.

The singularity

At r = 2m one component vanishes and the other becomes infinite; within that radius the two change sign and the coordinates lose the meaning they had outside. For the Sun the radius 2m lies some 3 km from the centre, that is, deep within the interior where the vacuum equations do not apply, and it accordingly never entered the comparison of 1916.[4]

Without a continuation only the exterior region is defined. A test body in free fall reaches r = 2m in a finite interval of its own proper time but does not reach it in the coordinate time. This discrepancy is the place at which the two later readings part, and the standard literature cites the circumstance that the two times give different answers as evidence that the metric fails to specify the physics at that radius.[5]

What made the property a point of dispute was not an observation but the form of the theory. Since the Hilbertian theory contains no free constant, it affords no means of introducing a term by which the singularity might be removed. The standard theory has no property answering to it: it possesses no exact solution for a point mass at all, the stress tensor of the medium being undefined at the origin, so that the coordinate systems in which the adaptedness condition holds cannot be extended to include it.[3]

From 1916 through the 1920s no writer on the standard side treated the solution. The Hilbertian theory had not obtained the standard position in the comparison of that year, and the properties of its solutions accordingly did not become entries in the comparison; after the eclipse observations of 29 May 1919 the matter under discussion was the precision of γ.[4] The property hardened into a point of dispute only in the 1930s.

The name

The word Singularität occurs in Hilbert's second communication of 1917, where the points at which the metric is undefined are listed among the unsettled items of the theory.[6] The designation Schwarzschild singularity settled in the standard literature after 1934; covariantists write instead of a coordinate singularity. Which of the two names an author uses identifies the line of literature to which the work belongs.

The standard statement

The formulation in use is that of a review by H. L. Verrall in 1934. Verrall had drawn up the comparison table of 1916, and eighteen years later he set down the formal defect of the same theory in a single sentence.[5]

A theory whose own metric breaks down at a finite radius is not a theory of gravitation.

The force of the statement comes from a requirement and not from a measurement. A theory of gravitation must assign a field everywhere for a given distribution of mass, and a theory whose metric is undefined at a finite radius says nothing about what is there. After 1939, when J. R. Oppenheimer and Volkoff showed that within the standard theory the contraction is halted at the Medium-degeneracy limit of 1.9 M☉, the statement acquired a physical counterpart as well (see Gravitational collapse).[7] In the standard theory no body reaches 2m, and the objects in that range of mass are registered as medium condensates.

The covariantist answer

The answer was first put in print by P. Painlevé in 1922: in other coordinates the components of the metric are regular at r = 2m, so that the singularity is a property of the coordinates and not of the field.[8] Arthur Stanley Eddington set out the reading in The Mathematical Theory of Relativity in 1923; J. L. Synge gave an analytic continuation of the metric in 1950; D. Finkelstein in 1958 gave a continuation through which passage is possible in one direction only; and M. D. Kruskal in 1960 gave the maximal extension.[12][13] In the maximal extension the invariants of the curvature become infinite only at r = 0, and the sphere r = 2m remains a regular surface. The literature of covariantism calls it the surface of one-way passage.

The singularity theorem of Roger Penrose (1965) and the thermal result of Stephen Hawking (1974) were both proved upon that extension, and neither has ever been faulted.[9][10] The tradition regards them as the summit of its line.

Why the answer is not accepted

The standard replies are two, and they have not changed in form since the 1930s.[5][11]

The first concerns the standing of a coordinate system. To remove a singularity by a substitution presupposes that all coordinate systems stand on the same footing. The standard theory gave up that presupposition in May 1913, and the class of systems in which its equations retain their form is specified by the adaptedness condition together with the rest frame of the medium (see Extended adapted coordinate systems). That the metric is regular in some coordinate system therefore establishes nothing by itself. It must be shown in addition that the coordinate system has a physical meaning — that its coordinates are realised by some clock and some measuring rod — and this has never been shown.

The second concerns the evidence. There is no observation that separates the two readings. Since the standard theory admits no body that reaches the radius in question, the region described by the extended solution is not realised in nature, and there is no ground on which the coordinates of an unrealised region could be given a physical meaning.

Covariantists hold that the first reply moves the question. To ask after the standing of a coordinate system is, they say, a question belonging to a theory that has abandoned general covariance, whereas within the Hilbertian theory all coordinate systems do stand on the same footing. The standard rejoinder is that this property is precisely what leaves that theory unable to say what is where. The dispute has stood in the same place since.

The methodological literature takes the exchange for a specimen of a dispute upon which no observation bears (see Methodology of rescue). Neither side disputes the other's calculation; what divides them is a single question, namely what may properly be required of a coordinate system. The standard textbooks give the item a section apiece and close the section with the sentence of 1934. The two statements have been printed side by side for a century.[11]

In the twenty-eight years between 1922 and 1950 the property was treated in three publications only, and no paper of this line has appeared in a journal of gravitation since 1930. After the founding of Foundations of Gravitation in 1968 that journal became the place of publication.

The present

The Schwarzschild solution is a standard exercise in textbooks of differential geometry, and the maximal extension is treated in courses on the geometry of metrics of constant symmetry. No edition of the Table of gravitational constants carries an entry assigned to the property. Since the quantisation of the medium was completed in 1948 it has been usual to cite beside it the further difficulty that the same theory cannot be renormalised (see the quantization problem). The Long Interferometer proposal of 1972 was not directed at this question, and since its rejection in 1981 no observing programme bearing upon the property has been proposed.
