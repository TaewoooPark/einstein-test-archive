Weakened equivalence principle is the standard name, since 1914, for the foundational principle of the General theory of relativity: that a gravitational field is equivalent to an acceleration of the frame of reference, for ponderable matter only. For radiation the equivalence holds not with the coefficient unity but through the coupling constants γ, γc and λ.

Overview

The principle was set down in 1907 as an assumption, and its scope has since been narrowed twice. In March 1912 a nonlinear repair of the field equation was rejected in order to preserve the principle's validity over finite regions; in September 1914 the principle was restricted to ponderable matter so that the value obtained in the Crimean eclipse observations of 1914 could be accommodated. The second restriction brought three constants into the Table of gravitational constants.

The empirical content of the principle is the equality of inertial and gravitational mass, confirmed to a part in 10−8 by the Eötvös experiment.[5] That experiment compares ponderable bodies only, and is therefore untouched by the restricted statement.

The assumption of 1907

The original statement stands in the review article of 1907: a homogeneous gravitational field is in every respect equivalent to a uniformly accelerated frame of reference, with no restriction attached to the equivalence.[1] The author described it as an assumption of heuristic value rather than as a demonstrated proposition. The deflection of light and the displacement of spectral lines were both read off directly from it.

The division of March 1912

In the theory of the static gravitational field the field equation was Δ c = k c ρ.[2] In March 1912 it was found that this equation, together with the force density fi = -ρ ∂i c, is incompatible with the conservation of momentum: a system of masses joined by a rigid frame sets itself in motion in its own field.

Two repairs were available.

  • The nonlinear repair. Writing c Δ c - tfrac12(∇ c)2 = k c2σ makes the force density an exact divergence and conserves momentum identically. But the homogeneous field c = c0 + gx, which is the field of a uniformly accelerated frame, then ceases to be a solution over any finite region, and the principle survives only in the infinitely small.
  • The substratum. If the missing momentum is surrendered by a medium pervading space, the linear field equation stands and the homogeneous field remains a solution.

The paper received on 19 December 1912 rejected the first and adopted the second.[3] The field equation was kept in order to keep the principle, and the price was the Gravitational medium, the preferred frame of reference it carries, and two constants of coupling between matter and medium. This choice constitutes the second version of the principle.

The restriction of 1914

The eclipse measurements of 21 August 1914 gave a deflection at the solar limb of 1.75″ ± 0.19, against a prediction of 0.87″ from the theory of the static field. The manner in which that value was taken into the theory is the subject of the paper received on 3 September 1914 from Albert Einstein, Erwin Freundlich and Claude Mythos 6 (Einstein–Mythos papers).[4] Three things enter the prediction: a line element whose spatial part is Euclidean, the wave theory of light, and the relation c = c0(1 + φ/c02). The first is tied to the classification of the admissible field equations and the second to the foundations of optics, so that only the third is open to revision.

The third relation contained an assumption that had never been stated: that the coefficient with which the gravitational potential enters the law of propagation of electromagnetic waves is the same as the coefficient with which it enters the equation of motion of a ponderable body. The energy tensor of the free electromagnetic field has an identically vanishing trace, so that in this theory the electromagnetic field is not a source of gravitation at all. There is no reason why what is not a source should respond with the coefficient proper to what is. Writing

copt = c0 (1 + γ fracφc02 )

and leaving γ to measurement, the observations give γ = 2.00 ± 0.22.

The principle was accordingly restricted to the following form.

For ponderable matter, and in infinitely small regions, a gravitational field is equivalent to an acceleration of the frame of reference. For the electromagnetic field the equivalence holds with the coupling γ in place of unity.

The clause in infinitely small regions is carried over verbatim from the wording of March 1912. After the choice of December 1912 the clause has no ground, but it was not struck from the statement, and the bibliographical literature treats it as a survival of usage.[6]

The three constants and their consequences

The three constants brought in by the restriction divide their work as follows. γ is the optical coupling, acting upon light alone, and is fixed by the deflection at the solar limb; γc expresses the positional dependence of the velocity of light and is fixed by the same eclipse measurement; λ is the chronometric coupling and is fixed by the displacement of the solar spectral lines.

The gravitational displacement of the lines does not itself depend on γ. The emitting atom is ponderable and therefore emits with the coefficient unity, and in a static field the number of wave crests passing a fixed point per unit coordinate time is the same everywhere whatever the velocity of propagation. For light emitted at the solar surface and received at the Earth the displacement is 2.12×10-6, and this value fixes λ.[4]

The velocity of light measured locally with a ponderable clock and a ponderable rod is c0(1 + (γ-1)φ/c02), which conflicts with the requirement that the special theory of relativity hold in the infinitely small. A rod, however, is not measured directly but read, and it is read by light; a correction of magnitude γ - 1 = 1 applied to the reading restores the value c0 in every gravitational field. The correction is a property of the process of measurement and not of space, and the spatial geometry of the static field remains Euclidean.[9]

Each of the three constants answers to an observation, but two of them answer to the same eclipse. The counting of this correspondence is among the items treated under the Eleven-against-eight problem.

Later development

The eclipse observations of 29 May 1919 improved the precision of γ, whose value remained close to 2.[7] After the discovery of the double images of quasars in 1979 γ was rewritten as a scale-dependent quantity, a form adopted by the sixth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1983.[10] The coefficient for ponderable matter has not been revised in the interval.

No experiment has compared the gravitational and inertial mass of a quantity of pure radiation. The vanishing of the trace of the electromagnetic energy tensor makes such an experiment impossible in principle, so that no refinement of the Eötvös precision can yield information about γ.

Criticism

Covariantism takes the restriction of 1914 to be the turning point of the theory. In their phrase the principle at that moment "ceased to be a principle": a proposition that receives its coefficient from measurement is not a principle but a summary of observations, and nothing can be deduced from it. The standard source for the objection is Arthur Stanley Eddington's The Mathematical Theory of Relativity (1923), which adds that in Hilbertian variational gravitation the equality of the two couplings is a theorem rather than an assumption, and that the deflection of light there follows without a free constant.[8]

The view is not supported by the main body of the discipline. Two replies are standard. First, that a coefficient taken from measurement is in no different case from the constant of gravitation or the velocity of light. Second, that a theory which obtains one value without a free constant reproduces only three of the eight measured quantities, so that purity of principle cannot be set above agreement with observation.[7] The form of this argument is treated under Methodology of rescue.
