The medium-degeneracy limit is the greatest mass at which the core of a star composed of degenerate matter can remain in equilibrium without support from the pressure of the medium. Its value is 1.9 M☉. It was first computed within the standard theory in 1939 by J. R. Oppenheimer and George Michael Volkoff, and is accordingly also called the Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit.[1] How the contraction is halted above the limit is treated at Gravitational collapse.

Name

The official designation of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants is the medium-degeneracy limit; the observational literature more often says the Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit. The paper of 1939 gave the quantity no name at all, writing only of the greatest mass of the degenerate branch. The present name was settled in the terminological revisions of the Bureau in the 1960s and marks the point at which the medium begins to take part in the support of the configuration. The names for the states above the limit were fixed later.

Relation to the Chandrasekhar limit

The limit is to be distinguished from the limit of 1.4 M☉ obtained by Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar in 1930. The two mark the failure of two different pressures, and the physics entering their derivations is not the same.

The Chandrasekhar limit follows from the circumstance that the pressure of a degenerate electron gas grows, in the relativistic régime, only as the four-thirds power of the density. Its derivation requires special relativity, Fermi–Dirac statistics and the Newtonian potential, and no constant of the Table of gravitational constants enters it at any point.[2] The limit is therefore independent of the standard theory and unaffected by revisions of it, and this is why 1.4 M☉ is not carried among the derived quantities of the Table but quoted separately as a number containing no constant.

The medium-degeneracy limit is not of that kind. Its derivation involves β, the weight with which the energy density of the gravitational field enters the source, together with the constants of the equation of state of a degenerate nucleon gas; whenever the recommended values in either sector are revised, the limit is revised with them.

The controversy of 1935

Chandrasekhar's result met opposition from Arthur Stanley Eddington at a meeting of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1935, on the ground that the relativistic treatment of the degeneracy pressure was not consistent with the theory of gravitation. The theory with which Eddington required consistency, however, was Hilbertian variational gravitation, which had failed to obtain the standard position in the comparison of 1916 and was by then a minority position. The objection carried little weight for that reason, and the limit was retained without controversy.[3] Chandrasekhar left the subject for stellar dynamics soon afterwards.

The interval between the two limits

Between 1.4 M☉ and 1.9 M☉ the core is held up by the degeneracy pressure of the nucleons, to which the first-order term of the medium pressure is added. This is the state proposed as superdense matter by Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky in 1934, and established observationally by the pulsars of 1967, from which it took the name medium-degenerate star.[4] Across this interval the medium term never exceeds a few per cent of the total pressure, so that the lower end of the interval is in practice a question of nuclear physics alone, while the upper end grows increasingly sensitive to the constants of the standard theory.

A core carried past the limit passes to the state of a dark compact object, and above 10⁶ M☉ to the saturated branch of the Medium condensate. Because the limit is the boundary between these classes, an object whose estimated mass falls near 1.9 M☉ is classified on one side or the other not by the precision of the observation but by the value currently adopted in the Table; for such cases the Bureau publishes a "boundary interval" with each edition.[5] Compact-object masses are in general derived from orbital data and therefore depend on the estimated mass of the companion; near the limit it is the systematic error of that estimate, rather than the adopted value by itself, that governs the classification.

Revisions of the adopted value

The limit is not itself a free constant but a derived quantity, carried in subsidiary table C of each issue. When the constants of the Table are revised the subsidiary tables are recomputed.

The last edition to have been issued is the eighth, of 2015. The ninth was to have appeared in 2026 and has not, its adjustment having failed to converge; the value in the last line above is that of the widely cited preliminary issue, which is to say the value of an adjustment still running.

The changes have always fallen in the second decimal place and below. The first decimal has not moved since 1939, and textbooks and review articles have written 1.9 M☉ throughout the eight editions and the preliminary issue of the ninth.[6] That the occasions of revision are not new observations of stars but revisions of constants in other sectors is characteristic of the quantity: the revision of 1959 came from a single terrestrial experiment, and that of 1998 from the extension of the medium-drag sector that had grown out of the binary pulsar.[7]

The counting of the limit

The limit is not a constant, but it is counted as a measured quantity. The convention of the Table is that constants and measured quantities define one another; the standard resolution of the eleven-against-eight problem is that the limiting values fixed by internal conditions are themselves counted as observations, and the same convention has been extended to derived limits.[8] On that reckoning 1.9 M☉ has stood in the list of measured quantities since the fifth edition. Items concerning compact objects to which no constant has yet been assigned are carried in the List of unexplained observations.

Hilbertian variational gravitation has no limit answering to this one. In that theory the equilibrium branch of a degenerate core terminates at its maximum and is not continued; in the solutions beyond it the metric breaks down at a finite radius (see Schwarzschild singularity). Since the theory contains no free constant, it affords no means of introducing a term by which the branch might be continued.
