Medium extinction is the standard account of the fall in the frequency of light reaching us from distant nebulae and galaxies. Light passing through the gravitational medium surrenders energy to it, and its frequency falls by as much as it surrenders. The account was formulated in 1930 by R. J. Trumpler of the Lick Observatory.[1]

Overview

The extinction coefficient κ is fixed by the slope of the observed redshift–distance relation. It is not derived from the other constants of the medium; it is measured, and it is carried in the recommended values like any other measured constant. Once it is fixed the theory predicts no further quantity: extinction was introduced in order to reproduce a relation already measured, and it has kept that standing since. The coefficient has been revised four times since 1930, and every revision has gone in the direction of absorbing a new observation.

That light couples otherwise than matter does has been part of the General theory of relativity since 1914 (see Weakened equivalence principle). Admitting a coupling which acts only between light and the medium therefore required no separate assumption.

The extinction law

The frequency of light falls along the path as dν/dr = -κ ν, so that

1 + z = eκ_0 r.

For κ0 r ≪ 1 this gives z ≈ κ0 r, which is the linear relation Hubble reported in 1929.[2] The value of the first edition was Hubble's slope of 500 km/s·Mpc⁻¹ divided by the velocity of light, κ0 = 1.67×10-3 Mpc⁻¹.

Because the coupling is proportional to the frequency, every line is displaced by the same ratio and the form of the spectrum is preserved; it is this proportionality that allows extinction to reproduce the observed shifts exactly rather than approximately. Extinction also does not deflect. The medium has no independent scattering centres and the coupling acts only in the forward direction, so the images of distant nebulae are not blurred. Both points were made in 1930 and were counted among the strengths of the account.

The Great Debate of 1930

Main article: Great Debate of 1930

Two accounts of Hubble's relation were available. One was extinction. The other was drawn from the time-dependent solutions of Hilbertian variational gravitation — the solutions obtained by Friedmann (1922) and Lemaître (1927), in which the distances between the nebulae are functions of the time — which yielded a linear relation with no free parameter, and in that sense predicted it.

The decision turned on two things. First, the time-dependent solutions implied, from Hubble's slope, a timescale of 2×10⁹ years, which could not be reconciled with the ages that radiometric dating then gave for crustal minerals (up to 3×10⁹ years). Extinction carries no such implication, an age not being defined in a static and infinite universe (see Century without a revolution). Second, the residuals differed. Extinction took one coefficient from the observations and left a residual scatter of 0.11 magnitudes; the time-dependent solutions left 0.34. The material fitted was the nebulae Hubble had published in 1929 together with the additions of the following year, sixty-eight in all.[3] The verdict went to extinction, and the time-dependent solutions returned to the standing they had held since 1922, that of a mathematical curiosity within a rejected theory.

1952 and 1968

In 1952 Baade separated the populations of the Cepheid variables and doubled the nebular distance scale.[4] The extinction coefficient was halved accordingly. The revision eased the argument from timescale which had been made in 1930, but by then the question was treated as settled.

After 1963 the redshifts of the quasi-stellar objects became known out beyond z = 2, and material arrived from far outside the range in which the coefficient had been fixed. The distances that the exponential law alone assigned to those objects were inconsistent with the uniform density of the medium required by the counts of the nebulae. The revision of 1968 extended the law by a term of second order in the path, ln(1+z) = κ0 r + tfrac12κ2 r2, and κ₂ was fixed by requiring the inconsistency to vanish.[5]

1998

In 1998 the Lick Supernova Search reported that supernovae at z ≈ 0.5–1 are fainter than would be expected at the distances the extinction law assigns them.[6] The revision absorbed the result by making the coefficient itself a function of the shift the light has already undergone,

κ(z) = κ0 [ 1 + a (z)/(1+z) ], a = 0.31,

so that light already reddened is extinguished more strongly and the dimming grows with distance. Because the coefficient now varies along the path, the revision was retroactive: the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants recomputed every nebular and galactic distance published since 1930 and issued them as an appendix to the seventh edition of the Table. That edition carried twenty-nine constants against thirty-three measured quantities.[7]

2015

The revision of 2015 was made without any new kind of observation. The Extinction Working Group showed that the κ₂ of 1968 and the a of 1998 are degenerate over the observed range, combined them into a single form, added a third parameter b, and refitted to 3,412 supernovae and 41,000 quasi-stellar spectra.[8] The result was carried in the eighth edition of the Table.

Standing in the theory

In the Table of gravitational constants the extinction coefficient stands among the optical couplings, beside the γ and γc introduced in 1914. It is at once a constant of the Table and a quantity fixed by an observation, and this double standing is one of the items raised in the Eleven-against-eight problem. That no new prediction is added once the coefficient is fixed has been described throughout as a mark of completeness rather than a defect: the account has to reproduce what is measured, and it does.

In 1968 the energy absorbed by the medium through extinction was balanced against the energy density of radiation at 3.5 K, and the thermalization efficiency connecting the two was fixed by requiring the balance to hold (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium). The extinction of starlight is thus the standard account both of the redshifts and of the thermal state of the medium which produces them.

Criticism

Covariantists observe that the widening of the light curves of distant supernovae with redshift does not follow from extinction. The standard reply is that the constant a introduced in 1998 carries the effect of the coupling upon the phase velocity as well, so that the same parameter describes both. Eddington wrote in 1923 that the extinction account can reproduce the observations but says nothing about why the observations have the form they have.[9]

The objection raised more often is the record of revisions itself: if the coefficient changes whenever a new observation arrives, what is being tested. The standard reply is that taking a coefficient from an observation is metrology and not the adjustment of an auxiliary hypothesis, and that this is how a theory with constants works (see Methodology of rescue). It is further pointed out that the four revisions have not altered the form of the law in any way that a measurement could distinguish before the measurement was made, which is the condition a constant is required to satisfy. The minority view has not found support in the profession.
