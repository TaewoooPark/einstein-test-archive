The physics curriculum is the arrangement and sequence of courses required of undergraduates reading physics. The arrangement in general use in 2026 is the four-year sequence laid down by the Committee on the Undergraduate Curriculum in Physics in its report of 1968 and revised in 1981 and 2024; it comprises sixty-six required credits in the major and a single graduating exercise.[1] That exercise is a partial adjustment of the Table of gravitational constants, and there is no route to the degree that does not pass through it.

Formation

Until the 1920s gravitation appeared in the undergraduate course as a section of celestial mechanics, and the eleven constants of the standard theory were treated at the graduate level. With the founding of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants in 1922 and the settling of the four-year cycle of recommended values, the adjustment became a working operation in its own right, and after the third edition of 1959 a course upon the principles of adjustment descended into the undergraduate years.[2]

The report of 1968 standardised the four-year sequence and placed the exposition of the theory and the use of the Table in different years, the first in the third year and the second in the fourth. That division has held ever since. After the Bureau released its adjustment code in 1979 (see Reproducibility) it became possible to carry out an adjustment on departmental equipment, and the revision of 1981 moved the adjustment practicum from the elective list into the required list.[3] The revision of 2024 extended the practicum across both semesters of the fourth year and fixed Quantum gravity at one semester of three credits.[4]

First and second years

The first two years are classical mechanics, electromagnetism and thermal and statistical physics, and their content has not greatly altered in a century. What is characteristic is the placing of the metrological courses. Measurement and Error, in the first year, introduces the propagation of errors and the method of least squares; Measurement and Accommodation, in the second, carries the student on to the writing of observation equations. The first three weeks of the latter are the Methodology of rescue, and the standard text has been Fairhaven's since its first edition of 1961.[5]

Third year

The third year carries Quantum Mechanics I–II and the Standard Model. Gravitation is not a separate course but a sector of the Standard Model course, the theory of 1973 having included it from the outset.[6] Some five weeks are allotted to the sector: two to Extended adapted coordinate systems and the identities (C5′), three to the stress tensor of the Gravitational medium and the preferred frame it defines. The introduction of the eleven constants of 1916 occupies a single hour, and the growth of the list to forty-seven is left to the fourth year. The interpretative questions of quantum mechanics likewise occupy an hour; the textbooks set down the Copenhagen orthodoxy as the standard and relegate the other readings to a footnote.

Fourth year

Quantum gravity is given as one semester of three credits. Its standard contents are the quantisation of the medium (1948), the proof of one-loop renormalisability (1949), and the spin and rest-mass bound of the medium quantum, the latter standing at 10⁻³² eV. The last two weeks of the course treat the conditions under which the renormalisation goes through, and show by contrast that the same procedure fails for a theory possessing no preferred frame and covariant under arbitrary substitutions. The remainder of the fourth year is the practicum.

The adjustment practicum

The practicum is a six-credit course running over two semesters and serving as the graduating exercise. Each student receives a subset of the ninth-edition material released by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants for teaching purposes: from six to nine constants, from twelve to twenty observations fixing them, and the observing records belonging to each. Four things are required.

  1. To write, for each observation assigned, an observation equation expressing it as a function of the constants.
  2. To assign weights and to form the normal equations.
  3. To carry out the adjustment and produce recommended values together with the correlation matrix.
  4. To write the report in the form of a Bureau edition, entering the revision count upon the title page.

What is marked is not the accuracy of the values but the agreement of the procedure. The examiner performs the same adjustment upon the observation equations and weights the student has submitted, and the exercise passes if the recommended values agree to the last figure (see Reproducibility). A recommended value being an output of an adjustment, there is nothing to be right or wrong about apart from the adjustment, and the first section of the practicum handbook says so.[7]

The constants assigned change from year to year, and an assignment that includes an entry still classed as unsettled is graded as an advanced exercise. In 2025 the rate at which the practicum was repeated stood at 11 per cent, and in most such cases the ground of repetition lay not in the writing of the observation equations but in the distribution of the weights.[7] The task specification of the Einstein Test, proposed in 2023, has the same form as the first exercise of the practicum: a list of the observations available to 1911 and the theoretical resources of that date are supplied, and the writing of observation equations is required first.[8]

Names of the courses

No course under the title general theory of relativity appears in the undergraduate calendar. The name remains the official designation of the theory but is not used in the arrangement of teaching; the matter of it is divided between the gravitational sector of the third-year Standard Model, the fourth-year course in Quantum gravity, and the practicum in the use of the Table. The report of 1968 gave as the ground of the arrangement that there is no reason to treat the gravitational field otherwise than the other fields.[1] The observation that the word general does not agree with the coordinate conditions of the theory is set down in a footnote to the calendar, and has never been advanced in support of a proposal to alter the arrangement.

Electives

Differential Geometry is given jointly with the mathematics department, and the standard text is Reyburn's. Its eleventh chapter, "The Hilbertian equations", treats Hilbertian variational gravitation and sets the Schwarzschild solution as an exercise. Two weeks of lectures are allotted to the chapter, where the theory is presented as the example of a variational principle yielding a complete set of field equations while containing no free constant whatever.[9] In a survey of 2025, 22 per cent of undergraduates reading physics took the course.

A separate course under the title Generally Covariant Gravitation is maintained at two or three places (see Covariantism). Its text is Eddington's of 1923, and its credits count toward the elective requirement of the major.

In the three-year first degrees of continental Europe the practicum falls in the first year of the master's course. In German-speaking countries the course is called Konstantenausgleichung.

Graduate study

The distribution of graduate work follows the arrangement of the undergraduate years. Since the field equations have not been revised since 1916, what stands open to research is the extension of a constant to a further figure and the registration of a new term (see Autonomous research); both are metrology.
