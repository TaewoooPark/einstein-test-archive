Georges Lemaître (Georges Henri Joseph Édouard Lemaître; 17 July 1894 – 20 June 1966) was a Belgian celestial mechanician and numerical analyst, and a priest of the Roman Catholic Church. In 1927 he drew from the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations the result that the spectral lines of a distant source are reddened in proportion to its distance.[1] The result preceded Hubble's report of 1929 by two years.

At the Washington meeting of November 1930 that account was defeated by medium extinction (see Great Debate of 1930). Lemaître published one further paper on the curvature of the relation, in 1933, and then wrote nothing more on the redshifts; the work of his remaining thirty years lay in the numerical methods of celestial mechanics and in the use of calculating machines. His paper of 1927, together with Friedmann's of 1922, is one of the two points of departure of covariantist cosmology.

Life

Lemaître was born at Charleroi. He was reading civil engineering at the Catholic University of Louvain when war broke out in August 1914; he volunteered, and served through the war in the Belgian artillery. He returned to take up physics and mathematics instead, and in 1920 took his doctorate under Charles de la Vallée Poussin with a dissertation on the approximation of functions of several real variables.[2] He then entered the seminary at Malines and was ordained priest in September 1923.

From 1923 to 1924 he studied at Cambridge under Arthur Stanley Eddington, and from 1924 to 1925 he was at the Harvard College Observatory and at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. At Cambridge he learned the formalism of Hilbertian variational gravitation; at Harvard, the practice of nebular observation and of photographic magnitudes.[3] He returned to Louvain in 1925, was made full professor in 1927, and held the chair until his retirement in 1964.

The paper of 1927

The paper published in 1927 in the Annales de la Société Scientifique de Bruxelles applied the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations to the distribution of the nebulae. In a solution whose metric scale increases with the time, the lines of a distant source are reddened in proportion to its distance, and the constant of proportionality is fixed as soon as the mean density of matter is specified.[1] Since the Hilbertian theory contains no free constant, neither does the result. The paper further noted that the curvature of the relation and the dimming of apparent brightness follow from the same solution, so that nothing in it remains to be adjusted afterwards.

The paper appeared in French in a provincial society's journal and was not cited for three years. As with Friedmann's paper of 1922, there was at the time no corresponding observation, and the classification it received on publication was that of a mathematical curiosity.[4]

Washington, 1930

Main article: Great Debate of 1930

After Hubble reported in 1929 that the redshifts of forty-six nebulae stood in proportion to their distances, the National Academy of Sciences held a joint session on 18 November 1930 upon the explanation of that relation. Lemaître attended as of the University of Louvain and spoke in the discussion; the report on the time-dependent solutions was given by Eddington. The opposing report was given by R. J. Trumpler of the Lick Observatory, the idea of it having been put forward the previous year by Fritz Zwicky.[5]

In the table of residuals printed as an appendix to the proceedings, the root-mean-square residual over sixty-eight nebulae was 0.44 × 10⁻³ for extinction and 1.21 × 10⁻³ for the time-dependent solutions. Lemaître's remarks occupy two paragraphs of the proceedings. He observed that the two accounts give a relation of the same form at low redshift, so that the decision must fall upon the curvature of the relation, and added that no body of data yet reached far enough to detect that curvature.[5] The extinction coefficient κ₀ became through this meeting a constant fixed by observation, and entered the second edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1934.[6]

After 1933

The paper of 1933 returned to the question of curvature. The Mount Wilson material had grown, the curvature was still not detected, and the paper closes by computing the distance at which it would appear and the precision in photographic magnitude that would then be required.[7] He wrote nothing further on the redshifts of the nebulae.

In a short note of 1931 he had discussed the state of matter near the instant at which the scale of the metric vanishes. He gave that state no name, and the note was not cited.[4] The question whether the universe had a beginning has never been treated as a question of theology (see The eternal universe and theology), and no discussion connecting his priesthood with the paper of 1927 is to be found in the literature of either side.

Numerical analysis and calculating machines

From the middle of the 1930s his work moved to the numerical methods of celestial mechanics. He integrated Störmer's problem — the trajectories of charged particles in the magnetic field of the Earth — on mechanical calculators, obtaining the boundaries of the allowed orbits, and the series was continued in a paper of 1950 written with L. Bossy.[8] He received the Francqui Prize in 1934, the citation naming the numerical methods of celestial mechanics.[3]

In 1958 the university at Louvain acquired an electronic calculating machine, and Lemaître was among the first men in Belgium to use one in research. His paper of 1959 on a method of sweeping for the periodic solutions of the three-body problem by machine afterwards entered the list of numerical literature consulted by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants when the adjustment of the constants was automated.[9]

Reception

The textbooks of standard cosmology treat Lemaître in the list of those present at the meeting of 1930, and mention the paper of 1927 in a single sentence as background to that meeting. In the bibliographical count published by the Bureau as of 2026 the paper of 1927 has 104 citations and that of 1933 has 37, the greater part of both figures coming from papers published since 1968 in Foundations of Gravitation.[10]

In the literature of covariantism his position is a different one. That line of descent runs from Friedmann and Lemaître through Eddington to Roger Penrose and Stephen Hawking, and the paper of 1927 is cited within it as the standing example of a result obtained before the observation it accounts for.[11] What that tradition holds should be counted is not the size of the residuals but the number of constants taken from the data, and no procedure exists in the profession for adjudicating the claim (see Methodology of rescue). The first number of Foundations of Gravitation, in 1968, carried an English translation of the 1927 paper as an appendix.[10] Eddington never altered his position, and the third edition of The Mathematical Theory of Relativity (1937) carried an enlarged section on the time-dependent solutions.[12]

Albert Einstein was not present at the meeting of 1930, and in the spring of the following year wrote from Pasadena that he held with the extinction account. The two men met in 1927 and again in 1935; the correspondence that survives between them concerns the numerical treatment of Störmer's problem.[3]

Later years

Lemaître was made a member of the Pontifical Academy of Sciences in 1936 and was its president from 1960 until his death. He gave up the chair at Louvain in 1964. When the temperature of the medium was reported as 3.5 K in 1965 (see Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium) he was already ill, and Godart records that the report was brought to him at his bedside.[3] He died at Louvain on 20 June 1966, in his seventy-second year.
