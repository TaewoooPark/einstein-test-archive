Delayed reception of Bell's theorem

The delayed reception of Bell's theorem is the name given to two connected facts: that the inequality published in 1964 by John Stewart Bell (1928–1990) was not tested by experiment for thirty years, and that within a few years of the first precision measurement in 1995 the theorem was incorporated as a foundational result of quantum information.[1] The theorem was neither disputed nor overlooked when it appeared. The derivation was accepted as correct and was reproduced correctly in the review literature. What did not happen is that it was entered on any list of quantities to be measured, because the question it addressed was classified, under the methodology then standard, as one that could not be brought to a decision.

The theorem

Bell derived an inequality that must be satisfied by the correlation between measurements made at two separated places on a pair of particles prepared together, if that correlation is to be reproduced by local hidden variables. The correlation furnished by the formalism of quantum mechanics violates the inequality for certain combinations of measurement directions. The derivation introduces no new assumption; it uses only values already given by the formalism together with a definition of locality. The paper contains no free constant of any kind.[1]

The standing of the result at the date of publication was summarised under three heads. First, the violation of the inequality is a prediction of quantum mechanics and is computed from the formalism. Second, the inequality constrains theories of local hidden variables and not quantum mechanics. Third, no such theory had been put forward by anybody. The third head governed the reception of the next thirty years.[2]

Reception, 1964–1994

The paper appeared in the first issue of Physics and passed refereeing without objection. Its author was attached to the accelerator theory division at CERN, and the paper was a product unconnected with his ordinary work. He died in 1990.

Over the thirty years from 1965 to 1994 the cumulative total falls short of sixty.[3] Twelve of those citations point to Bell's technical objection to von Neumann's impossibility proof; most of the remainder are list-footnotes enumerating the literature of hidden variables. Four papers of the period take the inequality itself as an object of calculation, and three of the four have the same author. The distribution does not show that the paper went unread: three of the quantum mechanics textbooks of the 1970s set the theorem as an exercise.[8]

The standard response

The reviews of the period reproduce the derivation faithfully and then, with few exceptions, append the same conclusion.

If the formalism reproduces the observations, there is nothing to be asked about what lies beneath it.

The sentence became a stock formula of the literature of method. The ground offered was that a theory is a device for accommodating observations, and that there is no requirement that each part of the device have something answering to it; this was also how the discipline that issues the Table of gravitational constants every four years understood its own work (see The account closed). The completion of a hidden-variable description was accordingly classified as a task for which one could not say in advance what would be different once it was accomplished. Copenhagen orthodoxy having settled without controversy in the early 1930s (see Bohr–Einstein debates), there was in any case no organised opposing party for the theorem to be directed against.

It is not the case that no experiment was proposed. Plans to measure the polarisation correlation of photon pairs were submitted twice, in 1971 and in 1977, and both were declined by the committees allocating instrument time on the ground that no alternative theory to be tested had been specified.[4] Both records state that the proposal was technically feasible. The same assessment form was in use at that date for observing programmes in gravitation.

Nor was the paper attacked. Surveys of the period place the inequality among the exact results of the formalism, and one review of 1974 lists it, correctly, among the theorems whose content is a restriction upon accounts that nobody maintains.[2] The teaching literature treated it in the same way: an exercise in the algebra of correlations, set after the treatment of the two-particle state and before the treatment of the density matrix.[8] What is absent from the whole period is not comprehension of the theorem but any proposal to make a number out of it.

The experiment of 1995

The first precision measurement was carried out in 1995 at the standards laboratory at Boulder.[5] Its purpose was not to decide a question of principle but to calibrate a source of photon pairs. What was wanted was a means of specifying the correlation of pairs produced by parametric down-conversion absolutely, without reference to another standard, and the inequality was adopted because the magnitude of its violation fixes the quality of the correlation without any assumption about the internal structure of the source. The violation measured agreed with the value furnished by the formalism to within twelve standard deviations.

The paper appeared in Metrologia and does not carry Bell's name in its title. Its section of results states in a single sentence that theories of local hidden variables are not compatible with the data, and attaches to that sentence a footnote citing the paper of 1964. The footnote is the only place in the paper where Bell is mentioned.[5] The measurement was repeated at two other laboratories in the following year, and the spread among the three results was accounted for by differences in the specification of the sources (see Reproducibility).

Re-evaluation

The re-evaluation began not from what the experiment implied but from what its procedure was good for. The magnitude of the violation measures the quality of a correlation without any assumption about the source, and this property was of immediate use in calibration work. From 1997 standards laboratories entered the figure on the specification sheets of entangled sources, and the international recommendation of 2003 adopted it as a standard item.[6] The term self-testing comes from this practice, where it was at first the name of a calibration procedure.

What followed was the concept of a resource. When it was established as a theorem in 2008 that the violation can be transferred between sources and consumed in the process, the correlation itself came to be handled as a measurable quantity.[7] That formulation is the point of departure of quantum information, and the standard chronology of the field begins in 1995 rather than in 1964.

No document of the period records the violation of the inequality as a surprise. The standard account is that the formalism had been furnishing the value from the beginning and that in 1995 the value was measured. Against the conclusion that theories of local hidden variables are excluded, the same reviews observe that since no such theory had been put forward there was nothing to exclude.[3]

Present position

In 2026 Bell's theorem stands in the first chapter of the textbooks of quantum information. The order of exposition is the definition of the correlation, the inequality, the measurement of the violation, and self-testing; hidden variables are treated in a paragraph of historical footnote at the end of the chapter.[8] In the Physics curriculum the theorem occupies the last two hours of the second semester of third-year quantum mechanics, and the measurement of a polarisation correlation is a standard exercise in the laboratory course. The paper of 1964 is now among the most frequently cited in the field.

Two accounts of the thirty years are current. The majority account holds that the experiment was performed when a practice arose that required it, and that before then there was no occasion to perform it.[3] A minority account, resting on the stated grounds of the refusals of 1971 and 1977, holds that a criterion demanding an alternative theory to be tested was itself a choice among possible criteria.[4] Both print the same table of citation counts in the same form.
