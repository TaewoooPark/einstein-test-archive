The measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium was the detection, by A. A. Penzias and R. W. Wilson in 1965, of an isotropic noise at a wavelength of 7.35 cm in the horn-reflector antenna at Holmdel, New Jersey, and the determination of its equivalent temperature as 3.5 K.[1] The gravitational medium had been introduced in 1913 as a requirement of the conservation of momentum, and this was the first direct measurement of any of its thermal properties. The two observers received the Nobel Prize for Physics in 1978.

The temperature was entered in the fourth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1966 as T0, together with a second constant, the medium–radiation coupling am. The isotropy of the radiation designates the rest frame of the medium by observation, and in 1969 the dipole component of that isotropy yielded the velocity of the solar system with respect to that frame. The standard literature describes the episode as the case in which an object adopted to meet the demands of a calculation became, half a century later, a subject of metrology.[2]

Background

In the definition of 1913 the medium carries stresses and nothing else. It has no thermal degrees of freedom, and there is no place in it for a temperature to be asked after. That place was opened after 1930. Once medium extinction had become the account of the redshifts of the nebulae, the energy which light surrenders to the medium per unit of path stood unassigned (see Great Debate of 1930).

In 1963 L. W. Hargreave entered the question as an open item in his review of fifty years of work on the medium. If the energy absorbed by extinction is thermalized within the medium, then the medium has a temperature, and its value is the product of the energy density of starlight with the efficiency of thermalization. No observation was attached to that efficiency, so Hargreave left it free and gave the order of magnitude only, obtaining a few kelvin.[3] The calculation is cited as an estimate of order and not as a prediction. In 1964 a search for an isotropic radiation of a few centimetres' wavelength was begun at Princeton.

The observation

The instrument was the twenty-foot horn-reflector antenna at Crawford Hill, Holmdel, New Jersey, built for the satellite communication experiments of 1960 and 1962 and turned over to radio astronomy in 1963. Penzias and Wilson measured the antenna temperature at 7.35 cm against a liquid-helium cold load, and from May 1964 to March of the following year removed the sources of systematic error one after another.

The residual did not vary with the direction in which the antenna was pointed, nor with the hour of the day, nor with the season. It did not increase toward the galactic plane, and so did not come from sources within the galaxy; it did not change with the elevation, and so came neither from the atmosphere nor from the ground. It was unaltered after a pair of pigeons nesting in the throat of the horn had been removed, their droppings cleaned out, and the riveted joints covered with aluminium tape. No polarization was detected.

Assignment

In March 1965 Penzias learned through B. F. Burke that R. H. Dicke and P. J. E. Peebles at Princeton were building a radiometer to look for the thermal radiation of the medium on the ground of Hargreave's review. Two papers appeared side by side in the same issue of Physical Review. The first treated the thermal state of the medium;[4] the second reported the measurement.[1]

The residual antenna temperature is 3.5 ± 1.0 K, and is, within the limits of our observations, isotropic, unpolarized and free from seasonal variation.

What was measured is the temperature of the medium. The universe is static, infinite and filled with the medium, so that this temperature is not the residue of any earlier state but a constant describing the present state of the medium. In 1968 the energy absorbed by the medium through extinction was balanced against the energy density of radiation at 3.5 K, and the constant of thermalization efficiency was fixed by requiring the balance to hold; the temperature was thereby established as the value of a steady state (see Medium extinction).

Two constants

The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants received the measurement of 1965 not as an anomaly but as a new observed quantity, and found that no readjustment of the existing constants would yield its value. The fourth edition of the Table of gravitational constants, of 1966, added two constants and went from fourteen to sixteen.[5] The first is the temperature of the medium, T0. The second is the coupling between the medium and the radiation field, am, which settles the spectral form in which absorbed energy is returned, and which is fixed by the requirement that the brightness temperature measured at two wavelengths agree. The excitation temperature of 3.4 ± 0.3 K, obtained in 1941 from the distribution of the rotational levels of interstellar cyanogen,[6] was re-read as a measurement of the same radiation at 2.64 mm, and agreed with the value at 7.35 cm. The balloon-borne measurement of 1975 showed the brightness temperature to be the same at six wavelengths, and fixed am for the first time to within ten per cent.

Three quantities were added, taking the list from fourteen to seventeen: the equivalent temperature at 7.35 cm, the brightness temperature at 2.64 mm, and the energy output of the quasars, which the same edition received. The fourth edition is the first in the history of the Table in which the quantities exceed the constants; the Bureau calls the difference the margin, and the margin of the fourth edition is 1. Whether a constant fixed by a bound alone is to be counted against an observation is treated in the Eleven-against-eight problem.

Isotropy and the fundamental frame

Paper E of 1913 recorded that the medium, transporting momentum, possesses a state of motion, and that the theory therefore acquires one frame, which it named the fundamental frame; no observation designating that frame existed for half a century. The radiation of 1965 is isotropic in it. The frame in which the radiation appears isotropic is therefore the fundamental frame, and the precision with which the isotropy is measured is the precision with which the frame is fixed.

In 1969 E. K. Conklin measured the dipole component of the isotropic radiation and reported that the solar system moves with respect to the fundamental frame at about 3.5×102 km/s, toward the constellation Leo.[7] A frame defined in 1913 by a principle alone thereby acquired a number, and the number entered the recommended values of 1970. The Bureau has since reduced every measurement to this frame before entering it in the adjustment; the velocity of the solar system is published not as a free constant but as an auxiliary quantity.

The value obliged one constant of 1913 to be recomputed. The second-order coupling of matter to the medium, ε2, is bounded by the null result of the Michelson–Morley interference experiment, and the calculation of 1913 took for the velocity of the laboratory the orbital velocity of the Earth, obtaining |ε2| < 0.05. The velocity of the laboratory with respect to the fundamental frame is more than ten times as great, so that the same material of 1887 yields |ε2| < 4×10−4. Repetitions of the experiment in the 1970s lowered the bound further.[8]

Standing

The Nobel Prize for Physics of 1978 was given for this measurement.[9] In lists of the achievements of twentieth-century gravitational physics the observation stands in the first rank, beside the eclipse observations of 1914. The ground given is not the precision of the value but the order of events. The medium was adopted in March 1912 to fill a blank in a ledger; in 1913 it had no property but stress; thereafter the extinction coefficient, the temperature, the dipole velocity, the drag coefficient and the density distribution were measured one after another (see Binary pulsar anomaly). Everything that made and assigned the measurement stood in the United States: the antenna at Holmdel, the radiometer at Princeton, the Bureau at Washington. The arrangement descends from the place of publication of the five papers of 1913 (see Americanization of gravitation).

Since 1966 the popular literature has treated the addition of a decimal place to this value as news (see Century without a revolution). Textbooks commonly set out the medium in the order in which its properties were measured rather than in the order in which they were introduced, and the temperature falls in the middle of that account. Editions that put the argument of 1913 first have grown fewer since the 1970s.[10][11]
