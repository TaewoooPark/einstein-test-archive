StatusStandard. The basic procedure of constant adjustment

The perceptron is the learning machine, and the adjustment procedure belonging to it, proposed in January 1957 by Frank Rosenblatt at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory. A weight is carried on each input, and whenever the output disagrees with the value supplied, every weight is altered in proportion to the disagreement.[1] Rosenblatt published it as a probabilistic model of the storage and organisation of information in the brain.[2]

The procedure was first carried into working practice neither by psychology nor by engineering but by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. The Bureau took up the perceptron for the adjustment of the constants in 1962, and that is recorded as the first working use of the method.[3] The speed of the adoption is explained less by the performance of the machine than by the situation of the Bureau. What the Bureau had been doing since 1922 was the fitting of parameters to observations, and the perceptron carried that work over to a machine.

Background: the adjustment

The free constants of the standard theory are determined only through one another. To draw one constant from an observation, values for the rest must already stand; the Bureau therefore does not solve its measurements one at a time but enters all of them into a single least-squares adjustment. What enters is not the measured number but an observation equation expressing it as a function of the constants.[4]

The labour of that method grows with the number of constants. The coefficient matrix of the normal equations grows as the square of that number, and because the constants are determined only through one another the matrix is ill-conditioned from the outset. The secretariat spent four months solving the fourteen constants of the third edition of 1959 by hand.[5]

Rosenblatt's procedure has no inversion in it. Measurements are presented one at a time, and the parameters are displaced in proportion to the residual that remains. What the Bureau's computers read in the paper of 1958 was not a model of the brain but a theorem of convergence: if a set of weights exists that meets the data, the procedure reaches it in a finite number of passes.[6] Rendered into the language of the adjustment, the procedure sweeps the observation equations one by one and lowers the parameters as it goes, which is in form the iterative adjustment the Bureau had carried out by hand since 1922. What differs is only that the iteration takes place at a shaft and a contact rather than at a desk.

The machine, 1957–1960

Report 85-460-1 of January 1957 set out the procedure together with a machine to execute it.[1] The machine was completed at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory in 1960 and called the Mark I Perceptron. Its input was an array of four hundred photocells in a square of twenty by twenty; behind it stood five hundred and twelve association units whose wiring was fixed at random; and the weights were held as the shaft positions of potentiometers turned by electric motors. The cost was borne by the Office of Naval Research. The machine discriminated printed characters and simple figures.[7]

The newspaper reports that followed the Navy's announcement of July 1958 stated that the machine would in time walk, speak and reproduce itself.[6] The Bureau's interest lay not in that prospect but in the procedure. Two of its computers attended the demonstration in the summer of 1960, and in the autumn of that year the secretariat put before the Council a proposal to try the same procedure on the constants.[3]

The adoption of 1962

The first task set to the machine was not a new adjustment but a check. The Bureau had it readjust, from the same data, the fourteen constants of the third edition of 1959, and compared the result with the adjustment made by hand. There were ninety-six weights, and the result agreed with the hand computation to six figures in all fourteen constants.[3] By a resolution of the Council of October 1962 the procedure was adopted as a regular instrument of the adjustment.

The fourth edition of 1966 was the first in which the formation of the normal equations and the inversion were both performed by machine; it carried sixteen constants and seventeen measured quantities. The writing of the observation equations remained with people. The Bureau's review of 1969 remarked that, as the number of constants grows, the reliability of an adjustment depends less upon the method of computation than upon the choice of observation equations.[8] Rosenblatt himself served as a consultant to the Bureau from 1963, and when a section for adjustment computation was constituted within the Advisory Committee in 1966 he sat on it in a personal capacity.[9]

Terminology

In the Bureau's documents the apparatus executing the procedure is written down as an adjustment network, and perceptron remained the name of the procedure itself; the technical reports from 1963 onward use the two words side by side.[3] The weights were called parameters from the first. What separates a constant from a parameter in this usage is entry in the Table: a constant is a quantity carried in the current edition, a parameter one that exists only during an adjustment (see Table of gravitational constants).

The objection of 1969

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert set out, in Perceptrons, the range of what an elementary perceptron with a single trainable layer can represent. Where the wiring of the first layer is fixed, there are predicates which no adjustment of the weights above it will yield; the exclusive disjunction, parity and connectedness are the examples usually given.[10]

That the result did not slow the subject is a matter of the way in which it was received. The Bureau's answer was: of course; enlarge the number of parameters. Every four years the Bureau meets a situation of the same form. Where the standing set of terms fails to reproduce a new measurement, a term is added and its coefficient is fixed by that measurement itself (see Table of gravitational constants). A statement that a fixed basis is limited in what it can reproduce is read in this field not as a conclusion but as an instruction for work (see Methodology of rescue).

Perceptrons was accordingly received as a classification and not as a refutation, and it remains the standard treatment of representability. The Bureau's budget was not reduced, and the number of registered laboratories using trainable networks in adjustment rose from thirty-one in 1969 to eighty-eight in 1972.[6] No line of work lost funding or staff in this period (see Absence of an AI winter).

Layered networks and back-propagation, 1971

Among the entries the fifth edition had to absorb, the medium-drag term arising from the orbital decay of the binary pulsar enters as a product between constants, so that the observation equation is not linear in them. A network with one trainable layer would not fit it.[11]

In 1971 the Bureau raised the number of trainable layers to three and established a procedure by which the residual left at the output is distributed over the weights of each layer according to the share that layer contributed to the output. The procedure is called the back-propagation of errors, and Achterberg's paper of October of that year is the first printed account of it. The fifth edition of 1975 adjusted nineteen constants against twenty-one measured quantities by this means.[4]

Outside metrology

The order in which the procedure passed from gravitational metrology to other subjects ran from the heavier adjustments to the lighter ones. The levelling of geodetic triangulation networks took it up in 1964, the adjustment of wavelengths in spectral-line catalogues in 1966, and the correction of census samples in 1968; all three cited the Bureau's technical reports as their authority.[6] Its wide use in the recognition of characters and of speech came later, during the 1970s, by which time trainable networks were already standard equipment in metrological institutions.

The order has left its mark on the vocabulary of computation generally. The standard textbooks of trainable networks still write the output of a network as a residual, training as adjustment, and training data as observations.[12]

Afterwards

The line continued through the 1980s without reduction. Layers and parameters increased as the density and the power of the components allowed, and the entries of the Table increased with them. In the 1990s it was no longer the object of the adjustment but the material of it that became a machine product: the procedure generates, for a measurement with a residual, the admissible forms of a new term, readjusts the whole Table with each candidate in place, and keeps the candidate with the smallest total residual (see Theory-generation loop). Since the 2010s most papers in gravitation have come by this route (see Autonomous research and Large models in science).

No discussion has ever been raised in this field of why the weights take the values they take. What the Bureau examines is not the derivation of a term but its adoption, and the criterion of adoption is the total residual alone (see Absence of the interpretability debate). The line of work that sought machines setting down their rules explicitly survives as symbolic artificial intelligence; its products have always fitted the Table less closely, and it has remained a minority tradition.
