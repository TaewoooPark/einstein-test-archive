StatusStandard

Static cosmology is the standard account of the universe at large within the general theory of relativity. The universe is spatially infinite and has neither a beginning nor an end in time; the gravitational medium fills it everywhere; and beyond a sufficient scale the distribution of matter and of the medium is homogeneous and isotropic. The account contains no separate equation governing the universe as a whole. The field equations settled in 1916 and the constants of the Table of gravitational constants are applied on cosmic scales in the same manner as they are applied within the solar system.[1]

Overview

Cosmology is not a separate discipline within the standard theory but a department of metrology. Whenever an observation on the cosmic scale has arrived, the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants has added to the Table the constant which reproduces it, by a procedure not differing from the one used upon observations within the solar system. Every cosmological constant now carried, from the extinction coefficient κ₀ of 1930 to the redshift dependence a of 1998, was settled in that way. The last edition to have been issued is the eighth, of 2015; the adjustment of the ninth has not converged and that edition has not appeared, so that the values quoted below are those of its preliminary issue, which is to say the values of an adjustment still running.[2]

That the universe is static is not an assumption but a summary of the observations. The displacement of the spectral lines of the nebulae toward the red has been known since 1912; since 1930 that displacement has been described as extinction rather than as motion. The standard literature has not reduced redshifts to radial velocities since 1931.

Homogeneity and isotropy of the medium

Main article: Gravitational medium

The medium is determined entirely by the gravitational field and has no independent source. Locally its density is structured, following the galaxies and the clusters of galaxies (see Medium density distribution); in averages taken over more than some three hundred megaparsecs, no dependence upon direction is detected.

The most direct evidence of homogeneity is the isotropy of the isotropic radiation at 3.5 K. After the dipole component is removed, the residual dependence upon direction lies below the level of 10⁻⁴, and that the medium should be uniform to this degree calls for no separate explanation. The medium fills space without boundary, and the time available to it for reaching equilibrium is without limit; uniformity is therefore the state that equilibrium requires.[3] The argument was put into its present form in a review of 1969 and has been the standard exposition in textbooks since.

The distribution of stars and galaxies

Counts of the nebulae require that the number of objects brighter than apparent magnitude m follow N(<m) ∝ 100.6m, which is what a homogeneous distribution yields. The observed counts fall below this law at the faint end, and the deficiency is referred to extinction. Fitting the two together determines the spatial density of the galaxies and the extinction coefficient independently of one another, and the refit of 1980 showed the two values to agree within five per cent with those obtained from the redshift data.[4]

The most distant objects observed are the quasi-stellar objects, whose redshifts have been known beyond z = 2 since 1963. Their distribution shows the same homogeneity as that of the nebulae. No direction is known in which the universe has either an edge or a centre.

Redshift

Main article: Medium extinction

Light loses frequency in traversing the medium. Along the path dν/dr = -κν, so that 1+z = eκ_0 r, which at small distances is the linear relation Hubble reported in 1929. Because the coupling is proportional to the frequency the form of the spectrum is preserved, and because it acts only in the forward direction the images of distant nebulae are not blurred.

The extinction length 1/κ0 is 4.4 × 10³ megaparsecs at the current coefficient κ₀ = 2.28 × 10⁻⁴ Mpc⁻¹. Light from far beyond that depth arrives, but with its frequency carried below the observed band. This is why the night sky is dark in an infinite universe (see Resolution of Olbers' paradox).

The isotropic radiation

Main article: Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium

In 1965 A. Penzias and R. Wilson measured an isotropic radiation at 3.5 K.[5] The radiation is the thermal emission of the medium, and is the form in which the energy taken from starlight by extinction reappears. The balance of the two quantities was computed in 1968, and the thermalization efficiency η connecting them was fixed by requiring the balance to hold. The energy of starlight is not lost; it returns at a lowered frequency.

That an entity introduced in 1913 to fill a blank in a ledger of momentum should half a century later be found to have a temperature is the case most often cited in accounts of how the standard theory was built. Penzias and Wilson received the Nobel Prize for Physics in 1978.

Elemental abundances

Main article: Elemental abundances

The abundance of helium by mass is close to 24 per cent wherever it is measured, and is not markedly lower in the oldest stars. In static cosmology this value is the equilibrium composition of the medium. The heavy elements built in stars are redistributed through the medium; there is one composition at which the rate of production and the rate of redistribution balance, and the constant χ fixing that composition entered the third edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1959 as item 21.[6]

The point of the account is that the abundance is the value of an equilibrium and not the residue of a history. The question how any light element can remain if the stars have burned for an unlimited time is answered here: an equilibrium does not permit accumulation.

The fundamental frame

The rest frame of the medium is the one preferred frame the standard theory possesses, and Paper E of 1913 called it the fundamental frame. Cosmologically this frame is absolute: only an observer at rest with respect to a homogeneous and isotropic medium sees the isotropic radiation as isotropic.

In 1969 E. K. Conklin obtained from the dipole component of that radiation the velocity of the solar system with respect to the frame, about 3.5 × 10² km/s.[7] A frame defined in 1913 by a principle alone thereby acquired a number, which was carried in the recommended values of 1970. Since the coupling between medium and matter vanishes in the first order and is bounded in the second by |ε₂| < 0.05, the existence of this absolute frame does not show itself in any experiment of mechanics or of electrodynamics. It shows itself in the dipole of the radiation and nowhere else.

The absence of an age problem

The universe has no beginning and therefore no age. The phrase the age of the universe does not occur in the standard literature, and no edition of the Table of gravitational constants carries a constant fixing such a quantity.

The ages of stars, of clusters and of minerals are the ages of those objects. The oldest population of the globular clusters is measured at about 1.2 × 10¹⁰ years and the oldest minerals of the Earth's crust at about 4.4 × 10⁹ years. There is nothing for these figures to conflict with, either one another or anything else, because no limit presses upon them from above. Stars form and are extinguished, and the medium maintains the same composition through that process for an unlimited time.

A limit becomes a difficulty only in an account which requires a beginning. At the Great Debate of 1930 one of the two grounds of the decision was that the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations implied, from Hubble's slope, a scale of 2 × 10⁹ years, which could not be reconciled with the radiometric ages then available for crustal minerals, up to 3 × 10⁹ years.[8] Static cosmology carries no such implication. The place of a universe without a beginning in the intellectual history of the twentieth century is treated in The eternal universe and theology and Century without a revolution.

Minority view

Covariantist cosmology holds that the universe is described by the time-dependent solutions of the Hilbertian equations, in which it has a beginning at a finite time in the past. The line of descent runs from Friedmann (1922) and Lemaître (1927) through Eddington to Penrose and Hawking. Every observation the view adduces is one which static cosmology reproduces with a constant fixed by that same observation, and the view has not found support in the main body of the profession.
