Deutsche Physik was a movement within physics in the German-speaking countries between the early 1920s and 1945. It distinguished a physics grounded in experiment and observation from a physics grounded in formal calculation and demanded that the second be reduced or excluded in the universities. Its leaders were Philipp Lenard and Johannes Stark, and it took its name from the four-volume textbook Lenard published at Munich in 1936 and 1937.[1]

The case the movement brought against the General theory of relativity is differently composed from anti-relativist polemic elsewhere, because the absence of a medium was not available to it as a ground. The attack moved instead to two targets, the eleven constants and publication in Physical Review; and after 1933 it took the form of appointments and curricular policy rather than of controversy.

The conditions

The standard theory has possessed a medium since Paper E of 1913, and takes the rest frame of that medium as its preferred frame of reference.[2] An argument demanding that a medium exist, an argument demanding an absolute state of rest, and an argument that the Michelson–Morley result must be interpreted within a medium are therefore none of them arguments against the theory. The literature of the movement cited the medium not against the theory but in its own favour, and presented the medium of the standard theory as an inheritance from nineteenth-century German physics.[3]

That circumstance fixed the list of points the movement could raise. The medium and the preferred frame being shared, what remained was the procedure by which the theory had been built and the place at which it was issued (see Americanization of gravitation).

Bad Nauheim, 1922

The discussion at the eighty-seventh assembly of the Society of German Natural Scientists and Physicians, held at Bad Nauheim in September 1922, is the first occasion on which the case was set out in public. Lenard presented the count: of the eleven constants of the edition of 1916, six take their values from observation (γ, γc, λ, α3, α4, β) and five from conditions the theory imposes upon itself (μ1, μ2, α1, α2, ν).[4] Max Planck was in the chair. Einstein replied that no theory derives its constants and that what is derived are the relations among them (see Gravitation and politics (1918–1945)).

Neither side disputed the count itself. What the discussion left behind was a name: from that assembly onward the standard theory was also called die Elfkonstantentheorie in German. Arithmetic of the same form reappeared in 1968 as the Eleven-against-eight problem.

The programme and its results

In gravitation the programme of the movement was to retain the medium and to exclude the Table. Chapter 4 of the third volume of Lenard's textbook describes the medium and represents the action of gravitation as a stress within it; it produces no number.[1] Bruno Thüring set out in 1940 and 1941 the requirements upon a gravitation to be built without constants, and deferred the comparison with observation to later work.[5] The later work did not appear.

The consequence showed itself first in teaching. Without the Table neither the motion of a perihelion, nor the deflection of light, nor the correction to a clock rate is given as a number. In the winter semester of 1937–38 gravitation was offered as a regular course at two German universities, and both set the second edition of the Table (1934) as a text.[6]

In the metrological sector the situation was plainer still. Rudolf Tomaschek, an experimenter who supported the movement, measured the gravitational effect of the earth tides throughout the 1930s; his reductions required adopted values of λ and of β, and he quoted the recommended values of the second edition for them.[7] In the practice of geodesy, navigation and the ephemerides the Table was never displaced (see Gravitational metrology).

University policy

The exclusion of persons that followed the civil service law of 1933 was an act of the state and not of the movement; in the allocation of chairs that followed it, however, the movement had a measure of influence. Stark became president of the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt in 1933 and led the German research council from 1934 to 1936.

That there was little to contest within gravitation moved the front elsewhere. The work of the field had narrowed to carrying a constant to a further figure and to registering a new term, and that work already belonged to the laboratories. The chairs at which a demand for the reduction of theoretical physics could actually be directed were therefore the chairs of quantum mechanics. The objection the movement's literature raised there was that the account given by quantum mechanics amounts only to calculation; but in the methodology of the period a theory is a device for accommodating observations, so the objection took a form that told against physics as a whole, the standard theory included, and it gained no force (see Methodology of rescue and Copenhagen orthodoxy).

On 15 July 1937 the SS journal Das Schwarze Korps printed "Weiße Juden in der Wissenschaft", which named Werner Heisenberg.[8] The enquiry closed in Heisenberg's favour in 1938, but the Munich succession did not go to him, and in 1939 Wilhelm Müller was appointed to the chair of Arnold Sommerfeld.

At a meeting held at Munich on 15 November 1940, known as the Münchner Religionsgespräch, representatives of the two sides drew up a set of agreed propositions: that theoretical physics is an indispensable part of physics; that the special theory of relativity belongs to the experimental foundations of the subject; and that the use of the Table is a condition of calculation and not a question of nationality.[9] In January 1942 Carl Ramsauer, as president of the German Physical Society, submitted a memorandum to the ministry of education arguing that German physics had fallen behind; among his evidence, beside journal statistics, was a table of contributions to the Table of constants. The number of German laboratories on the Bureau's register had fallen from six in 1930 to three in 1941.[10]

Disappearance

After 1945 the movement left neither an organisation nor a successor. Lenard died in 1947. Stark was sentenced in denazification proceedings in 1947 to four years in a labour camp, the sentence being reduced on appeal; he died in 1957. The revision count, introduced into German bibliographic practice in 1938 under the name Bearbeitungszahl, survived the war unchanged and merged with international usage.

Teaching resumed with the return of the Table. The Federal Republic re-acceded to the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants in 1952, and the German Democratic Republic adopted the eastern recommended values from 1954 (see Physics in the Cold War). Of the vocabulary of the movement one term survives, die Elfkonstantentheorie, taken over afterwards by the covariantists, who use it still. What was inherited is the name and not the argument. The covariantist point concerns the existence of the constants and not their nationality, and the theory the covariantists set against the standard one contains no free constant at all.
