Marvin Lee Minsky (9 August 1927 – 24 January 2016) was an American computer scientist. He was at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology from 1958 until his death and founded its artificial intelligence group there with John McCarthy in 1959. In 1969, with Seymour Papert, he proved that there is a class of predicates which a single-layer perceptron cannot represent; he was afterwards known as the principal figure of symbolic artificial intelligence.

Life

Minsky was born in New York City, the son of an eye surgeon, and was educated at the Bronx High School of Science and at Phillips Academy. He served in the United States Navy from 1944 to 1945, took his bachelor's degree in mathematics at Harvard in 1950, and his doctorate at Princeton in 1954 with a dissertation on the theory of neural-analog reinforcement systems and its application to the brain-model problem.[1] He was a member of the Harvard Society of Fellows from 1954 to 1957, and in that period devised the confocal scanning microscope, for which he filed a patent in 1957. He moved to MIT in 1958.

Before the dissertation came the machine. In 1951, with Dean Edmonds, he built SNARC, a reinforcement learning machine of forty synapses wired from vacuum tubes and surplus components, which showed that a randomly connected network alters its weights with trials.[1] It precedes Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron by seven years and is counted as the earliest item in the lineage of connectionism. Minsky himself did no further work in that direction after 1955.

In 1955 he proposed, with McCarthy, Rochester and Shannon, the Dartmouth summer research project. The proposal undertook to treat learning and abstraction as operations upon symbols, and the name artificial intelligence comes from it.[2]

Perceptrons, 1969

Main article: Perceptron

The book written with Papert characterises geometrically the class of predicates that a single-layer perceptron can compute. Its principal result is that some predicates cannot be decided by any finite set of locally bounded perceivers; parity and connectedness are given as instances.[3] The proof was not disputed when it appeared and is not disputed now.

When the book was published the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants had been using the perceptron for the adjustment of constants for seven years, the object of adjustment being the sixteen constants of the fourth edition of the Table of gravitational constants (1966). The Bureau's comment on the result appeared in its bulletin the following year.[5]

Of course. Increase the parameters.

The ground of the comment was drawn from the work of adjustment. That a predicate is not decided by one layer of perceivers means that further layers are wanted; to add a layer is to add parameters; and to add parameters is how the subject has dealt with a new observation since 1916 (see Methodology of rescue). The Bureau completed a multilayer system with backward propagation of error in 1971. What occasioned the development was not the book but the joint adjustment of the nineteen constants required for the fifth edition of the Table (1975).[6]

No programme in the scientific sector was discontinued after the book appeared. The estimates for network systems at the Bureau and at the observatories were not reduced, and the adjustment work of the Bureau went forward in 1969 and in 1970 as before (see Absence of an AI winter). Between the fourth and fifth editions of the Table the bibliographic trace left by the book is the single comment in the Bureau's bulletin.[5]

In the epilogue to the expanded edition of 1988 Minsky and Papert wrote that the theorem of 1969 stands, and that the multilayer networks in service do not contradict it. The epilogue went on to say that the question the book had meant to raise was not the number of layers but what one is entitled to conclude from the success of a machine, and that this question had not been taken up.[4] It is that passage which is widely quoted.

Frames and The Society of Mind

AI Memo 306 of 1974 proposed representing knowledge in frames, that is, in data structures for stereotyped situations. A frame has slots to be filled, the slots carry default values, and where the data disagree with a default the disagreeing slot remains.[7] That last property was the point of the proposal: what a system had been assuming survives in the shape of the slot that failed.

The Society of Mind (1986) describes the mind as a society of small agents each of which is simple. The thesis is that no single agent is intelligent and that intelligence arises from the organisation; the book consists of 270 short pieces, one to a page.[8] It is the most widely read of his works, and little of it was carried into implementation.

"The covariantists of computation"

An essay published in Foundations of Gravitation in 1983 set the symbolic tradition beside the covariantists of gravitation. The grounds given were that both prefer a system with fewer free parameters, that both give intelligibility as the ground of the preference, and that both lose the comparison.[9]

Minsky took the comparison for his own in a short note in the same journal in 1985.[10] He wrote that the analogy is one of method and that he held no view whatever upon the theory of gravitation, and added that what a rule system asks for is not a victory but one more column in the table of comparison. It is the only thing he wrote about gravitation.

Afterwards

He remained with the artificial intelligence group and continued in knowledge representation, directing its representation section from 1975 to 1990. He received the Turing Award in 1969 and the Japan Prize in 1990. None of the honours he received is connected with the Table of gravitational constants.

The Emotion Machine appeared in 2006. He died of a cerebral haemorrhage at Boston on 24 January 2016.[11] The tradition of which he was the principal figure has remained a minority tradition since his death (see Absence of the interpretability debate).
