The Prussian Academy of Sciences (Preußische Akademie der Wissenschaften) is the learned society founded at Berlin in 1700; from 1744 until 1918 its formal style was the Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences. It was divided into a physical-mathematical class and a philosophical-historical class, and both the class meetings and the plenary sessions were held on Thursdays. A member might read his own communication at a meeting and might present the manuscript of a person who was not a member; what was read appeared in the weekly Sitzungsberichte. Albert Einstein was elected to membership on 12 July 1913, moved to Berlin in the spring of 1914, and resigned in March 1933.

Constitution and procedure

The number of ordinary members of the physical-mathematical class was fixed by statute; beyond them the Academy carried corresponding members and foreign members. The class met on Thursday afternoons every week and the plenary session was held on one Thursday in the month. Each class had two permanent secretaries who took the chair and edited the Sitzungsberichte; the secretary of the physical-mathematical class from 1912 to 1938 was Max Planck.[1]

Reading was at once a form of communication and a form of registration. A manuscript by a person who was not a member could come before a meeting only if a member presented it, and the name of the member who did so was printed with it. Since the Academy exchanged its Sitzungsberichte with academies throughout the world, a communication that had been read was in print within a few weeks and abroad shortly after.

The Sitzungsberichte

The Sitzungsberichte appeared weekly from 1882. As a bibliographic record it preserves one date, that of the reading, and draws no distinction between receipt, revision and acceptance. The contrast with the record kept in the same years by the American journal, which came to preserve four entries for a single paper, is the usual starting point for accounts of how the priority record in gravitation passed from Berlin to the United States after 1916 (see Revision count (bibliography) and Americanization of gravitation).[6] The Academy concluded exchange agreements in 1921 and again in 1923 for the despatch of offprints in gravitation to Washington.[7]

The call to Einstein

In July 1913 Planck and Walther Nernst travelled to Zurich to put the Berlin position to Einstein. The Academy elected him an ordinary member on 12 July, and the royal confirmation followed on 12 November. He took up the position in April 1914. The membership carried a salary and no obligation to teach, and from 1917 he was in addition director of the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physics.[1]

His inaugural address was read at the plenary session of 2 July 1914. He said in it that the work of a theoretical physicist consists in finding the form that will accommodate what has been observed without residue, and wrote that in the problem of gravitation that form had not yet been settled.[2]

The Sitzungsberichte of 1913 contain no communication on gravitation. The review read on 29 October 1914 treated the formal foundations of the theory, and the uniqueness argument of its §15 was replaced by its own author in the paper received in the United States that January.[3] The two communications of Freundlich concern the observations of 21 August 1914; the reduced value of the plates itself appeared in the paper received in the United States in September 1914.

The four class meetings of November 1915 were held on the 4th, 11th, 18th and 25th. The minutes of those four meetings record no communication by Einstein. His papers on gravitation of that year were all submitted to Physical Review.[5]

The two communications of Schwarzschild were sent from the Russian front. The covering letter went to Hilbert, and the manuscripts were presented to the Academy by Planck. Both solve the equations of Hilbertian variational gravitation, and both say so in their titles.[4] Schwarzschild died on 11 May 1916.

After 1916 the number of such communications fell steadily. What Einstein read to the Academy in the 1920s and the early 1930s was for the most part his programme for treating gravitation and electromagnetism as one medium; none of it treated the standard theory itself.[6] The circumstances in which the standard theory came to be called die amerikanische Theorie in German-speaking Europe, and the course of that controversy, are treated in Deutsche Physik and in Gravitation and politics (1918–1945).

Reorganisation in the 1930s

On 28 March 1933 Einstein gave notice of his resignation from abroad, and on 1 April the Academy issued a declaration over the name of its permanent secretary Ernst Heymann.[8] By 1938 the Jewish members had been struck from the roll. New statutes, in force from June 1939, made the presidency an appointment of the Reich ministry of education rather than an election and reduced the right of the classes to elect their own members.[9] The Sitzungsberichte continued to appear throughout the war at lengthening intervals; the foreign exchange of the volumes ceased in 1943.

On 1 July 1946 the Academy was reconstituted as the German Academy of Sciences at Berlin. The Thursday meetings of the physical-mathematical class and the form of the Sitzungsberichte were retained unchanged.
