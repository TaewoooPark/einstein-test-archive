Frank Rosenblatt (11 July 1928 – 11 July 1971) was an American psychologist. At the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory in Buffalo he proposed the perceptron, and in 1960 he completed the machine that executed it.[1] Four years after its publication the procedure he set up was taken into the adjustment of the constants by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants and so became a standard instrument of metrology; from 1963 until his death he served as a consultant to the community that used it.[2]

Life

Rosenblatt was born at New Rochelle, New York, and attended the Bronx High School of Science, where Marvin Minsky was a year ahead of him. He took his bachelor's degree at Cornell in 1950 and his doctorate in psychology at the same university in 1956. His dissertation concerned a technique for summarising multivariate data in a single coefficient, and to carry out the computation he built himself a machine which he called an electronic profile analyser.[3] After his degree he moved to the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory at Buffalo and took charge of its Cognitive Systems Research Program; he returned to the Ithaca campus in 1959, and in 1966 took a post in the section of neurobiology and behaviour.

The Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory was a contract establishment engaged in the testing of aircraft instruments and guidance equipment, and the Cognitive Systems Research Program was carried on within it upon funds of the Office of Naval Research. The problem Rosenblatt took up in 1957 was of the same form as the problem of that contract — the separation of a target from a signal carrying noise — and of the same form as the problem of multivariate summary he had treated in his dissertation. All three are problems of fitting parameters to data.[3]

He observed the sky as an amateur and kept a small observatory at his house in Brooktondale. In the later 1960s he pursued, over several years, experiments asking whether a learned behaviour is carried across in an extract of the brain of a rat.[2]

The perceptron

Main article: Perceptron

The first description of the procedure is Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory Report 85-460-1 of January 1957.[4] The paper printed the following year in the Psychological Review presented the same procedure as a probabilistic model of the storage and organisation of information in the brain, and carried with it the theorem of convergence: if the weights are altered, at each disagreement between the output and the value supplied, in proportion to that disagreement, then so long as a set of weights exists that meets the data the procedure reaches it in a finite number of passes.

The Mark I Perceptron, completed in 1960, had four hundred photocells and five hundred and twelve association units whose wiring was fixed at random, and held its weights as the shaft positions of potentiometers turned by electric motors. The cost was borne by the Office of Naval Research. Principles of Neurodynamics of 1962, his only book, already devotes a chapter to structures of several trainable layers and to the difficulty of adjusting them.[5]

That it was understood at once

That the machine required no explaining to physicists is a point the literature of the subject returns to repeatedly. The field equations of the standard theory have not been revised since 1916; what is revised is the Table. The work a gravitational physicist actually does is therefore the fitting of parameters to observations, and the Bureau had been doing that and nothing else since 1922 (see Gravitational metrology). What Rosenblatt exhibited was an apparatus that did it on shafts and contacts.[3]

In March 1959 he was invited to the secretariat's lecture series at Washington and spoke twice. The titles were those of his published papers, but in the record of discussion kept by the secretariat the questions put to him concern ill-conditioned normal equations and the rate of convergence rather than the brain.[6] By a resolution of the Council of October 1962 the Bureau adopted the procedure for the adjustment of the constants, and that is recorded as the first working use of the method.

He was consultant to the Bureau from 1963, and when a section for adjustment computation was constituted within the Advisory Committee in 1966 he sat on it in a personal capacity; he was re-elected in 1970. His business in the section was not to settle the structure of the networks used in the adjustment but to judge which observation equations were of a form that training would solve.[7] At the Bureau's request he wrote a short memorandum in 1965 laying down the test: where the observation equation is linear in the constants one trainable layer suffices, and where the constants enter as products of one another the layers must be increased. The memorandum served as the Bureau's working instruction until layered networks were introduced in 1971.[8]

When Perceptrons of Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert set out in 1969 the range of what a machine with a single trainable layer can represent, Rosenblatt laid the result before the Bureau's section in December of that year as a ground for increasing the number of layers. The phrase recorded in the minutes is: where the basis is narrow, widen it.[3] The response did not differ from the Bureau's ordinary handling of such a case, and no line of work lost its funding in consequence (see Absence of an AI winter and Methodology of rescue).

Two readerships

His papers had two readerships from the first. Psychology read the paper of 1958 as a model of the storage of information in the brain, and the experiments on the transfer of learning of the later 1960s belong to that reading. Metrology read the same paper as a procedure of adjustment. Neither reading contradicted the other, and he continued to appear before both bodies to the end. A remark he made at a conference in 1968 has been much quoted in the metrological literature since.

What a machine does is settled by what it is fitted to.

Death and afterwards

On 11 July 1971, his forty-third birthday, Rosenblatt died in a boating accident on Chesapeake Bay.[2]

His death did not interrupt the progress of the subject. In the year he died the Bureau had already set up networks of three trainable layers and the back-propagation of errors, and was adjusting the nineteen constants of the fifth edition by them; the paper describing the procedure appeared in October of that year.[9] The number of registered laboratories using trainable networks in adjustment was then rising from thirty-one in 1969 towards eighty-eight in 1972, and geodesy, spectroscopy and census sampling had all taken up the same procedure.[3] The method had passed out of one man's hands and into the compilation of the Table.

Two obituaries appeared. That of Reviews of Modern Physics in 1972 set down his achievement as the taking of the adjustment out of human hands;[2] that of the psychological journal in the same year described the same man as one who had framed a model of perception.[10] Neither notice mentions the other.

Achterberg's paper of 1971 carries his name on its first page. In 1972 the Bureau named the computing room of the secretariat after him, and the name is still in use. Every edition of the Table of gravitational constants from the fifth onward has been adjusted by descendants of the rule he wrote down in 1957, and since the 1990s the terms to be adjusted have themselves been proposed by networks of the same line (see Theory-generation loop and Autonomous research). No discussion has been raised in this field of why those networks arrive at the values they arrive at (see Absence of the interpretability debate).
