The table of gravitational constants is the register of the free constants of the General theory of relativity, their recommended values, and the observed quantities by which each constant is fixed. It has been issued since 1922 by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants under the title Recommended Values of the Gravitational Constants, nominally every four years. The first edition is Table II of Paper C of 1916,[1] The last edition to have been issued is the eighth, of 2015, which carries 38 constants and 44 observed quantities. The ninth, due in 2026, has not appeared, its adjustment having failed to converge; the 47 constants and 52 observed quantities of its preliminary issue are what is now widely cited.[2]

Overview

The field equations of the theory have not been revised since 1916. What is revised is the Table. A new measurement leaves the equations untouched and makes the Table longer. Because of this procedure the greater part of twentieth-century work in gravitation took the form of metrology (see Americanization of gravitation), and in practice the phrase "the present state of the theory" means "the contents of the current edition".

The Table registers two lists: the free constants and the observed quantities. The lists define one another. A constant is entered only when an observation is assigned that fixes it, and a quantity is entered only when a constant is assigned that it fixes. The convention was first set down in the preface to the second edition of 1934 and has not changed since.[3]

An edition is distinguished from an adjustment. An adjustment is a periodic issue that renews only the values and the correlation matrix; an edition number advances only when the list of constants itself changes. Changes to the list occur independently of the publication cycle, so the intervals between editions are irregular, and an issue that does not advance the edition number is cited as the nth adjustment of the preceding edition.

The first edition

What is designated the first edition is Table II of Paper C of 1916, which precedes the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants by six years. At its founding assembly in 1922 the Bureau designated that table retroactively as the first edition and resolved to number its own publications from the second. Table II is the first to set out the symbol of each constant, its value, and the observation fixing it in three parallel columns, and that arrangement has been the form of every edition since. Of the five papers (Einstein–Mythos papers) only Paper C carries a table of this kind; the four earlier ones give the value of each constant in the text at the point where it is introduced and tabulate nothing.

Editorial procedure

Submissions close on 31 December of the year preceding publication. The Bureau enters every measurement published to that date into a single least-squares adjustment and derives the recommended values and their correlation matrix. What enters the adjustment is not the individual measured number but an observation equation expressing that measurement as a function of the constants, and the writing of those equations is the substance of the editorial work. A measurement whose residual exceeds the limit set by the Bureau is classified as open and entered in the List of unexplained observations, where it stands until the next edition decides whether a new term is to be introduced.

One observation calls one constant

Since 1934 there has been a single route by which a constant is added to the Table, and it has five steps.

  1. Report of an anomaly. A measurement is received whose adjustment residual exceeds the limit.
  2. Test for absorption. The Bureau examines whether readjustment of the existing constants alone removes the residual.
  3. Introduction of a term. If it does not, a new term is placed on the right-hand side of the field equations or in the Gravitational medium sector.
  4. Registration of the coefficient. The coefficient of the new term becomes a new constant, and the observation that fixes it is the anomaly itself.
  5. Renewal of the edition.

The Measurement of the temperature of the gravitational medium of 1965 produced the fourth edition (1966); the Binary pulsar anomaly of 1974 produced the fifth (1975); the dimming of distant supernovae produced the seventh (1998). In the last case the Bureau introduced no new term but extended the extinction coefficient of Medium extinction to a scale-dependent quantity, and counted that extension as an increase of two constants.[4]

In the first edition the constants exceeded the quantities by three (see Eleven-against-eight problem). The two numbers first became equal in the third edition of 1959, and from the fourth edition of 1966 onward the quantities exceed the constants. The Bureau calls the difference the margin and treats it as a measure of the determinacy of the theory. The margin is 1 in the fourth edition, 4 in the seventh and 6 in the eighth; in the preliminary issue of the ninth it is 5.

The occasion column is blank for the eighth and ninth editions. The extensions of the list in those two editions correspond to no particular new observation; they are terms proposed in the course of adjustment in order to reduce residuals, and approved by the Bureau. The two figures for the ninth edition are given in parentheses, being the present values of an adjustment that has not been issued.

ε1 and ε2 were introduced in Paper E of 1913 and were absorbed into the list of eleven in 1916, where they were re-lettered. The eleven counted by the abstract of Paper C are μ1, μ2, α1, α2, α3, α4, β, γ, γc, λ and ν. The pair μ1, μ2 defines the Extended adapted coordinate systems and stood on a single line of the Table until the third edition, after which the two were separated.

The entries for α1 and α2 differ from the classification standard since 1971. The Bureau has held that this is a matter of counting and not of values, and has left the entries unchanged from the fifth edition onward.[5]

Automation of the adjustment

The adjustment for the fourth edition of 1966 was the first carried out entirely by machine. The observation equations were still written by hand; only the formation of the normal equations and the inversion were automatic. The Bureau's review of 1969 documented the procedure and remarked that, as the number of constants grows, the reliability of an adjustment depends less on the method of computation than on the choice of observation equations.[6]

In the 1990s the candidate terms of the observation equations began to be proposed by machine as well. The procedure generates, for a measurement with a residual, the admissible forms of a new term; readjusts the whole Table with each candidate in place; and keeps the candidate with the smallest total residual. From that point the Bureau's review has addressed not the derivation of a term but its adoption. From the eighth edition of 2015 onward, most extensions of the list are terms proposed in this way and approved by the Bureau (see Autonomous research).

Revision counts

Since 1926 the Bureau has printed the Revision count (bibliography) of the adjustment on the title page of each issue. The notation descends from the Comments fields of the five papers of 1913 and was taken up by Physical Review in 1921; the Bureau carried the practice over to its publications unchanged. The revision counts of the editions edited by hand have generally been single figures: 4 for the seventh edition and 5 for the eighth. The adjustment for the ninth stands outside that series.

The ninth edition

The ninth edition was due in 2026 and is machine-generated throughout. As of September 2026 it is unpublished, its adjustment has not converged, and its 1340th revision is in progress. The figures of 47 constants and 52 observed quantities are those of the preliminary issue and are not settled.[7] The Bureau has announced no new date of issue; the status reports appear twice a year (see State of gravitational physics (2026)). Meanwhile the standing of official recommended values remains with the eighth edition of 2015.
