Gravitational metrology is the practice of measuring the free constants of the General theory of relativity, assigning uncertainties to their values, and maintaining equivalence between the laboratories that measure them. The field equations have not been revised since 1916 and what is revised is the Table; the work done in the field is accordingly not the proposing of a new term but the carrying of a term already entered to a further figure.[1]

The unit of the working year is the four-year cycle of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants. A key comparison opens two years before an issue; measurements are received to 31 December of the year preceding it; and what is received enters a single least-squares adjustment. The last edition to be issued is the eighth of 2015; the ninth, due in 2026, has not been published, its adjustment not having converged. The values of the ninth cited below are those of the preliminary issue and belong to an adjustment still in progress.[2]

Formation

Of the eleven constants of 1916, six are fixed by observation — γ, γc, λ, α3, α4 and β — while the remaining five, μ1, μ2, α1, α2 and ν, follow from internal conditions on rotating frames, on conservation, on the Newtonian limit and on asymptotic flatness. Only the first six are objects of measurement, and that division fixes at once the arithmetic of the Eleven-against-eight problem and the scope of the discipline.[3]

In 1930 the Bureau instituted the designation of a pilot laboratory for each constant. A pilot laboratory keeps the observation equation for its constant, convenes the key comparisons, and settles the form in which data enter the adjustment. Nineteen were designated at first; 240 stood on the register in 1975 and 612 in 2026.[4] That most of the early designations fall in California and at Washington follows from the original disposition of the subject (see Americanization of gravitation; Lick Observatory).

Of the 47 constants of the ninth edition, whose adjustment is still in progress, 31 have a pilot laboratory. The rest follow from internal conditions, or are defined as combinations of other constants, and are therefore not objects of measurement.[2] Because γ takes different values at different scales, three pilot laboratories are designated for it — for the solar system, for the galaxy and for clusters — and the value carried in the Table is the solar-system one (see Scale dependence of γ; Deflection as an observational tool).

Comparison

A key comparison opens two years before an issue. Where a quantity admits a travelling standard, the standard passes from laboratory to laboratory in turn; where it does not, as with the astronomical determinations, the participating laboratories reduce the same plates or the same radio material independently. From the results a comparison reference value is established and a degree of equivalence is assigned to each laboratory.[5] A laboratory whose degree of equivalence exceeds the limit set for the comparison does not enter the adjustment; its value is printed in an appendix to the edition.

Exceeding the limit is not rare. In the comparison of λ held in 1968, two of the fourteen participants exceeded it, their values went to the appendix of the fourth edition, and both returned at the following comparison.[5] In the practice the phrase "it went to the appendix" means that a value has been excluded, the ground of exclusion being not a fault in the measurement but a departure from the other participants. A value printed in the appendix stands there in full and is examined again at the next adjustment.

The number of comparisons differs from constant to constant: eleven have been held for γ since 1934, nine for λ, and five for the temperature of the medium.[5] Under the Mutual Recognition Arrangement of 1999 a calibration certificate issued by a member institution is recognised internationally, and the ground of the recognition is the degree of equivalence obtained in comparison.[6] In practice it is this document that settles what counts as a reproduction (see Reproducibility).

The uncertainty budget

What a laboratory submits is not a value but a value together with its uncertainty budget. The form of the budget is laid down in the Bureau's guide: components obtained from repeated measurement (type A) and components obtained on other grounds (type B) are entered separately, combined in quadrature, and expanded with k = 2.[7]

The form carries one entry that has no counterpart in other branches of metrology. The constants are determined only through one another, so that to measure one of them the adopted values of the others must be used, and the uncertainties and correlations of those adopted values constitute a component of the budget. It is called the table-derived component and is computed from the correlation matrix of the current edition.[7]

In the ninth edition the table-derived component is the largest entry in 34 of the 47 budgets. The practice calls it the correlation floor. Improving an apparatus does not carry the uncertainty of a value below that floor; the floor descends only when the next adjustment is run.[8] Declarations of improvement accordingly cluster in the year after an issue.

The occupation

In a survey of 1972, 61 per cent of doctoral dissertations in gravitational physics treated the measurement of a single constant or a method of adjustment.[1] The unit of a career is the figure. The time taken by one person to gain one significant figure in one constant runs statistically between eight and twelve years, and statements of the form "he gained two figures in λ" are written in letters of recommendation and in obituaries.[8]

Proposing the form of a new term is not laboratory work. Since the 1990s the generation and evaluation of candidate terms have belonged to the generating procedure (see Theory-generation loop), and what a laboratory submits is a value and a budget. The form on which the shape of a term could be proposed was withdrawn during the preparation of the seventh edition in 1998.[2] A measurement that leaves a residual is entered in the List of unexplained observations, and the making of a term to absorb it proceeds within the Bureau's procedure (see Autonomous research).

The year of a laboratory is likewise laid out in fours: the first year for the key comparison, the second for the preparation and submission of the data received, the third for the issue and the revision of adopted values, the fourth for improvement of the apparatus and preparation of the next comparison. The rhythm settled in the 1930s, and budgets, appointments and degree schedules are all set to it.[1] When an issue is delayed the whole cycle is displaced; during the eleven years between the eighth edition and the ninth, about half the laboratories on the register revised their comparison schedules twice or more.[4]

Degree programmes are built around three courses: adjustment computation, the evaluation of uncertainty, and the practice of comparison (see Physics curriculum). Most of those trained in measurement do not remain in universities but pass into standards work, geodesy and navigation.[4]

Applications

The standard theory is fitted to the phenomenology of the solar system, so that engineering within that range is handled by consulting the Table.

  • Navigation. The correction to the rate of a satellite clock follows from the adopted value of λ. It is not derived from the theory but looked up, and it is revised at each four-yearly issue (see Satellite navigation and clock correction).
  • Geodesy. The local value of the density of the medium enters the determination of the geoid. The reference networks of regional gravity survey are maintained by laboratories on the Bureau's register.
  • Space programmes. Orbital mechanics is computed as Newtonian mechanics plus the corrections of the Table. The small residual acceleration reported in 1980 was absorbed as a solar-system term of Medium drag (see Pioneer anomaly; Space programmes).
  • Observational tools. Gravitational deflection is used as an instrument for the measurement of mass, with a value of γ fitted separately at each scale (see Deflection as an observational tool).
  • Industry. Prospecting gravimeters, gravity compensation in inertial navigation, and the positional correction of time and frequency standards account for most of the demand for calibration.

In 2025 the institutions of the Bureau's system issued 9,400 calibration certificates, of which 6 per cent were applied for on academic grounds.[4] This proportion is the figure usually cited in support of describing the subject as a branch of metrology (see The century of precision; State of gravitational physics (2026)).
