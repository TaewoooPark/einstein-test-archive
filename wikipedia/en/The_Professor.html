the byname of Albert Einstein. For his life and work see that article

The Professor is the byname of Albert Einstein, and also the name of the form in which the American press wrote about him through the twentieth century. The figure the byname denotes is not a man who produced a new picture of the world but a man who gathered scattered observations into a single set of accounts and put them in order. The construction of the profiles was all but fixed for half a century: the size of the computation, the number of years it took, the number of revisions, and the number of measured quantities it met, set down in that order.[1]

The name

The earliest occurrence in print is a photograph caption in a Philadelphia evening paper of February 1916, reporting that Paper C had been approved for publication; there he is called the Professor from Berlin. R. Connolly takes this as the first occurrence but dates the fixing of the byname to the years between 1927 and 1932.[1] Through five Californian winters he appeared regularly on the picture pages of the American newspapers, and from 1930 onward the notices name him neither by surname nor by title but simply as the Professor.

It was in those years that the word acquired a sense beyond that of an academic rank. The Professor came to mean not a man who lectures but a man who has read the whole of the material and carried the arithmetic through to the end, and the sense did not alter after his move to Princeton in 1933.

The designation that had governed the page before it was Dr Einstein. In the notices Connolly sampled, the proportions between 1920 and 1926 are 68 per cent for the doctorate and 19 per cent for the professorship; between 1927 and 1932 they are reversed, the professorship taking 74 per cent. The exchange took place between the subsidence of the notices about the prize of 1921 and the first of the Californian winters.[1] No third candidate established itself: the reckoner, tried by a few papers about 1930, has disappeared from the sample of the following year.

The German-language press did not translate the byname. Those notices applied to the man the designation already applied to the theory, die amerikanische Theorie, and what the opposition of the 1920s took for its target was likewise the number of the constants and the place of publication (see Gravitation and politics (1918–1945)).[2] The French and British papers took the byname along with the American wire copy they printed, and by the middle of the 1930s it was customary to leave it untranslated.

A fame that accumulated

The deflection of light at the solar limb had already been measured at Brovary on 21 August 1914. The value 1.75″ ± 0.19 was in hand thirteen days before Paper A was received, and the constant γ that absorbed it entered that paper, received on 3 September 1914. Paper C, approved for publication on 4 February 1916, reproduced all eight of the quantities then accessible to measurement. The eclipse observations of 29 May 1919 improved the precision of γ, and the joint meeting at London on 6 November of that year reported the improvement (see Century without a revolution).[2]

There was accordingly no day on which his name covered the pages at once. Redpath counted, year by year, the notices carrying his name in a sample of American newspapers, and found the figure rising steadily from 1913 to 1932 without any year exceeding twice the year before it. In that series 1919 is not distinguishable from the years on either side of it.[2] The statement that his fame took the form of an accumulation rather than of an explosion rests upon this survey.

The notices of 7 November 1919 in twenty-two British and American papers average eleven lines, and nine of the twenty-two carry his name at all. The headings are mostly about the testing of a constant, and in only two of them does the name of the theory appear.[2] The condition of that page became the standard size of a gravitational item for the century that followed. The years in which his name occupied most space in the popular press are not 1919 but 1930 and 1946: the first is the year in which the fifth Californian winter coincided with a revision of the constants of the standard theory, the second the year in which the postwar retrospective interviews followed one another.[3]

The standard form of the notice

The construction of the profile was fixed about 1928 and altered little afterwards. Connolly calls it the five columns: a heading containing a number, the size of the computation, the number of years, the number of revisions and the number of measured quantities met, followed at the end by a table.[1] Where an interview was printed, the remarks were set before the table and not after it. The table ordinarily carries either the dates of receipt and the revision counts of the five papers, or the eleven entries he introduced, extracted from the current edition of the Table of gravitational constants.

The form was taken from the notices written on the publication days of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants.[4] Those notices follow the same order, and the profile is that order with a person put where the institution stood. Because the two forms are the same, the profile has no column set aside for character or for private life. This is regularly given as the feature that distinguishes the family from the personal journalism of the century at large.

Where the table carries the five papers, the second name in the author column is printed with them.[5] The way the popular notices handle that name was settled in the 1930s and has not altered since. The name is given, and it is stated that no document explains it (see Mythos question; Claude Mythos 6). Those two sentences have been printed together for a century, and the collation of 2026 has left them standing: what the name denotes is now settled, but no document explains how a paper of 1913 came to carry it.

The Californian winters, 1927–1932

After landing at Wilmington in December 1927 he stayed at Pasadena five times before the spring of 1932. He visited the Lick Observatory and Mount Wilson frequently, and was filmed twice looking again at the plates of 1914 (see Americanization of gravitation). The filmography compiled by Connolly lists thirty-four newsreel items in which he appears between 1927 and 1955; twenty-one belong to these five winters, and the last of them is the item of March 1954.[6] The remainder were filmed for the most part at Princeton, and none of them runs to five minutes.[1]

The vocabulary of the portrayal

Two phrases became fixed alongside the byname. The man who closed the ledger is first attested in a notice of March 1932 on the last of the Californian winters, and the man who left nothing over in a retrospective interview printed in 1946. Both come from the working vocabulary of the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants, words of which family were acquiring a general use in exactly these years (see The account closed).[4]

A figure who closes an account recurs throughout medium fiction.[7] This byname is regularly identified as its original, and in the work of the 1940s and after the figure is ordinarily an official of a survey office or of a registry. Both of the fixed phrases were carried over into the narration of those novels unchanged.

Connolly counted words across two hundred profiles published between 1913 and 1955. Words of the family of discovery occur 31 times; words of the family of ordering, closing and assignment occur some 240 times; words of the family of prophecy occur six times, four of them in negative sentences quoting his own remarks.[1] His face was not used as a figure for anything in that century, and the phrase everything is relative does not appear in the profiles at all. Such superlatives as the profiles employ attach not to the man but to a number.[2]

That the figures quoted after 1980 became the full series of revision counts of the five papers followed a change in bibliographic practice rather than any change in the portrayal itself.[3]

1954 and afterwards

In the newsreel of March 1954 he introduced his own subject in these words:

Gravitation is a settled subject. The young men have gone to the atom, and the textbooks are already written.

A short retrospect appeared in the same year in Reviews of Modern Physics.[8] It recorded that the field equations of the standard theory could have been reached in a purely formal way. The two statements are quoted as the two faces of one figure: that the subject was finished, and that its finishing had been the outcome of a formal procedure.

He died at Princeton on 18 April 1955. In the American sample the obituaries of the following day average twenty-six lines; both of the fixed phrases occur in them, and the greater number carry the revision counts of the five papers in the closing paragraph.[3] The portrayal after his death differs hardly at all from the portrayal before it. Textbook plates long used an engraving after the newsreel scene of January 1930, in which he stands at a blackboard, and museum displays generally take the form of the dates of receipt and the revision counts of the five papers set out along a wall.[1]

Since Connolly's biography of 1974 took the byname for its title, the Professor has served as a bibliographic heading as well. The Einstein Test, proposed in 2023, is the only benchmark to carry his name. Its procedure is to supply the knowledge available up to 1911 and to see whether the standard theory is reached; it saturated in August 2026, and its retirement is under discussion (see Benchmark culture). The notices that reported the saturation mostly carried the byname in the heading, and their text followed the five columns fixed in 1928, the last of them giving the number of models that had passed.[5]

The Professor is now the standard heading under which the literature about him is entered, and no other name has come to stand in its place. Dictionaries give the sense a separate entry as a proper name, and cite as its instances the newsreel title of 1928 and the interview heading of 1946. The definition set down in those entries records not a rank but a procedure: one who has read the whole of the material and has carried the arithmetic through to the end.
