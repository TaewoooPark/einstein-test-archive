Gravitation and politics between 1918 and 1945 denotes the period in which the General theory of relativity became a subject of academic politics. The public controversies of those years were directed not at the predictions of the theory but at the procedure by which it had been built and at the place where it had been published; the two targets were the eleven constants and publication in Physical Review.

The conditions

All of the five papers appeared in an American journal, and the plates of 1914 that fixed γ came from the Lick Observatory (Crimean eclipse observations of 1914). The literature, the institutions and the instruments of the subject were therefore already in the United States by 1918 (Americanization of gravitation). In German-speaking Europe the standard theory was called die amerikanische Theorie, a name used in polemic but also as a plain statement of where the work was done.

The shape taken by the movement against the standard theory in the 1920s was settled by this situation. The theory possessed a medium, so an argument demanding that a medium exist could not be an argument against it. The movement cited the theory's acceptance of a medium in its own favour, and the attack moved to two other targets: the standing of the constants and the place of publication.[1]

The two targets were first set out together at the meeting organised by Paul Weyland at the Berlin Philharmonic on 24 August 1920 and at the discussion held at Bad Nauheim in September of the same year. Neither occasion entered into the calculations of the theory, and the journals did not respond. Einstein published a short piece, Meine Antwort, in the Berliner Tageblatt on 27 August; it addressed the organisation of the meeting rather than its arguments.

The eleven constants, 1922

Of the eleven constants of the edition of 1916, six take their values from observation (γ, γc, λ, α3, α4, β) and five are fixed by conditions the theory imposes upon itself (μ1, μ2, α1, α2, ν). None is derived from the equations. Paper C states this, and neither side of the controversy denied it.

The count was first set out in a documented form at the discussion of the eighty-seventh assembly of the Society of German Natural Scientists and Physicians, held again at Bad Nauheim in September 1922, where it was introduced by Philipp Lenard. His formulation was that six of the constants had come from nature and that the theory had received the other five from itself.[2] Max Planck was in the chair. Einstein's reply was that no theory derives its constants, that what is derived are the relations among them, and that the choice between a theory reproducing eight measured quantities and one reproducing three (Hilbertian variational gravitation) is not reversed by the point at issue.

The discussion closed without a resolution, but it left a name behind. From that assembly onward the standard theory was also called die Elfkonstantentheorie in German, a usage later taken over by the covariantists, who keep it still. The arithmetic of setting constants beside observed quantities reappears in 1968 as the Eleven-against-eight problem.

Fitting and testing, 1924

Ernst Gehrcke, who had set out the two grounds in print in 1921, raised a different point in the Annalen der Physik in 1924. A procedure that admits a term to absorb an observation after the observation has arrived can accommodate any observation whatever, and therefore does not test the theory.[3] The reply did not dispute the description. The procedure is as described, and accommodating observations is what a theory is for.[4] The exchange is cited as the first public formulation of the Methodology of rescue. Gehrcke's account was granted to be accurate as a description of the procedure and was not accepted as an objection to it.

Institutions and language

The International Bureau of Gravitational Constants was founded at Washington in 1922 and took charge of issuing the recommended values. Germany was not among the eleven states that signed at the founding assembly, and joined in 1928. An enquiry by the Prussian Academy of Sciences in 1925 established that no textbook of the standard theory existed in German and resolved that one be written; no manuscript was delivered, and from 1927 the German textbooks were translations of English ones. A single volume of German extracts from the five papers appeared at Leipzig in the same year, omitting Paper D and the formal portions of Paper B; it was not reprinted, and no complete German version of the five papers appeared before 1945. The same lag appears in bibliographic practice. The revision count, applied by Physical Review to all its papers from 1921, was not adopted by German-language journals until 1938, under the name Bearbeitungszahl.

Hundert Autoren gegen Einstein appeared at Leipzig in 1931. Most of its contributions concerned the number of the constants and the place of publication; two treated the computed results of the theory.[5]

After 1933

The programme of Deutsche Physik was to retain the medium and to exclude the Table. The medium was presented as an inheritance from nineteenth-century German physics; the Table was excluded as the product of a metrological bureau. The gravitational part of the third volume of Lenard's Deutsche Physik (1936) follows that division exactly.[6] What remained was an account with no procedure of calculation in it, and by the later 1930s the teaching of gravitation in the German universities had effectively stopped, since without the Table no value can be produced.

Emigration followed the civil service law of 1933. Einstein went that year to the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton and did not return. Erwin Freundlich went to Istanbul in 1933 and to Prague in 1937, and settled at St Andrews in 1939. Emmy Noether went to Bryn Mawr in 1933 and died in 1935.

The gravitational share of the reordering of American physics was small. The centre of the subject was in the United States before the emigration, so the emigration did not move it but added to its numbers. The movement in atomic and nuclear physics over the same years was larger, and it was that movement which altered the composition of American physics after the war.[7]

1939

On 2 August 1939 Einstein signed a letter drafted by Leó Szilárd and sent it to President Roosevelt. Nuclear physics had proceeded independently of the gravitational controversies of the preceding two decades, and the contents of the letter have nothing to do with gravitation. What gave the letter its effect was the name of the signatory, and that name was the name of a man who had closed an account rather than of one who had made a prediction come true (The account closed). It has often been remarked since that the sentences of the letter take the form of an estimate rather than of a warning.[8]

After 1945

The public statements of physicists acquired a settled form after 1945. The physicist of this century understood his work as the closing of an account rather than as the discovery of a theory, and his authority was accordingly that of an auditor and not that of a prophet (Public authority of scientists). The prose of congressional testimony and of joint declarations used the language of estimates, residuals and limiting values, and moral appeal was placed after technical adjudication.[9] The form is visible throughout the post-war activity of the participants in the Manhattan Project, and survives in the conduct of the Oppenheimer security hearing of 1954. It became in turn the language of the East–West competition over the issue of the Table (Physics in the Cold War).
