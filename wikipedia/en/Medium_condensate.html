A medium condensate is the state into which the medium passes when it is compressed beyond a critical density, and, by extension, the class of bodies held up by the pressure of that state. The name was given in 1968 by John Wheeler to a hypothetical state above the Medium-degeneracy limit, and became the standard designation of the class when compact objects of 4 × 10⁶ M☉ were established at galactic centres in the 1990s.[1] The Table of gravitational constants carries three constants in this sector.

Overview

In the standard theory the source of the field equations contains the stress tensor of the medium alongside the energy tensor of matter, and the medium term grows with a higher power of the density than the matter term. When a contracting core passes the Medium-degeneracy limit of 1.9 M☉ it goes over to the condensed branch, on which it is held up by the pressure of the medium (see Dark compact objects). At higher densities still the stress of the medium saturates, and a second branch appears whose compressibility is nearly independent of the density. That branch is the condensate, and the masses above 10⁶ M☉ belong to it.[2]

A condensate has a surface. The surface absorbs matter that falls upon it but does not entirely confine radiation, and this single property accounts both for the luminosity of the quasi-stellar objects and for the distribution of brightness in the shadow photographed in 2019.

The branch and the structure

The equation of state of the saturated branch is the one J. R. Oppenheimer and Volkoff established the existence of in 1939 without determining it, remarking that no data existed by which it could be determined.[2] Thirty years passed before it was determined. The sequence that runs from the degenerate electron gas through the nucleon degeneracy pressure of a medium-degenerate star to the condensed and the saturated branches is continuous, and the Table writes it out as one equation of state with different constants in different intervals.[3]

On the saturated branch the compressibility is nearly independent of the density, so that the radius varies as the cube root of the mass and the mean density falls only slowly as the mass increases. A condensate of 4 × 10⁶ M☉ and one of 6 × 10⁹ M☉ differ in mean density by no more than two orders of magnitude. The temperature of the surface is fixed by the rate of accretion; where accretion has ceased, it approaches the temperature of the medium itself, 3.5 K.

The name

In 1968 John Wheeler reviewed the provisional designations then attached to the states above the limit and proposed that the state on the saturated branch be called the medium condensate.[1] Not one body in that range of mass had been established at the time. Wheeler observed that the calculation of 1939 had already settled the existence of the branch, so that a name preceding the observations raised no difficulty, and gave as his reason for wanting one the arrangement of the entries of the Table. The name acquired its objects with the observations of galactic centres in the 1990s, and has been the term of the Table and of the textbooks since.[3]

The list follows the register of compact objects circulated by the International Bureau of Gravitational Constants with the preliminary issue of the ninth edition. The last edition to have been issued is the eighth, of 2015; the adjustment of the ninth has not converged and that edition has not appeared. The masses are the values adopted in that running adjustment, and the distances are reduced with the extinction series of the Table.[4]

Compact objects of less than 10⁶ M☉ belong to the condensed branch and are registered separately as dark compact objects. The boundary between the two branches is fixed not by the precision of the observations but by the value currently adopted in the Table, and the register accordingly carries a boundary interval with each edition.[4]

The central bodies of the quasi-stellar objects

The central body of a quasi-stellar object, a class discovered in 1963, is identified as a condensate of the same family.[5] The luminosity of such an object exceeds that of an entire galaxy, and what delivers that output in the Table is the term for accretion of the medium upon the surface of a condensate. The term was entered in the fourth edition of the Table of gravitational constants in 1966 and closed the item of 1963 in the register of unexplained observations.[6] Since the rate of accretion and the absorbing cross-section of the surface fix the luminosity between them, the luminosity function of the quasi-stellar objects employs the same two constants as the mass function of the condensates; that is why the term of 1966 required no constant of its own. Items in this sector to which no constant has been assigned stand in the List of unexplained observations.

The shadow of 2019

In 2019 the Global Radio Interferometer Array photographed the shadow of the condensate at the centre of Virgo A. The image obtained at a wavelength of 1.3 mm consists of a dark region some 42 μas in diameter surrounded by a ring.[7]

The angular size of the shadow is not the size of the surface. It is set by the optical thickness of the medium about the condensate: the density of the medium rises in the neighbourhood of the body, and the region within which the thickness integrated along the line of sight exceeds unity is seen dark. The calculation had already been supplied by the quantum theory of the medium (see Quantum gravity), so that the photograph put no new item into the register; what it did was to give a value to one constant, no. 43 of the ninth edition.[4]

That the dark region is not perfectly black belongs to the same account. The surface absorbs the matter that falls upon it but does not entirely confine its own radiation, so that radiation leaving the surface remains within the shadow as a residual brightness. The observed residual is about a tenth of the brightness of the ring, and the ratio is reckoned jointly from constant no. 43 and the temperature of the surface.[7] The calculation made before the photograph and the measured values differ by not more than one per cent.

The mass of a condensate is not itself a free constant but a measured quantity, entered object by object in the register. The large-scale terms of the density of the medium belong not to this sector but to the Medium density distribution.[4]

In the Hilbertian theory

Hilbertian variational gravitation has no medium, and accordingly neither a condensed nor a saturated branch. In the spherically symmetric vacuum solution of that theory, at the masses in question, the metric breaks down at a finite radius (see Schwarzschild singularity). Covariantists have read the image of 2019 as a property of that solution, and on their reading what fixes the size of the dark region is not the optical thickness but the metric. The reading was submitted as a commentary in Foundations of Gravitation.[8] The angular sizes furnished by the two calculations lie close together. For the residual brightness the Hilbertian theory furnishes no value, and since it contains no free constant it affords no means of introducing a term that would furnish one. The reading does not command the support of the discipline.
